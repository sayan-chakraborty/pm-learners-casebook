Case 5.2 · Measuring Instagram’s creator product tags MetricsMediumIIM A Product Mindset 2026–27, pp. 288–289
Try it first. You bought a kurta after a creator’s Reel. List everyone who gained from that sale: you, the creator, the brand, Instagram. Now ask which of those gains Instagram can actually see and measure. Five minutes.
{{CASE:A288}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Confirmed what the feature does before measuring it, including the detail that checkout happens on the brand’s website.
  • Mapped each side’s journey first, so the metrics follow real steps rather than a list from memory.
  • Added operational metrics: link success, page load and attribution accuracy. Few candidates think of these, and they decide whether the numbers can be trusted.
  • Never asked why Instagram wants this. Instagram earns from ads, not from these sales. Is the goal to keep creators earning (so they post here, not on YouTube), or to win brand ad budgets? The North Star depends on it.
  • About 25 metrics, none ranked. The interviewer can’t tell which three matter, or what would make the team stop or expand.
  • GMV as the North Star rewards the wrong things: a few expensive items or discount spam can move it while most creators earn nothing.
  • No guardrail on the feed itself. If tagged Reels feel like ads, people scroll past, watch time falls and creators lose followers. None of the guardrails watches that.

Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing ✓ · Goal ✗ · Who ✓ creators, buyers, brands · Today and the trigger ✗ new or six months old? · Edges ✗ which country?. Metrics add-ons: the job for each side ✓ · product stage ✗ · what decision the metric will drive ✗. Without the goal and the decision, the answer can list metrics but can’t choose among them.

Check · “North Star: creator-attributed purchase GMV” Overstated

GMV (gross merchandise value, the total value of goods sold) is easy to report, but three things weaken it here. Instagram doesn’t see most purchases: they happen on the brand’s site, and since Apple’s 2021 tracking rules many iPhone purchases can’t be linked back to the tap. A single ₹40,000 watch counts as much as 40 kurtas from 40 different creators. And Instagram doesn’t earn from GMV; it earns when brands spend more on ads and when creators keep posting. GMV is a useful input, not the goal.

Check · “Purchases attributed using creator and campaign IDs” Directionally right

Tagging the link with the creator’s ID tells you who got the last click. It misses people who saw the Reel, didn’t tap, and bought later by searching the brand, and it credits sales that would have happened anyway. The fair test is a holdout: hide tags from a random 5% of users and compare purchases from both groups.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

How long has the feature been live, and where? Why does Instagram want it: to keep creators earning here, to win brand ad budgets, or both? And what decision will these metrics feed, for example whether to open tagging to every creator?

Q

Live in India for six months with 20,000 invited creators. Leadership must decide whether to open it to all creators. The aim is creators earning on Instagram.

Why this move: “measure success” means nothing until you know which decision it serves. Here it is one yes-or-no call, so the answer must end with a rule for that call.

A
② Goal

Then success is creators earning money from Instagram without making the feed worse. Each side wants something different ({{REF:grid52}}): creators want sales, buyers want useful finds without feeling sold to, brands want sales at a fair cost, and Instagram wants creators to keep posting and viewers to keep watching.

Why this move: writing each side’s win in one line shows where they clash. That clash is what the guardrails must watch.

A
③ One number

Goal (North Star): creators who earned from at least one tag-driven sale this week. It grows only if many creators succeed, not a few big ones, and a creator who earns keeps posting. I’d report GMV and commission beside it, not instead of it.

Why this move: a count of creators earning rewards breadth. It moves when a small creator in Indore makes her first ₹200, which is exactly the behaviour Instagram wants to spread.

A
④ Inputs

Follow one tagged Reel with 10 lakh views ({{REF:funnel52}}). 2% tap the tag: 20,000. 40% of them view the product: 8,000. 30% go to the brand’s site: 2,400. 3% buy: 72 orders × ₹1,200 = ₹86,400 of sales, and at 8% commission the creator earns about ₹6,900 (illustrative). The biggest lever Instagram controls is the first step, the tap rate, which depends on whether the product fits the Reel.

Why this move: the chain shows where the numbers are small and which steps Instagram controls. The last step happens on the brand’s site, so it is watched but can’t be the main lever.

A
⑤ Guardrails

Safety checks (guardrails): watch time on a creator’s tagged Reels against the same creator’s untagged ones; “not interested” taps and unfollows after tagged posts; returns and complaints about tagged products. If tagged Reels are watched 10% less, viewers are treating them as ads.

Why this move: comparing a creator with herself removes the difference between popular and unpopular creators, so any gap is caused by the tag.

A
⑥ Decide

Before opening it to everyone, run a holdout: hide tags from 5% of viewers for four weeks. Open to all creators if (a) creators earning grows week on week, (b) tagged Reels’ watch time stays within 3% of untagged ones, and (c) complaints per 1,000 orders stay flat. Trade-off: more tags earn creators more but make the feed feel like a shop. Next: let brands boost a creator’s tagged Reel as an ad, which is where Instagram earns.

Why this move: the rule turns the metrics into the yes-or-no call leadership asked for, and the holdout proves the sales are extra rather than relabelled.

How the answer hangs togetherThe decision (open to all creators?) and the goal (creators earning) chose the North Star: creators with at least one sale a week. The funnel showed that the tap rate is the lever Instagram controls. The guardrails compare each creator with herself so feed damage shows up clearly, and the holdout plus a three-part rule turns the numbers into a decision.
Key idea · The who-wins, who-loses grid

List the product choices down the side and every stakeholder across the top, then mark each cell with a plus or a minus. It takes two minutes on a whiteboard. It shows the interviewer that you have thought about everyone the feature touches, and every minus becomes a guardrail.
