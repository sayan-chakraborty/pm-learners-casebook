Case 5.3 · Users moving from WhatsApp to Telegram StrategyMediumIIM B Sigma PM Casebook 2026–27, pp. 149–150
Try it first. Open your phone. Which groups do you have on Telegram and which on WhatsApp? Why is each one where it is? If WhatsApp disappeared tomorrow, which of your groups would move, and which would simply die? Five minutes.
{{CASE:B149}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Asked why users are leaving, and set a goal when the interviewer left it open.
  • Split users into segments and chose two, with a reason.
  • Linked each feature to a pain point and ranked them by impact against effort.
  • Three of the four features already existed. WhatsApp has encrypted every chat end to end since 2016 and offered encrypted backups with a user’s own password or 64-digit key since 2021; it raised the file limit to 2 GB in 2022, the same as free Telegram. The gap was awareness, not features.
  • Never defined “moving”. Uninstalling WhatsApp, or starting new groups on Telegram while keeping WhatsApp? Almost everyone does the second.
  • Never sized it. How many, which age group, which country?
  • Didn’t test the interviewer’s reason. Telegram’s normal chats are not end-to-end encrypted; only “secret chats” are. People who move for privacy often move to less of it.

A
① Clarify

What does “moving” mean in our data: people uninstalling WhatsApp, or using it less while Telegram grows? Which country and age group? I’ll assume India.

Q

India. Daily users are flat, but among 18–24s new groups are increasingly started on Telegram, and their time on WhatsApp is slipping.

Why this move: “leaving” turned out to mean “starting new groups elsewhere”. That is a different problem from uninstalling, and needs a different fix.

A
② Size

Illustrative: about 10 crore WhatsApp users in India are 18–24. If 15% now start their new groups on Telegram, that is 1.5 crore people. Few will uninstall: in January 2021, the biggest scare WhatsApp ever had, Telegram gained 25 million users worldwide in three days, yet WhatsApp stayed India’s most used messaging app ({{REF:jan21}}).

Why this move: the 2021 episode is a natural experiment. Even a huge scare moved new activity, not whole families, which tells you where the real risk lies.

A
③ Why they go

Each app does a different job. Telegram wins where people don’t know each other: channels with lakhs of followers (exam notes, stock tips, movie updates), groups up to 2 lakh, usernames so you needn’t share your number, and chats stored in the cloud so a new phone gets everything. WhatsApp wins where people do know each other: family, friends, colleagues, local shops. Privacy is rarely the real reason, since Telegram’s normal chats are less private.

Why this move: naming the job each app does explains who moves and who doesn’t. A college batch of 400 strangers is a Telegram job; the family group is not.

A
④ What holds them

Farhan’s family group shows the strongest force: every member must move together, or the group splits. If each of 47 members has a 90% chance of joining, the chance all of them do is 0.9 multiplied by itself 47 times, about 0.7% ({{REF:allmove}}). Add years of photos and voice notes, and a phone book that is already the contact list. So WhatsApp won’t lose its old groups. It is losing the new ones.

Why this move: this separates what is safe (old groups) from what is at risk (new, large groups), so the fixes can aim at the right place.

A
⑤ Moves

Three moves, in order. 1. Tell the privacy story where people look: a one-line banner in new group chats, “Only members can read this group; not even WhatsApp”, and a nudge to switch on encrypted backup. Cost: almost nothing. 2. Usernames for large groups, so a college batch can join without sharing phone numbers, with message requests from strangers kept in a separate folder. 3. Better communities for 18–24s: bigger announcement groups inside Communities, and file sharing that matches Telegram. I would not copy Telegram’s open public channels for everything; they bring piracy and scams.

Why this move: the first move fixes a belief, which is cheap; the second and third close the real gaps for the one job WhatsApp is losing, without giving up what makes it trusted.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): weekly active senders aged 18–24, rather than “users who remain”. Inputs: new groups created per week by 18–24s; share of those groups with 50+ members; encrypted-backup switch-ons. Safety checks (guardrails): spam and unknown-sender reports (usernames can bring strangers in); messages per family group. Trade-off: the more WhatsApp opens up to strangers, the less it feels like a private space. Next: track people who use both apps, to see which jobs keep moving.

Why this move: counting senders, not openers, catches the real warning sign: people who still open WhatsApp but start their conversations elsewhere.

How the answer hangs togetherDefining “moving” turned the problem into “new groups start elsewhere”. The 2021 episode and the 0.7% maths showed that old groups are safe and new, large ones are at risk. The moves target exactly that job, and the North Star (weekly senders aged 18–24) and guardrails (spam, family-group activity) watch the same thing.
Framework · Switching costs, and why people keep both apps

1 · The situation.Telegram was free, had bigger groups and promised privacy. Farhan’s family still came back in three days. Something other than features was holding them.

2 · An everyday example.Moving house to another city. The rent is lower and the flat is bigger, yet many people stay. Moving costs money (packers, deposit), you lose things (old papers, the plants), you must learn a new place (routes, shops, a new doctor), and hardest of all, you leave your neighbours, friends and the school your children love. And many people who work in a new city keep the old house for weekends: they live in two places for different needs.

3 · The framework.A switching cost is everything a user loses, pays or must redo to change products. There are four kinds: money, data (what you’ve built up), learning, and people (the others who must move with you). For messaging the last one is by far the largest ({{REF:stack}}). Multi-homing means using two rival products for different jobs, like carrying two SIM cards, one for cheap data and one for calls. When keeping both costs almost nothing, a rival doesn’t need to win your old habits. It only needs to win one new job, and grow from there.

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. List the four costs for one real user, here one family group, and say which is biggest: people.
  2. Ask whether keeping both apps is cheap. Here it is: both are free and sit on the same phone.
  3. Find the job the rival does better, for which people will multi-home: large groups of strangers.
  4. Defend the new job, not the old one: old groups are held by people-costs already.
  5. Raise good switching costs (history, trust), never bad ones (locking data in). Bad ones invite regulators.

Common slip · treating high switching costs as safety. They protect what users already do on your product, not what they will start next. Most losses begin as a second app for one new job.

4 · When it misleads.Switching costs can be cut from outside. Mobile number portability (2011) let Indians change telecom operators without losing their number, and people switched far more freely afterwards. Europe’s Digital Markets Act now requires large messaging apps to let users of other apps message their users, which lowers the “people” cost. Lock-in also hides unhappiness: users who stay only because leaving is hard are gone the moment it becomes easy.

5 · In the interview.“Old groups won’t move because everyone would have to move together; the risk is new groups for jobs Telegram does better, so I’ll defend those jobs and measure where new groups start.”
