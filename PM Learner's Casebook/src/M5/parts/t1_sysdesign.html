System design IV: one WhatsApp message, end to end

9:14 pm. Farhan’s aunt records a voice note, “Beta, the wedding is on the 14th”, and sends it to the 47-member family group. Farhan’s phone is in his pocket with WhatsApp closed. His cousin Zoya is on a flight to Dubai with her phone in flight mode. Within a second his aunt sees one grey tick, then two. Farhan’s phone buzzes. Three hours later, when Zoya lands, the note is waiting for her. None of this feels special, and that is the achievement: it happens more than 100 billion times a day. This section follows that one message, one step at a time.

The load, in numbers

100 billion messages a day ÷ 86,400 seconds ≈ 11.6 lakh messages every second, on average. At midnight on New Year’s Eve it can be several times that. India is about 18% of WhatsApp’s users, so roughly 2 lakh messages a second on a normal evening. Every message also creates two or three smaller ones (the receipts behind the ticks). And at 9 pm perhaps 25 crore Indian phones are connected at once. If one server can hold about 20 lakh open connections, that is 25 crore ÷ 20 lakh = 125 servers just to keep the lines open, about 250 with spare room (illustrative). Text is cheap to carry; the hard parts are the number of open connections, the bursts, and the photos and videos.

1. An always-on connection, not constant asking

Everyday picture. Waiting for an important letter, you could walk to the post office every ten minutes and ask. Most trips would be wasted, and you would be exhausted by evening. Or the postman who walks your lane could simply hand it to you when it arrives.

How it works. When WhatsApp is open or running in the background, the phone opens one long-lived connection to a chat server and keeps it open. The server pushes messages down it the moment they arrive. Mobile networks close lines that stay silent too long, so the phone sends a tiny heartbeat (a keep-alive message of a few bytes) every few minutes. The other way, polling, means the phone asks “anything new?” again and again ({{REF:poll}}). Polling every 10 seconds is 86,400 ÷ 10 = 8,640 requests a day per phone, almost all answered “nothing”, and each one wakes the phone’s radio and drains the battery. A heartbeat every 4 minutes is 1,440 ÷ 4 = 360 a day: 24 times fewer, and a message arrives in under a second instead of waiting up to 10.

Real example. WhatsApp’s engineers wrote in 2012 that one of their servers was holding more than 20 lakh open connections at the same time. They used Erlang, a programming language first built by Ericsson for telephone exchanges, which were designed for exactly this: millions of calls waiting, few of them active. PM decision: the heartbeat interval trades battery against speed of noticing a dropped connection. It also decides how quickly “online” changes to “last seen”. On cheap phones with small batteries, a slightly longer interval can be the difference between a loved app and an uninstalled one.

2. The journey of one message, and the three ticks

Everyday picture. Registered post. The counter clerk gives you a receipt when she accepts the letter. An acknowledgement card comes back when it is delivered. If the person phones to say they have read it, you know that too.

How it works ({{REF:ticks}}). ① The aunt’s phone gives the voice note a unique ID, locks it with encryption and sends it on its open connection. ② The chat server saves it and replies “got it”: one grey tick. ③ The server looks up which server holds Farhan’s connection, in a session directory (a constantly updated list of which phone is connected where). ④ It passes the message there and down Farhan’s line. ⑤ Farhan’s phone replies “delivered”, which travels back: two grey ticks. ⑥ When Farhan opens the chat, his phone sends “read”: two blue ticks. Each receipt is itself a tiny message, so one message sent to one person creates about three messages’ worth of work.

Real example. Blue ticks arrived in November 2014, and many users hated being seen to have “read and not replied”. Within weeks WhatsApp let people switch read receipts off, with a fair rule: if you hide yours, you can’t see anyone else’s. Groups always show who read a message, under “Message info”. PM decision: each tick is a promise, so it must be exactly true. Showing two ticks before the other phone confirms would be faster, and it would be a lie that users would discover. Privacy settings that cut both ways, like read receipts, feel fair and avoid endless arguments.

3. Store and forward: when the other phone is off

Everyday picture. You go to your village for a week. The post office holds your letters and hands them over when you are back. If you never come, they go back to the sender after a month.

How it works. If Zoya’s phone isn’t connected, the server puts the message in her offline queue, stored on disk, with copies on more than one machine so a single failure can’t lose it. Her aunt sees one tick. When Zoya lands and her phone reconnects, the server sends everything in her queue in order, waits for each “delivered”, then deletes it. WhatsApp keeps delivered messages on its servers for no longer than it takes to deliver them, and deletes undelivered ones after 30 days ({{REF:store}}).

Order and duplicates. Phones lose signal in lifts and trains, then try again, so the same message can be sent twice. Because every message has an ID, the receiving phone keeps a list of IDs it has seen and quietly drops repeats, like throwing away a second copy of page 3. The server numbers messages within each chat, so they appear in the order they were sent. The promise users feel is simple: everything arrives once, in order, even if the network was a mess. Real example. The clock icon next to a message sent in a metro tunnel turns into a tick when the train comes out, and the message never appears twice. PM decision: how long to hold undelivered messages (longer helps people with a broken phone; shorter keeps less data), and what the sender sees in the meantime.

4. Group fan-out: one message, 46 deliveries

Everyday picture. Farhan’s mother prints one wedding card design, then posts a copy to each relative. The design work happens once; the postage happens 46 times.

How it works. The aunt’s phone sends the voice note to the server once. The server looks up the group’s members and puts a copy into each member’s delivery path, to their connection or to their offline queue. This is fan-out on write: the copying happens when the message is sent. For a WhatsApp Channel with one crore followers, copying every post into one crore inboxes would be wasteful, so channels use fan-out on read: the post is stored once, and each follower’s phone fetches it when they open the channel ({{REF:fanout}}). The maths for Farhan’s group: 212 messages × 46 other members = 9,752 deliveries a day, plus a delivered and a read receipt for each: about 29,000 small messages, from one family.

Real example. WhatsApp groups held 256 people for years. It raised the limit to 512 in 2022 and to 1,024 later that year, when it launched Communities, and it launched Channels in 2023 for one-to-many broadcasts. Telegram lets groups reach 2 lakh members. PM decision: the group size limit is both a cost and a product choice. Every extra member multiplies deliveries and receipts, and very large groups become noisy and good at spreading rumours. The better design gives broadcasters a separate tool (Channels) instead of ever-bigger groups.

5. Push notifications: waking a sleeping app

Everyday picture. You can’t hear a knock on your flat’s door from the back room, so the society guard rings your intercom: “Parcel for you at the gate.” The guard doesn’t open the parcel; he only tells you it’s there.

How it works. To save battery, iPhones and most Androids stop apps working in the background after a while, which closes WhatsApp’s open line. So when a message arrives for a sleeping phone, WhatsApp’s server sends a short request to the phone maker’s own service: Apple’s APNs or Google’s Firebase Cloud Messaging (FCM). Every phone keeps one connection open to that service, shared by all apps. It wakes WhatsApp for a few seconds; the app reconnects, fetches and unlocks the message, and shows “Aunt: Voice message” ({{REF:push}}). Because chats are encrypted end to end, the push itself carries nothing readable, only a nudge or a locked message.

Real example. Some phone brands popular in India shut background apps very aggressively to make the battery last longer. Messages then arrive only when the user opens the app, and people blame WhatsApp. Apps now show guides for turning off battery optimisation on specific brands. PM decision: what the lock screen shows (a message preview is convenient but anyone holding the phone can read it); how often to notify (big groups default to fewer alerts, and “mentions only” is a lifesaver); and testing on the cheap phones most users have, not only on the team’s own.

6. Photos and videos: send a collection slip, not the parcel

Everyday picture. A heavy parcel stays at the post office; each person gets a slip to collect it.

How it works. The phone first shrinks the photo or video, then locks it with a fresh random key and uploads the locked file once to WhatsApp’s media servers. The chat message carries only the link, the key and a fingerprint of the file (to check it arrived intact). Each member downloads the file and unlocks it with the key ({{REF:media}}). The maths for a 16 MB wedding video sent to a 200-member group: sending it inside each message would mean 200 × 16 MB = 3.2 GB leaving the aunt’s phone; by link it is 16 MB, 200 times less. When someone forwards the same video, WhatsApp can reuse the file already stored instead of uploading it again.

Real example. WhatsApp’s settings let users choose which media downloads automatically on mobile data, Wi-Fi or roaming, and since 2023 whether to send photos in HD. PM decision: quality against data. Many Indians are on a 1.5 GB-a-day pack; if a family group auto-downloads ten videos a day, that is 150 MB gone before lunch. Sensible defaults (photos yes, videos on Wi-Fi) matter more than any new feature.

7. Presence: “online” and “typing…”

Everyday picture. The light in a neighbour’s window tells you they’re home. Nobody writes down when it was on.

How it works. Presence updates are sent only to people who have your chat open right now, and are thrown away after. They don’t need to be perfect, so they are handled the fast way: if one is lost, the next one fixes it. This is the “availability over consistency” choice made for a whole feature. PM decision: social cues against privacy. In 2022 WhatsApp let users hide “online” from everyone, after years of complaints from people who felt watched.

8. Forward limits: fighting rumours by design

Everyday picture. A post office that stamps chain letters “copied many times” and lets you send each one to only one address at a time.

How it works. WhatsApp can’t read messages, so it can’t check whether a forward is true. But the phones can count. Each message carries a forward counter, locked inside the encryption. After five hops the phones label it “Forwarded many times” and let it go to only one chat at a time. Real example. After rumours on WhatsApp led to mob killings in India in 2018, WhatsApp limited forwards to five chats in India that July, and worldwide in 2019. In April 2020, during Covid rumours, it added the one-chat limit for highly forwarded messages and later said this cut such forwards by 70%. PM decision: friction is a safety tool. It also slows useful forwards, like a school notice, so the limit applies only to messages that have already travelled far.

9. When a chat server dies

Everyday picture. The postman for your lane falls ill. The post office sends another one, the letters in the sorting room are still there, and you might get your post an hour late, but nothing is lost.

How it works. If a chat server crashes, the 20 lakh phones connected to it lose their line. Each phone reconnects through the load balancer to a healthy server, and the session directory is updated. Nothing waiting is lost, because offline queues are stored on several machines. Anything the phone sent but never saw a tick for, it simply sends again with the same message ID, and the ID stops it arriving twice. The danger is the reconnect storm: if 20 lakh phones all retry in the same second, they can knock over the next server too. So each phone waits a random time before retrying, and waits longer after each failure ({{REF:storm}}). Spread over 60 seconds, 20 lakh ÷ 60 ≈ 33,000 reconnections a second: easy to absorb.

Real example. On 4 October 2021 WhatsApp, Instagram and Facebook all went down for about six hours. A routine network change at Meta cut its data centres off from the rest of the internet, and even its engineers’ door badges stopped working. Telegram’s founder said it gained about 7 crore users that day. PM decision: what users see while things are broken (the clock icon, an honest banner, never a false tick), how the company talks to users during an outage, and whether a burst of new sign-ups for a rival that day is a warning or a blip.
