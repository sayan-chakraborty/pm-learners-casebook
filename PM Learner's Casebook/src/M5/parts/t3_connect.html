Back to Lucknow · A sketch answer for the family-group question

Why the move failed. Everyone had to move together, and they didn’t. If each of 47 relatives has a 90% chance of joining, the chance that all do is 0.9⁴⁷, under 1%. One grandmother who stays on WhatsApp keeps the wedding plans there. The group also had years of photos and voice notes on WhatsApp, and the trigger was a false rumour: WhatsApp wasn’t charging for groups. Telegram offered this family nothing they needed. Even Telegram’s January 2021 feature for importing WhatsApp chat history lowered the cost of losing old messages, not the cost of moving people.

What it would take. A rival can’t win the old group; it must win a new job that WhatsApp does badly for this family, and grow from there. Examples: a 300-guest wedding group where most guests are strangers and don’t want to share phone numbers, or a family photo album that keeps full-quality videos for years. Each is a new group with low switching costs. Over time, if enough of the family’s new activity lives elsewhere, the old group quietens.

What WhatsApp should watch. Goal (North Star): weekly active senders, by age group. Safety checks (guardrails): where new groups are created; messages per family group; spam reports. Trade-off: opening up to strangers (usernames, bigger groups) wins new jobs but weakens the “private, people you know” promise that holds the old ones.

Key idea · Networks are won one group at a time

Social and messaging products are made of small networks: a family, a college batch, one suburb’s households. Each is won or lost as a whole. When you design, measure or defend such a product, ask first: which small group is this for, what makes it worth coming back to, and what would it take for all of them to move?
