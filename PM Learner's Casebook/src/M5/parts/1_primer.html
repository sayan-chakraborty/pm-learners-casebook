Social, creators & messaging

Lucknow, Sunday 9:15 pm, the family WhatsApp group

Farhan’s family group has 47 members and has sent 212 messages today: good-morning flowers, a cousin’s wedding card, a forwarded video that claims to be “breaking news”, and three voice notes from his aunt. At 9:15 pm his cousin Sana posts a link. “WhatsApp is going to charge for groups. Everyone join our Telegram group instead.” Twenty-two people join within an hour. By Wednesday the Telegram group is silent. The wedding plans, the flowers and the voice notes are all back on WhatsApp.

Try it: why did the move fail, and what would it take for a rival to pull a family group away? Think for five minutes before reading on.

How a free app pays for itself

Social apps and messaging apps are free to use. They earn in other ways. A feed app such as Instagram sells your attention to advertisers, a few seconds at a time. A messaging app such as WhatsApp can’t put ads inside your private chats without losing your trust, so it charges businesses that want to talk to you. Everything a PM does in this sector comes back to three questions: will people keep coming back, will enough of them create things worth watching, and can the app earn from that without making it worse?

Who does what, and who pays whom

Five groups meet on every social app. Users scroll, react and message; they pay nothing, but they give their time and their data. Creators post the videos and photos that users watch; a few earn a living, most post for fun. Advertisers, from Amazon to a bakery in Kochi, pay the platform to show ads to the right people. Businesses pay WhatsApp for each message they send to customers. And the platform decides what each person sees, runs the ad auctions and keeps the servers going. {{REF:money}} follows the money.

The unit economics, worked in rupees

Take one hour of Reels. A Reel lasts about 15 seconds, so an hour of scrolling shows about 240 of them. Say 1 in 10 is an ad: 24 ads. Advertisers pay per 1,000 views, the CPM (cost per mille, the price of 1,000 ad views). In India a Reels CPM might be around ₹60; in the US, around ₹700 (both illustrative). So one hour earns 24 × ₹60 ÷ 1,000 = ₹1.44 from an Indian user and 24 × ₹700 ÷ 1,000 = ₹16.80 from an American one ({{REF:hour}}). Same app, same hour, about twelve times the money.

That gap shows up in Meta’s own numbers. The last time it split revenue per user by region, a US or Canadian user earned it about $68 in a quarter, a European $23, and an Asia-Pacific user under $6 ({{REF:arpu}}). This is ARPU (average revenue per user: revenue ÷ users, for a period). India is a volume market: huge numbers of users, each worth little. It shapes every product decision. An Indian feature must be cheap to run, light on data and able to work on a ₹8,000 phone.

Why a free messaging app is hard to earn from

Ads inside a chat with your mother feel like someone reading over your shoulder. So WhatsApp keeps chats ad-free and charges businesses instead. Since 2025 it bills per message, by type. In India, from January 2026, a marketing message costs a business about ₹0.86, a utility message (an order update, a bill) about ₹0.115, and replies to a customer who wrote first are free. A clothing brand that sends 2 lakh order updates and 50,000 offers in a month pays 2,00,000 × ₹0.115 + 50,000 × ₹0.86 = ₹23,000 + ₹43,000 = ₹66,000. Meta’s “other revenue”, mostly this business messaging, reached $1 bn in April–June 2026, up 73%.

Four turning points

1. Cheap data (2016). Reliance Jio launched in September 2016 with free data and then very low prices. Mobile data became cheap enough to watch video all day, and the next 30 crore Indians came online through WhatsApp and YouTube, often in Hindi and other languages, often on their first smartphone. 2. The TikTok ban (June 2020). India blocked TikTok and 58 other Chinese apps. About 20 crore short-video users were suddenly free. Moj, Josh and MX TakaTak raced for them; Instagram launched Reels in India in July 2020, within two weeks of the ban, and won. 3. The privacy scare (January 2021). WhatsApp asked users to accept a new policy about sharing data with Facebook. Rumours spread that it would read chats. Worldwide, Telegram gained 25 million users in 72 hours, India giving it more installs than any other country, and Signal was downloaded 75 lakh times in five days. WhatsApp delayed the policy and explained that chats stayed encrypted. Most people stayed. 4. The rules arrive (2021–27). The IT Rules 2021 made large platforms appoint grievance officers, remove flagged content quickly and, for messaging apps, be able to trace who first sent a message. The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, notified in November 2025, bring consent and data-security duties from about May 2027.

Five mechanics that aren’t obvious

1. Most people watch; few post. Roughly 90% of users only watch, 9% react or comment now and then, and 1% create most of what everyone sees ({{REF:pyramid}}). This 90-9-1 rule is a rough pattern, not a law, but it explains why apps pamper creators: lose a few thousand top creators and the feed goes stale for millions.

2. A ranking system decides who sees your post. Nobody sees everything. For each person, the app scores thousands of candidate posts by predicting what they will do: watch to the end, share, comment, or tap “not interested”. Each prediction gets a weight, and the post with the highest total goes first ({{REF:rank}}). Weights are product decisions. When Instagram said DM shares matter most, creators started making posts people want to send to a friend.

3. Friends to strangers. Feeds once showed posts from people you follow (the social graph). TikTok showed that posts picked from anyone, by what you watch (the interest graph), hold attention far better, and a new user needs no friends for the app to work on day one. Instagram and Facebook rebuilt their feeds this way from 2022. The price is that the app feels less personal, so both keep adding features for friends. 4. Messaging spreads rumours faster than feeds. A forward travels through trusted people and nobody can see the whole chain, not even the company when chats are encrypted. WhatsApp’s answer was design, not deletion: from 2019 a message can be forwarded to at most five chats, and since April 2020 a “forwarded many times” message to only one chat at a time. 5. Brands pay creators directly. A sponsored Reel can earn a mid-sized Indian creator ₹20,000–1 lakh (illustrative) without the platform seeing a rupee. That is why platforms build tools for tagging products and brand deals: to get a share of money that already flows around them.

In the interview · Questions this sector loves
  • “How does Instagram make money if it’s free?” Users × time × ad load × price per 1,000 views; better ranking raises the price because ads work better.
  • “Should WhatsApp show ads in chats?” No: trust is the product. Earn from businesses, Status and Channels instead, which is what Meta chose.
  • “Design a new social app for X.” Start with the smallest group that works on its own (one college, one trade in one area), not with “everyone in India”.
  • “Engagement fell 5%. Diagnose.” Split creators from viewers first: fewer posts or less watching? Then new vs old users, and any ranking change.
  • “How would you fight misinformation?” Limits on forwards, labels and fact-checks, without breaking encryption.
