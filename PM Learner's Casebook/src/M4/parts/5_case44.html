Case 4.4 · MakeMyTrip enters Dubai Go-to-marketMediumIIM B Sigma PM Casebook 2026–27, pp. 151–152
Try it first. A friend who runs a successful dosa restaurant in Chennai wants to open in Dubai. What three questions would you ask before saying yes? Now ask the same three about MakeMyTrip.
{{CASE:B151}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Asked the objective and got a usable one: revenue and return over five years.
  • A clear three-part structure: is it worth it, can MMT do it, how to enter.
  • Concrete localisation: Arabic, local cards and wallets, local packages.
  • Compared entry modes (organic, joint venture, partnership, acquisition) and gave a phased plan.
  • Never asked what exists today. MakeMyTrip has run a UAE site and app for residents since about 2014, with Arabic support, a Dubai office and Umrah packages. The real question is how to grow it.
  • No sizing. “Expected revenue is substantial” was accepted without a number, so there was nothing to test the plan against.
  • Tourists book at home. A visitor from London books Dubai on a UK site before flying. A Dubai business mainly reaches the 39 lakh residents.
  • No right to win. Why would a resident leave Booking.com or Almosafer? MMT’s real edge is the India corridor: over half of the UAE’s 44 lakh Indians live in Dubai.
  • No success measure or stop rule for a five-year bet.

Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing ✓ · Goal ✓ revenue and ROI, 5 years · Who ✓ residents and tourists · Today and the trigger ✗ · Edges ✗ budget. GTM add-on “standalone or add-on: existing assets, right to win” ✗. Skipping “today” cost the most: the plan re-invents a business MMT already runs.

Check · “The fixed costs are essentially zero because of an already established technical platform” Overstated

The code is reusable; the rest is not. UAE payment methods, VAT, licences, Arabic and Malayalam support, and airline and hotel contracts in a new market all cost money. The biggest cost is buying customers against rivals who already spend heavily on ads: in online travel, customer acquisition, not technology, decides the payback.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

MakeMyTrip already has a UAE site for residents. Is this about growing that, or something new, such as selling Dubai tours to every visitor? And what budget and horizon: five years, as you said?

Q

Grow the UAE business, Dubai first. Five years. Budget is open if the case is strong.

Why this move: “grow what exists” changes the question from “can we enter?” to “where can we win?”, and it lets us use data MMT already has.

A
② Size it

Illustrative, per year ({{REF:tam}}). TAM: all travel booked by Dubai’s 39 lakh residents: 39 lakh × 1.5 trips × ₹50,000 ≈ ₹29,000 Cr. SAM: trips where MMT has an edge, by Indian-origin residents: 22 lakh × 1.2 trips × ₹45,000 ≈ ₹12,000 Cr; online, 60%: ₹7,200 Cr. SOM: 15% of that by year five ≈ ₹1,080 Cr of bookings, about ₹110 Cr of revenue at a 10% take.

Why this move: ₹110 Cr is about 1% of MMT’s revenue. Knowing that early tells you this is worth doing only as the start of something bigger.

A
③ Forces

For a general travel app in Dubai, four of five forces are strong ({{REF:forces}}): global rivals discount hard, airlines sell direct, and customers compare prices in seconds. In the India corridor it looks different: Booking.com has fewer Kerala homestays and Indian holiday packages, and Indian families trust a brand they used at home.

Why this move: the forces don’t say “enter” or “don’t”; they say where the market is winnable.

A
④ Right to win

MMT has what rivals lack for this group: India’s hotel and holiday inventory, support in Hindi and Malayalam, and a brand most Indian residents already know. Its costs to acquire them are lower, because many already have the app from India.

Why this move: a right to win is an advantage rivals can’t copy quickly. Without one, a market is only attractive to whoever already leads it.

A
⑤ Entry: wedge and phases

0–6 months: “going home” for UAE Indians: flights to Kochi, Mumbai and Delhi with baggage clearly shown, festival-season fare alerts (Onam, Diwali, Eid), UAE cards, Apple Pay and pay-later. 6–18 months: India holidays and family packages, where the margin is. 18 months on: the same offer for Indians in Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Oman, about 90 lakh across the Gulf. Only then add general Dubai staycations.

Why this move: a wedge (a narrow first offer where you are clearly better) wins a loyal base cheaply; the phases then widen it where margins and reach grow.

A
⑥ Measure, with a stop rule

Goal (North Star): gross bookings from repeat UAE customers. Inputs: cost to acquire a customer by channel; share of flight bookings that add a hotel or package. Safety checks (guardrails): contribution margin per booking, so share is not bought with discounts; refund complaints. Stop rule: if acquisition cost doesn’t pay back within 18 months by month 18, stop spending beyond the corridor. Risk: MMT said in August 2026 that international travel was subdued by the West Asia conflict, so plan a slower case too.

Why this move: a stop rule written in advance protects a five-year bet from sunk-cost thinking; the margin guardrail stops “growth” that is only discounting.

How the answer hangs togetherAsking what exists turned “enter Dubai” into “grow a small UAE business”. Sizing showed the prize is small unless it grows across the Gulf. The five forces showed the general market is hard but the India corridor is winnable, and MMT’s assets give it a right to win there. So the entry is a narrow wedge (going home), widened in phases, judged on repeat bookings, with a margin guardrail and a stop rule.
Framework · TAM, SAM and SOM

1 · The situation.The candidate and the interviewer agreed revenue would be “substantial” without a number. Nobody could say whether Dubai was worth one team or twenty.

2 · An everyday example.You plan a biryani kitchen in Koramangala. All the biryani Bengaluru orders in a year is huge, but you can deliver only within 5 km, and only at ₹250 a plate or more. Of that, with three famous biryani brands nearby, you might win 5% in three years. The last number is the one your business plan should use.

LevelBiryani kitchen in KoramangalaMakeMyTrip in Dubai
TAMAll biryani ordered in BengaluruAll travel booked by Dubai residents, ≈ ₹29,000 Cr
SAMOrders within 5 km, at your priceTrips by Indian-origin residents, online, ≈ ₹7,200 Cr
SOMThe 5% you can win from the famous brands15% of that by year five, ≈ ₹1,080 Cr of bookings

3 · The framework.TAM (total addressable market) is all the spending on the need. SAM (serviceable available market) is the part your product, price and reach can actually serve. SOM (serviceable obtainable market) is the share you can realistically win in a stated time, given competitors. Build each level by multiplication you can defend (people × trips × spend), and state the time frame.

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. Define the need and the buyer: travel booked by people who live in Dubai.
  2. TAM from the bottom up: residents × trips a year × spend a trip.
  3. SAM: keep only what you can serve well: Indian-origin residents, booked online.
  4. SOM: pick a share you can defend from rivals’ positions and your edge, for a year you name (15% by year five).
  5. Turn SOM into revenue (× take rate) and compare it with the cost of winning it.

Common slip · “if we get just 1% of a ₹29,000 Cr market…”. A share of the total means nothing unless you can say who those customers are and why they pick you.

4 · When it misleads.Top-down numbers can be made as large as the deck needs: define the market as “all Gulf travel” and TAM grows tenfold with no change in the business. And SOM often hides the real assumption. The 15% share is a guess until you know what it costs to win each customer.

5 · In the interview.“Let me size it three ways: all the spending, what we can serve, and what we can win in five years. The last one decides the plan.”

Framework · Porter’s five forces

1 · The situation.The candidate listed risks (rules, competition, culture) but couldn’t say whether Dubai’s travel market is one where anyone makes money.

2 · An everyday example.A chai stall outside a college gate. Is it a good business? It depends on five things: how many other stalls are on the same pavement; how easily a new one can open; whether students can just buy a café coffee instead; whether the milk supplier can raise prices; and whether students can walk ten metres to a cheaper stall.

ForceChai stall at the college gateOnline travel in Dubai
RivalryThree stalls on the pavementBooking.com, Expedia, Agoda, Almosafer, Wego
New entrantsA new stall needs only a cartAn app is cheap; airline and hotel deals are not
SubstitutesCafé coffee, cold drinksAirline apps, offline agents in Karama
SuppliersThe one milk vendor nearbyAirlines (strong), hotels (weaker)
BuyersStudents compare prices across stallsResidents compare fares in seconds

3 · The framework.Michael Porter’s five forces (1979) explain how much profit an industry leaves for the companies in it. The stronger each force, the more profit leaks away: to rivals through price wars, to suppliers through high costs, to buyers through discounts. Rate each force high, medium or low, with one line of evidence, then say what the ratings mean for your move ({{REF:forces}}).

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. Define the industry narrowly: online travel booking for Dubai residents.
  2. Rate each force with one piece of evidence: airlines sell direct, so supplier power is high.
  3. Read the pattern: four high forces means thin margins for a general OTA.
  4. Re-rate a niche: for the India corridor, rivalry and buyer power fall.
  5. Choose the niche where the forces are weakest and your assets are strongest.

Common slip · listing the five forces as a ritual and stopping. The value is in the verdict: “general OTA, unattractive; India corridor, attractive”.

4 · When it misleads.The forces describe an industry as a snapshot, and they miss platforms and partners that help you. Google’s search ads, for instance, are both a “supplier” of customers to OTAs and a rival. Use the forces to find where profit leaks, not to predict who wins.

5 · In the interview.“Before choosing how to enter, I’ll check where profit leaks in this market. If it leaks everywhere, I’ll look for a niche where it doesn’t.”

Check · “A SWOT analysis is the way to structure market entry” Doesn’t hold up

SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) produces four lists with no weights and no decision. “Strong brand” sits next to “Arabic support needed” as if they mattered equally. And a strength means nothing without saying in which market: MMT’s India inventory is a strength for Indian residents and irrelevant for European tourists. Sizing, five forces and a right to win do SWOT’s job and end in a decision; use them instead.
