Mobility, maps & travel

Bengaluru, Friday 6:40 pm, heavy rain on Outer Ring Road

Ananya, 29, has finished work in Bellandur and wants to get home to Jayanagar, 14 km away. Her cab app says “finding your driver” for the fourth time. Two drivers accepted her ride and cancelled within a minute. The fare now shows 1.8× surge, ₹610 instead of ₹340. A bike taxi is ₹190, but it is raining. Across the city, thousands of riders are staring at the same spinning wheel, while hundreds of drivers sit in traffic with a passenger they picked up 40 minutes ago.

Try it: drivers keep cancelling tonight. Is this a pricing problem, a matching problem or a driver problem, and how would you tell which? Think for five minutes before reading on.

How a ₹300 cab ride pays three people

A ride-hailing app sells one thing: a car or bike that arrives in a few minutes, at a price you expected, and takes you there safely. It owns no cars. It rents the time of drivers who own or lease their vehicles, and it earns either a slice of each fare or a flat daily fee. Everything a PM does in this sector comes down to one balance: enough drivers near enough riders, at a price both accept. Maps and travel apps sit on the same foundations: knowing where everyone is, how long things take, and who will pay for what.

Who does what, and who pays whom

Five parties touch every ride. The rider pays the fare, by UPI, card or cash. The platform (Uber, Ola, Rapido, Namma Yatri) runs the app, sets the price, matches the ride and handles safety and support. The driver drives, and usually owns the vehicle or pays an EMI on it. Some drivers work for a fleet owner who owns 20 or 200 cars and pays them a daily wage or a share. And behind every screen sits a maps provider (Google Maps, or the platform’s own map, like Ola Maps) that turns addresses into routes, times and prices. {{REF:money}} follows the money on one ride.

The unit economics, worked in rupees

Take Ananya’s ₹300 ride from the driver’s side. The trip is 12 km, and the driver drove 3 km empty to reach her. A CNG hatchback costs about ₹5 a km to run, fuel and tyres and servicing together: 15 km × ₹5 = ₹75. The commission is 20%: ₹60. The car’s EMI is about ₹15,000 a month; spread over 26 working days and 12 trips a day, that is about ₹48 a trip. The driver keeps about ₹117 ({{REF:wfd}}).

Now add time. A driver who is online 11 hours and completes 12 trips of about 35 minutes each spends 12 × 35 = 420 minutes, 7 hours, with a rider in the car. That share, 7 ÷ 11 = 64%, is utilisation (the share of online time spent carrying a paying rider). The driver earns 12 × ₹117 = ₹1,404 a day, or about ₹128 per online hour. If utilisation falls to 50% (fewer trips, longer waits), earnings per hour fall by a fifth, and the driver logs off or switches app. Utilisation is the number that decides whether drivers stay, and drivers decide whether riders get a car.

Commission or subscription: the fight of 2025–26

For a decade apps took 20–30% of each fare. Then Namma Yatri (Bengaluru, 2022) and Rapido charged drivers a small flat fee instead: Namma Yatri asks ₹25 a day for unlimited rides. Drivers loved it. Uber moved autos to a subscription in February 2025, and Ola announced “zero commission” in June 2025. {{REF:commsub}} shows why the switch is so attractive to drivers and so painful for platforms.

The subscription model is counter-positioning (a move a rival can copy only by hurting its own business): Uber could match Rapido’s cheaper autos only by giving up commission income it depended on. It did, for autos, and kept commission on cabs, where riders pay more and drivers have fewer alternatives.

Three turning points

1. The discount war (2013–17). Uber and Ola each paid riders to ride and drivers to drive. Riders learned that a cab could cost less than an auto. When the money stopped, prices rose and drivers’ bonuses vanished, and the driver strikes of 2018 followed. The lesson: subsidies buy trips, not loyalty. 2. Drivers get choice (2022–25). Namma Yatri and Rapido showed that a platform could charge a flat fee, and Uber and Ola had to follow for autos. 3. The rules arrive (2025). The Motor Vehicle Aggregator Guidelines let states cap surge at 2× the base fare, set a floor at 50% off-peak, give drivers at least 80% of the fare on their own vehicles, and allow a 10% cancellation penalty (up to ₹100) on whoever cancels without a valid reason. BluSmart’s collapse the same year, after SEBI found EV loans had been diverted, ended the idea that an owned, salaried fleet was the safe alternative.

How the fare is built

Most apps build a fare from four parts: a base fare for starting the trip, a rate per kilometre, a rate per minute (so a driver stuck in traffic is still paid), and a platform or booking fee. Ananya’s normal-hour fare: ₹50 + 12 km × ₹14 + 35 min × ₹1.5 + ₹30 = ₹50 + ₹168 + ₹52 + ₹30 = ₹300. At 1.8× surge the first three parts are multiplied: (₹50 + ₹168 + ₹52) × 1.8 = ₹486, plus the ₹30 fee = ₹516 ({{REF:fare}}). Apps now show this upfront fare (a fixed price quoted before booking) instead of running a meter, so the app, not the rider, carries the risk of a traffic jam.

Five mechanics that aren’t obvious

1. Drivers cancel because they can see the maths. A driver who accepts a ride and then learns the drop is far out, with no return trip, faces an hour of empty driving. Cash versus UPI, a short low fare and a far pickup are the other usual reasons ({{REF:cwhy}}). Showing drivers more information can raise cancellations.

2. Surge is a signal to drivers more than a price for riders. A 1.5× price tells drivers across town where the money is tonight, and pulls them in. It also sends some riders to the metro, which frees cars for those who need them most. Capped too low, surge stops working as a signal. 3. Multi-apping. Most drivers run two or three apps at once and take the best offer. Loyalty is earned per trip, which is why cancellation rates rise the moment a rival app pays more. 4. Airports and stations are separate markets. Drivers queue for an hour in airport lots for one long, high-fare ride, so a city can be short of cars while 300 wait at the airport. Apps run special queues and pricing there. 5. The map is a hidden cost and a moat. Every search, price estimate and ETA calls a maps service. Ola’s founder said moving to its own Ola Maps in 2024 cut a bill of about ₹100 Cr a year to near zero. Better ETAs also mean better prices and fewer cancellations.

Travel booking in one paragraph

An online travel agency (OTA, such as MakeMyTrip, ixigo or Booking.com) sells other companies’ seats and rooms and keeps a cut. The cut depends on how much choice the seller has. Airlines sell their own tickets and pay OTAs little, so a ₹10,000 flight earns an OTA a few hundred rupees, mostly convenience fees. A hotel, which needs help filling rooms, pays 15–20%. That is why every OTA pushes you from a flight to a hotel and a holiday package ({{REF:ota}}).

In the interview · Questions this sector loves
  • “Cancellations are up. Diagnose.” Split driver from rider cancellations first; then look at drop-off distance, pickup distance, payment mode and time of day.
  • “Should Uber move cabs to subscription?” Compare lost commission against extra drivers and cheaper fares; pilot in one city; guard ETA and driver earnings.
  • “Is surge fair?” Explain it as a signal that brings drivers out, then say where it fails: floods, emergencies, and when the cap makes it too weak to move supply.
  • “Launch in a new city.” Drivers first, a few busy corridors, density before breadth.
  • “How would you improve Maps?” Pick a user with a hard job (a tourist, a delivery rider), not “everyone”.
