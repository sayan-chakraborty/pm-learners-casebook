Try it first. How many times did you or your family take an app cab last month? Would the answer be similar for most people in a big Indian city? Build the number of drivers from that, for five minutes.

What worked · what was missing
  • Pinned down the unit (drivers serving the city in a day) and confirmed it.
  • Chose supply from demand at the peak, the right approach for a fleet.
  • Stated the equation first, then filled in numbers, and included shared rides.
  • 40% of working people commute by cab is far too high for any Indian city; buses, metro and two-wheelers carry most work trips.
  • Everyone rides in the same hour. 3.4 lakh rides in one peak hour means all Uber commuters leave work at once.
  • A 30-minute ride is not a 30-minute cycle. The empty drive to the pickup and waiting add 10–15 minutes.
  • No check. 1.7 lakh drivers doing even 8 trips a day is 13.6 lakh trips, one for every 10 residents daily.

Check · 40% of working professionals commute by cab Doesn’t hold up

Bengaluru’s city buses alone carried about 40 lakh passenger trips a day in 2025, and two-wheelers are the most common way to get to work. App cabs are a small share of daily trips, a few per cent. A 40% cab share would put more cars on the Outer Ring Road than it could physically hold.

Stronger answer
A
1 · Unit

I’ll count distinct Uber car drivers who drive in Bengaluru on a typical weekday. Autos and bikes I’ll add at the end, since Uber runs those too.

Why this move: “drivers” could mean registered, monthly active or online now; these differ by 3–5×, so pick one out loud.

A
2 · Approach

Supply from demand: daily trips, then the busiest hour, then how many trips one driver completes in that hour, then how many of the day’s drivers are online at that hour.

Why this move: drivers are capacity, and capacity is set by the peak.

A
3–4 · Tree and numbers

1.4 crore people. About 2% take an app cab on a weekday (commuters, airport runs, late nights), about 1.8 trips each: 1.4 crore × 2% × 1.8 ≈ 5 lakh cab trips. Uber’s share of cabs is about 50%: 2.5 lakh. The busiest hour carries about 10% of the day: 25,000 trips. A trip cycle at peak, 35 minutes riding plus 10 to reach the pickup, is 45 minutes: 1.33 trips an hour. 25,000 ÷ 1.33 ≈ 19,000 drivers online at the peak. About 60% of the day’s drivers are on then: 19,000 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 31,000 drivers ({{REF:gtree1}}).

Why this move: every number has a one-line reason, and the cycle includes the empty minutes that the candidate left out.

A
5 · Check

Per driver: 2.5 lakh trips ÷ 31,000 ≈ 8 trips a day, a little below a full-timer’s 10–12, which fits a mix of full- and part-timers. Per resident: 5 lakh cab trips for 1.4 crore people is one app-cab trip per person every four weeks. Both sound right for Bengaluru.

Why this move: two quick ratios, one per side, catch almost any error in a fleet estimate.

A
6 · Range

The swing factor is the share riding each day: at 1%, about 16,000 drivers; at 3%, about 47,000. Monthly active drivers are higher, perhaps 1.5×, because few drive every day. With Uber Auto and Moto added, the whole fleet is roughly double.

Why this move: a range with its driver tells the interviewer which assumption to argue with.

How the answer hangs togetherThe unit (distinct car drivers on a weekday) fixed what to count. Supply from peak demand gave the tree, the 45-minute cycle made the capacity realistic, and two ratios, trips per driver and trips per resident, confirmed the scale. The range names the one input that moves it.
The reusable trick

Drivers needed at peak = peak trips an hour × cycle time in hours. 25,000 trips × 0.75 hours = about 19,000. This is Little’s law: things in the system = arrival rate × time each one stays. Use it for drivers, riders, delivery bikes, checkout counters and servers.
