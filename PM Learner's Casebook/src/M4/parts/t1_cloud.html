Cloud computing: renting the computer instead of owning it

On an ordinary Tuesday a ride app in India might need 40 servers. At midnight on New Year’s Eve it needs 130, for about two hours. In 2005 a company would have bought 130 servers, paid for power and cooling all year, and watched 90 of them sit idle on 364 days. Today it rents them from Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure or Google Cloud by the second, and hands them back at 2 am. A PM doesn’t configure any of this, but decides what must stay up, how much the bill may grow and which provider the product depends on.

The idea: computing as a utility

A century ago, big factories ran their own power plants. When the grid arrived, they plugged in and paid per unit. Cloud computing is the same move for computers: servers, storage, databases and AI models delivered over the internet, paid for as you use them. Three things change for a product team. You pay as you go instead of buying up front (running cost, not capital cost). You can add capacity in minutes, called elasticity. And someone else replaces the failed disk at 2 am. The providers are few and large: in April–June 2026, AWS had about 28% of the world market, Azure 20% and Google Cloud 15% (Synergy Research). All three run data centres in Mumbai and Hyderabad or Delhi, which matters for Indian rules that keep payment data in India.

How much do you want to run yourself? IaaS, PaaS, SaaS

The classic analogy is pizza ({{REF:pizza}}). Make it at home and you do everything, from the oven to the dough. Take-and-bake gives you the kitchen and ingredients; you still cook. Delivery hands you a finished pizza. Dining out means you only eat. IaaS (infrastructure as a service) rents you virtual machines, storage and network; your team installs and runs everything above them. PaaS (platform as a service) also runs the operating system and scaling, so you bring only your code and data. SaaS (software as a service) is finished software used through a browser: Gmail, Zoho, Freshdesk.

Two newer rungs. Containers (Docker) package an app with everything it needs, so it runs the same on a laptop and in the cloud; Kubernetes runs thousands of containers and restarts or adds them automatically. Serverless (AWS Lambda, Google Cloud Functions) goes further: you upload a small function that runs only when something triggers it, and you pay per call, by the millisecond. It is excellent for spiky, occasional jobs (send the trip receipt when a ride ends) and expensive for constant heavy work (the matching engine that runs every second).

Three ways to pay for the same server

On-demand: pay by the second, no commitment; the most flexible and the highest price. Reserved or savings plans: commit to one or three years of use for discounts of up to about 72% on AWS; right for the base load you are sure of. Spot: spare capacity at up to about 90% off, but the provider can take it back with two minutes’ notice; right for work that can pause, such as retraining the ETA model overnight. A sensible bill mixes all three ({{REF:costmix}}).

When it breaks

Bill shock: a forgotten test cluster or a runaway loop runs up lakhs in a weekend; budget alerts and tagging every server by team are basic hygiene. A region goes down: on 20 October 2025 a fault in AWS’s Virginia region took down many apps worldwide for hours; apps that ran in only one region went down with it. Lock-in: the deeper you use one provider’s special services, the costlier it is to leave, and moving data out (egress) is charged per GB.

In the interview

“Our load swings 3× in a day and 10× on New Year’s Eve, so owning servers for the peak would waste most of them. I’d reserve the base, rent the peak, run model training on spot, and keep payments data in an Indian region. The decision I’d push for is a second region for the booking path, because a one-region outage stops every ride.”
