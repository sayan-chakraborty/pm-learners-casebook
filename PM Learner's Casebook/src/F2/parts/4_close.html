Stress rounds, curveballs and the last five minutes

When the interviewer pushes on purpose

Some rounds are built to rattle you. The 2026 reports describe several:

  • Mastercard: asked for a favourite data product, then a second favourite, then a third, before finally settling on one (Datadog) and discussing it.
  • A CXO round: described as “a stress test, much like a B-school interview”, with questions on weaknesses and what the candidate was doing about them.
  • Cashfree: the CPO asked about non-work passion projects and how the candidate would measure their success, where there’s no framework to lean on.
  • Amadeus: the interviewer “drilled down on each line of my answer” and “tested the confidence in my answer”.

The pushback is usually the format, not a sign you’re failing. Stay calm, and keep your story the same. If they keep drilling the same point, they’re checking that the story holds when it’s repeated, not waiting for a better version. Inventing a better version under pressure is the one sure way to lose, because the next question will find the gap.

CurveballWhat to do
“I don’t have an example of that”Don’t invent one. Say “the closest I have is…” and tell a real, nearby story; or say what you would do and why, and label it as hypothetical.
You forget a numberSay you don’t recall it exactly and give the direction and size you’re sure of (the Practo candidate’s CAC answer).
“That doesn’t sound right”Ask what part doesn’t fit, answer that part, and keep your version unless they give you a new fact.
They jump to a product questionTreat it as a small case: two or three clarifying questions, then structure. Your story bank and your case practice are the same muscle.
Values questions (“pick one of our values”)Read the values before the day. Pick one, and back it with a real story, not an adjective.

How long to talk

The questions you ask at the end

Worth asking

  • “What does a new PM on your team ship in the first three months?”
  • “What’s one decision your team argued about recently, and how did you settle it?”
  • A question that comes from your own research, such as “You closed X last year. What did the team learn from it?”

Better avoided

  • Anything on the careers page (“What does your company do?”)
  • Pay, leave or work-from-home rules, in a first-round interview
  • “How did I do?”, which puts the interviewer on the spot

Connect the dots

The habits from case interviews carry straight over. Clarifying the question becomes “do you want a time I was right or a time I was wrong?”. Giving numbers becomes “calls fell from 9,000 to 7,400”. Naming the trade-off becomes “we lost agent detail, so we kept it on their screen”. And the stronger answers throughout are built the same way as a good case answer: a clear goal, a few steps with reasons, a result you can check, and what you’d do next.

Key idea · The weak fest answer, fixed in four lines

“I led fest sponsorship with a ₹4 lakh target, and I sent our biggest sponsor’s deck two weeks late because I kept polishing it. We lost ₹1.5 lakh. That week I called 11 local sponsors and got back ₹90,000. Since then I send work when it’s good enough to be useful, not perfect, and track every deadline with an owner. The next year all nine decks went out three weeks early.”

Same story, same facts. The difference is the word “I”, the numbers, and the last two sentences.
