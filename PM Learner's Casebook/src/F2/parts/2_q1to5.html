Behavioural interviews · The questions

The questions, most asked first

Each question starts with how it was actually put in 2026 PM interviews, taken from ISB students’ reports. Where a candidate wrote down what they actually said and how the interviewer reacted, that is shown in a “From a real interview” box, in summary. The worked answer after it is written for this guide and labelled so: the reports record questions, not full answers. The follow-up questions inside each answer are the kinds the reports show interviewers really asking.

Question 1 · Walk me through a projectAsked in 145 of 285 rounds
As actually askedDetailed deep-dive into a project where you showcased that skill-set … What component were you responsible for? (Sirion) · Tell me about the most complex project you have worked on, why it was that complex, and what you did to handle that (Ethos) · What would you want to change about it if you were to go back? (Salesforce)

What they’re checking. Whether you did what your resume says, whether you understood why, and whether you can think about your own work like a product. The first answer matters less than the third follow-up. Prepare every line on your resume with five parts ({{REF:deep}}): why it was needed, what you did, how you chose to do it, the impact, and what you’d do next.

From a real interview · Practo, 2026 (co-founder round)

The candidate described a user-acquisition project. The co-founder asked straight away: “What was the CAC?” (customer acquisition cost: what it cost to win one user). The candidate didn’t remember the exact figure and chose not to guess, because the interviewer could easily have shown a made-up number didn’t add up. Instead they said they didn’t recall the exact number, but they knew it was too high to keep going, and that was why the team shut that channel down. The interviewer accepted this and moved to a spreadsheet problem on costs and break-even, to check the candidate was comfortable with numbers. The candidate listed that honesty as one of the things that “worked in favour”.

Source: ISB BTC Handbook 2026, interview reports, pp. 107–108. Summarised.

Example answer (written for this guide) · analyst at a general insurer, 2 years
Q

Walk me through the claims-status project on your resume.

A

Customers who filed a motor claim had no way to see where it stood, so they called us. About 9,000 “where is my claim?” calls a month, roughly a third of all our calls. I was the analyst on the claims team. I proposed a simple status page with SMS updates and owned it from the idea to the launch, working with two developers.

Q

What exactly did you do yourself?

A

Three things. I pulled six months of call logs and found that 70% of these calls came in the two days after the surveyor’s visit, when nothing visible happened. I turned our 14 internal claim stages into 5 that a customer understands. And I wrote the SMS that goes out whenever a claim moves to the next stage.

Why this move: “three things” with a number each makes the answer about the speaker. The call-log finding shows the idea came from evidence.

Q

Why five stages and not fourteen?

A

Customers don’t care about “pending level-2 approval”. They want to know if someone is working on it and when the money comes. Fewer stages also meant fewer confusing messages when a claim went back a step internally. The cost was that agents lost detail, so we kept all 14 stages on their screen.

Q

And the result?

A

Status calls fell from about 9,000 to 7,400 a month within a quarter, about 18% down. I had hoped for 30%. When I read the remaining calls, half were about how much would be paid, not the stage. If I went back, I’d show the approved amount and the expected payout date. That’s the question behind the question.

Why this move: admitting the miss, and knowing why, is the strongest part of the answer. Interviewers keep asking “what would you change?” because it shows whether you think like a PM about your own work.

Weak line
“We built a dashboard that really improved efficiency across the team.”
Better line
“I built the status page; ‘where is my claim’ calls fell about 18% in a quarter, short of my 30% goal, and here’s why.”

If you don’t know the number: say so, and give the direction and what you did about it, as the Practo candidate did. A guessed number that falls apart under one follow-up costs more than an honest “I don’t remember exactly”.

Question 2 · Tell me about yourself28 rounds, usually the opener
As actually askedWalk me through the work you have done, in one line for each role (Sirion) · Tell me about yourself. Why do you want to be a product manager? Why do you want to join us? (2026 report, p. 161)

What they’re checking. Whether your choices make sense together, and whether you can be clear in 90 seconds. Don’t recite the resume in date order; they have it in front of them. Explain the choices behind it and the thread that joins them, then land on why this role ({{REF:tmay}}).

From a real interview · Practo, 2026

One candidate described planting “2–3 curiosity cues” in their introduction: small details that make the interviewer want to ask more, so the next question is on ground the candidate knows well. They also looked up the interviewer on LinkedIn beforehand, which gave them common points to talk about from the start, and reworded their introduction to match the company’s mission and the role. ISB BTC Handbook 2026, p. 107. Summarised.

Example answer (written for this guide) · career switcher: mechanical engineer → supply chain → MBA
A

I’m Arjun. I studied mechanical engineering in Pune because I liked fixing machines that others had given up on. I joined an FMCG company as a supply-chain analyst, and spent three years in depots in Maharashtra. The work I enjoyed most was building a simple stock tracker that 28 of our 30 depots ended up using. It cut month-end stock-outs by more than half. That’s when I noticed the part I liked wasn’t the spreadsheet. It was watching how depot managers really worked and making something they’d actually use. That’s why I came to XLRI, and why I spent my summer at a fintech, where I shipped a small fix to the KYC flow that lifted sign-ups by about 6%. I want to be a PM because I want to own the whole problem, not one piece of it. And your lending app for small shops fits, because I’ve spent three years seeing how small businesses think about stock and cash.

Q

You said depot managers “really worked” a certain way. What did you see?

Why this move: the depot detail was a curiosity cue. The interviewer picked it up, so the next five minutes are on the candidate’s strongest story.

Weak opening
“I did my B.Tech in 2019, then I joined XYZ as an analyst, where my responsibilities included…”
Better opening
“I studied mechanical because I liked fixing machines others gave up on. That habit is the thread through everything since.”
Question 3 · Why product management? Why now?23 rounds
As actually askedWhy PM? Why now? … What is PM in one word? (HiLabs) · why product after such deeply technical SDE roles (Razorpay) · Why PM and not coding? (Microsoft)

What they’re checking. Whether you know what the job is really like, and whether you want it for a real reason. The weak answer is a template: “I love solving problems and working across teams.” Every candidate that season says it. The strong answer names a real moment when you did PM-like work, what you liked about it, and what you still need to learn.

From a real interview · HiLabs, 2026

After “Why PM? Why now?”, one interviewer asked candidates to describe PM in one word. According to the report, he was listening for “prioritisation”. The candidate felt the round was “more of a personality test”. The lesson: have a short, honest view of what the job is mostly about, in your own words. Choosing what not to build is a good one. ISB BTC Handbook 2026, p. 83. Summarised.

Example answer (written for this guide) · software engineer, 2 years at a payments company
A

In my second year as an engineer, our refund API was failing for about 2% of requests. I fixed the bug in a day. But I kept reading the support tickets, and saw that the bigger problem was that merchants couldn’t tell whether a refund had gone through. I wrote a one-page note proposing a refund-status webhook, got my manager to take it to the product team, and it shipped a quarter later. Tickets on refunds fell by about a third. Fixing the bug was satisfying. Deciding what the merchant actually needed was the part I couldn’t stop thinking about.

Q

Why not stay an engineer and do that on the side?

A

I did, for a year, and it only worked because a PM made room for my note. I want to be the person who decides what gets built and answers for it. My engineering background helps with trade-offs. What I still need is practice with users outside payments and with saying no, which is why I did my summer on a consumer app.

Why this move: naming a gap (saying no, non-payments users) shows you know what the job is. The “why now” is answered by the summer project, not by a slogan.

Template
“PM sits at the intersection of business, tech and design, and I love all three.”
Real
“I fixed the bug in a day, but kept reading the tickets. Deciding what the merchant needed was the part I couldn’t stop thinking about.”
Question 4 · Why this company?21 rounds
As actually askedWhy do you want to join Acko? (Acko) · Why Affinity Global? … What makes you a better fit vs. others? (Affinity Global) · Values of Atlassian and which one value will you pick? (Atlassian)

What they’re checking. Whether you’ve used their product and have a view on it, and the unspoken part: why you, over the forty others who also read the website. Praise alone doesn’t answer it. The Acko report put it well: having a dialogue about their strategy, with your own point of view, “goes a long way in showing interest”.

From a real interview · Microsoft, 2026

Asked “Why Microsoft?”, the candidate said they wanted to be at a cutting-edge tech company. The interviewer tested it at once: what interesting technology had they learned about recently? The candidate talked about design patterns for real-time collaborative editing. They couldn’t recall the exact algorithms, and the report says “that wasn’t a problem”. The interviewer then asked them to design a collaborative text editor. Any general reason you give for joining will be tested, so have something real behind it. ISB BTC Handbook 2026, p. 101. Summarised.

At Practo, another candidate brought a short, unasked-for document on the company: its user groups, their pain points, what they liked and disliked about the product, and a rough wireframe. The interviewer asked them to send it over. p. 108.

Example answer (written for this guide) · fresher, for a PM role at PhonePe
A

I’ve used PhonePe for five years, and I’ve watched my father’s hardware shop in Nagpur move from cash to the PhonePe speaker on the counter. You’ve won payments: about 46% of UPI transactions in August 2026. But UPI earns almost nothing per payment, so the next few years are about what you build on top, like merchant loans and the devices on the counter. Closing Pincode in December 2025 told me you’re choosing where to focus. That’s the part I want to work on: turning a shop’s payment history into a loan it can trust. I’ve seen the other side of that from my father, who was turned down by two banks because his sales were “on the phone”.

Q

What would you change in the merchant app?

A

I’d show a shopkeeper why their loan offer is the size it is, and what would raise it. My father ignores offers he doesn’t understand.

Why this move: a real personal link, one sourced fact, a view on their strategy and one concrete idea. It is also specific enough that no other candidate could say it.

Facts: UPI share from NPCI data (Aug 2026, via Inc42); Pincode closure (TechCrunch, 5 Dec 2025). Check such facts again on the day; they date quickly.

Question 5 · How do you prioritise? A time you said no19 rounds
As actually askedA time you didn’t build a feature despite planning to, what made you stop, how did you communicate it (Ethos) · Example of a time you gave up short-term gains for long-term value (Ethos) · How would you prioritise features … how would you present a case to senior leadership (2026 report, p. 78)

What they’re checking. Whether you compare options against a goal, instead of doing whatever the loudest person asks. And whether you can say no without making an enemy. Put the requests on value against effort ({{REF:prio}}), then explain the one you turned down.

Example answer (written for this guide) · summer intern, product team at a lending app
A

In my internship I had one engineer for three weeks and five requests. The team’s goal that quarter was completed loan applications. I found that 3% of sign-ups on Jio numbers never got their OTP, so fixing that was first: small effort, and every one of those users was lost at the door. The sales head wanted a custom weekly report, which would have taken two of the three weeks and helped one team.

Q

How did you tell the sales head no?

A

I met him with the numbers, not over chat. I showed what the OTP fix would add in applications, and offered a version of his report from our existing dashboard in two days, with 80% of what he needed. He took it. The OTP fix brought failed sign-ups on Jio down to under 1%.

Why this move: a “no” with a reason tied to the shared goal, plus a cheaper option, is what makes it a yes to something else.
