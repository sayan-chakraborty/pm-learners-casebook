Behavioural interviews

What this round is and how it runs

A behavioural question asks what you did, not what you would do. The idea is simple: how you handled a real disagreement last year tells an interviewer more than any promise about the future. In the 2026 placement reports that ISB students wrote up, 285 of 585 interview rounds were behavioural, HR or resume rounds ({{REF:themes}}). The single most common thing interviewers did was pick a line from the resume and keep asking “why?” and “how?” until the story either held or fell apart.

A round rarely follows a script. It usually opens with “tell me about yourself”, moves to one project on your resume, then drills: why that way? what did you do yourself? what was the number? what went wrong? Somewhere in the middle come “why PM” and “why us”. It can jump to a product question at any point: one candidate named Google Maps as their favourite product and was then asked to build a Maps feature for a specific group of users. So don’t prepare separate answers for separate rounds. Prepare a handful of true stories deeply, and you’ll be ready wherever the conversation goes.

What the interviewer is scoring

Behind the friendly chat, most interviewers fill in a short scorecard. The five things on it are the same almost everywhere ({{REF:score}}). Each one has an easy good sign and an easy red flag. Most candidates who fail this round are not bad people with bad stories. They are people who said “we” for three minutes, gave no number, or offered a weakness that was a boast in disguise.

Key idea · Your resume is the question paper

Half the round is built from what you wrote. Mastercard’s round was described as “detailed grilling on each and every point of the resume”. Before any interview, read your resume line by line and ask yourself: could I talk about this for two minutes, give a number, and say what I’d do differently? If not, fix the line or learn the story behind it.

Behavioural interviews · Story bank

Build a story bank

You can’t make up a good story on the spot, under pressure, in two minutes. So build a small bank before the interviews: six true stories, each known well enough that you can tell it from any angle. One story about a messy project can answer a conflict question, a prioritising question or a failure question, depending on which part you tell.

The six stories that cover almost everything

The ISB handbook found that nearly every “tell me about a time” question its students faced collapsed into a few underlying stories. For PM rounds, six are enough:

  1. A project you drove from start to finish (for deep-dives, “most proud of”, execution).
  2. A disagreement with a manager, a teammate or another team (for conflict, working with people).
  3. A real setback or failure, with what you changed afterwards (for failure, criticism, weakness).
  4. A time you persuaded people who didn’t report to you (for leadership, influence).
  5. A hard trade-off or a “no” (for prioritising, saying no, short term vs long term).
  6. A time data or a user changed your mind (for judgement, being wrong, curiosity).

The story card: bullets, not a script

Write each story as short bullet memories, never as a paragraph to learn by heart. A memorised speech breaks the moment the interviewer interrupts, and good interviewers interrupt on purpose. Bullets let you start anywhere. Here is one card, filled in, for the stock-tracker story ({{REF:card}}).

Behavioural interviews · Framework
Framework · STAR, used loosely

1The situation that needs it. A typical weak answer to “tell me about a time you failed”: three minutes on a college fest where “the team” missed a sponsor deadline, one line about the speaker, and “but it all worked out”. The interviewer learns a lot about the fest and nothing about the candidate.

2An everyday example. You come home at 1 a.m. and your parents are waiting up. They don’t want the full story of the party. They want three things: what happened, what you did about it, and whether it will happen again. If you spend ten minutes on who was at the party, you’re in trouble.

3The framework. STAR stands for Situation (the background, briefly), Task (what you had to get done), Action (what you did, and why) and Result (what changed, with a number if you have one). PM interviewers almost always add a fifth part: what you learned or would do differently. The key is the time split. About half of a two-minute answer should be Action, because that is the only part that shows you ({{REF:budget}}).

Using it, step by step

  1. Start with one sentence of context and one of your task. If you need a third, cut something.
  2. Tell your actions in order, using “I”. For each important one, add the reason: “I did X because Y.”
  3. Give the result as a number (“stock-outs from 14% to 6%”) or, if you have no number, a clear direction (“complaints stopped”).
  4. End with one line on what you’d change or now do differently. Then stop talking.

Common slip Using “we” all through the action. Saying “we” for the team’s result is fine. For the actions, say which part was yours.

4When it misleads. STAR is a checklist for preparing a story, not a script for telling it. The ISB handbook warns that forcing every answer through four boxes sounds rehearsed. A candidate who says “The situation was… the task was…” out loud sounds like they’re reading a form. And interviewers will interrupt after the situation to ask “why?”, which breaks a memorised run. Like a recipe you know well, you don’t read out the steps; you just cook.

5In the interview. Don’t announce the boxes. Say something like: “Short version of the background, then what I did, then how it turned out: …” and go.
