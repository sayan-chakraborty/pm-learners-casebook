Try it first. You have been watching Netflix on your cousin’s account in another city for two years. Tomorrow it stops working. What is the most you would pay for your own plan? Would ₹99 to stay on your cousin’s account change your answer? Five minutes.

What worked · what was missing
  • Restated a two-part goal: more paying members and more revenue from former sharers.
  • Found the new segment, the “converted sharer”, and described what that person needs: low price, keep my profile.
  • Asked for competitor prices instead of guessing, and rightly set cost-plus pricing aside.
  • Recited a framework. “5 Cs” filled time; only the customer segments were used in the recommendation.
  • Recommended something Netflix chose not to do in India. When sharing rules reached India in July 2023, Netflix did not offer the paid “extra member” here, because prices had already been cut. The first question should have been “what do sharers in India get today?”
  • No test of what people would pay, and no count of existing members who might move to a cheaper arrangement.
  • Stale competitor price. JioHotstar’s Premium annual plan rose from ₹1,499 to ₹2,199 in January 2026. Prices move; say which date they are from.

Check · “The variable cost of serving every additional subscriber is close to null” Directionally right

Content is fixed, so the big cost doesn’t grow with members. But some costs do: 18% GST comes off every rupee, payment fees take 1–2%, a telecom bundle gives the operator a cut, and a 4K viewer uses several times the bandwidth of a phone viewer. On a ₹149 plan, GST alone is about ₹23. “Small” is right; “close to null” is too strong, and it matters most on the cheapest plans.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

Two checks. Is the goal paying members, revenue, or both over the next year? And what happens today to someone in India using a relative’s account in another city: can they pay to stay, or must they get their own plan?

Q

Both, over a year. In India they must get their own plan; they can move their profile across.

Why this move: whether an extra-member option exists decides the whole answer. One question avoids recommending a product that isn’t there.

A
② Segments

Four groups, each with a different price in mind. Young phone-first viewers: ₹149 or less. Families with a smart TV: will pay ₹499–649 for screens and picture quality. Former sharers: have never paid, so any price feels like a loss. And telecom customers: they get Netflix inside a recharge and never see its price.

Why this move: pricing is about what each group would pay, so the segments must be described by money and habit, not by age alone.

A
③ Price ladder

Netflix costs more than its rivals at every step except the phone plan ({{REF:ladder63}}). JioHotstar’s phone plan is ₹79 a month; Prime costs about ₹125 a month on the annual fee and includes delivery. So Netflix shouldn’t race to the bottom. Its job is to make the first paid step, ₹149, an easy yes.

Why this move: laying the prices side by side shows where Netflix competes (phone plan) and where it charges for quality (TV plans), which keeps the fix narrow.

A
④ Test what they’ll pay

Before choosing a price, ask a sample of former sharers: would you pay ₹99? ₹149? ₹199? Illustrative answers: 70%, 45%, 25%. Revenue per 100 sharers: 70 × ₹99 = ₹6,930; 45 × ₹149 = ₹6,705; 25 × ₹199 = ₹4,975. The two lower prices earn almost the same ({{REF:wtp63}}).

Why this move: a quick survey turns “price-sensitive” into a curve, and the curve shows that going from ₹149 to ₹99 adds people but hardly any money.

A
⑤ Count who moves down

Now compare the two options for 1 crore sharer households (illustrative). Own plan at ₹149: 25% sign up: 25 lakh × ₹149 = ₹37 Cr a month. Extra member at ₹99: 45% pay: 45 lakh × ₹99 = ₹45 Cr. But 10 lakh households that pay ₹499 for a second screen used by a sister elsewhere would switch to ₹199 + ₹99 = ₹298, losing ₹201 each: ₹20 Cr. Net ₹25 Cr. The own-plan route earns more ({{REF:share63}}).

Why this move: a cheaper option is sold to new people but also chosen by existing ones. Counting both is what separates a pricing answer from a guess, and here it explains Netflix’s real choice.

A
⑥ Recommend and measure

Keep “get your own plan”, make ₹149 the landing plan with a one-tap profile transfer, and reach the most price-sensitive through Jio and Airtel bundles rather than a lower list price. Test an annual plan with a small discount to cut churn after big releases. Goal (North Star): revenue: paying members × revenue per member. Inputs: sharers converting within 30 days; profiles transferred; phone-to-TV upgrades. Safety checks (guardrails): cancellations and downgrades among existing members. Trade-off: a firm sharing rule gains revenue but annoys families spread across cities.

Why this move: the recommendation follows the numbers, and the guardrail watches the exact risk found in step ⑤: existing members trading down.

How the answer hangs togetherThe “today” question showed that no extra-member option exists in India. The survey showed ₹99 and ₹149 earn about the same from sharers, and counting existing members who would trade down made ₹149 the better price. Bundles reach the most price-sensitive without cutting the list price, and the guardrail watches downgrades, the risk the maths exposed.
Framework · Tiered pricing: good, better, best

1 · The situation.One Netflix catalogue is sold at four prices, from ₹149 to ₹649. The question is how to set those steps so each kind of viewer picks the plan meant for them, and nobody who would pay more slips down to a cheaper one.

2 · An everyday example.Indian Railways. The same train, the same journey, the same arrival time. A sleeper berth, a 3AC berth and a 2AC berth cost very different amounts. A student going home takes sleeper; a family takes 3AC; a manager on a company trip takes 2AC. You can’t sit in 2AC with a sleeper ticket: the ticket checker enforces the fence.

3 · The framework.Tiered pricing sells one product at a few levels, usually three: good, better, best. Each level adds something a particular group values. A fence (a rule that stops people using a cheaper tier the way the dearer one is used) keeps people in the right tier. Charging different people different prices for nearly the same thing is price discrimination; the fences are device (phone-only plans), time (annual plans, student plans), bundle (inside a recharge) and country (Netflix costs far less in India than in the US). A decoy is a tier that exists mainly to make another look good: at a cinema, small popcorn ₹250, medium ₹380, large ₹400 makes large feel like a bargain. To set the steps, run a simple willingness-to-pay test: ask a sample whether they would pay at four or five prices, and multiply the share who say yes by the price ({{REF:wtp63}}).

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. List the groups and what each values most: price (phone viewers), screens and quality (families).
  2. Give each tier one clear thing the tier below lacks, and one fence that stops trading down.
  3. Test prices with the would-you-pay questions, and compute revenue at each price.
  4. Count who moves: new buyers of a cheaper tier and existing customers who drop to it.
  5. Launch the smallest change first, in one city or for one group, and watch downgrades.

Common slip · judging a new cheap tier only by the new customers it wins. The bill arrives from existing customers who move down to it.

Real-life example · A family plan, worked

In the casebook’s JioSaavn case, one person pays ₹99 a month and Spotify sells a six-person family plan for ₹179. Suppose JioSaavn launches a family plan at ₹149. A house where only the father paid now pays ₹149: +₹50, and three more people build listening habits, which makes the family less likely to cancel. But a house where four people each paid ₹99 (₹396) moves to one ₹149 plan: −₹247. So a family plan pays only where most families had one payer. The fence is “same home address”, checked the way Spotify does, by location.

4 · When it misleads.Tiers that are too close make everyone pick the cheapest: if Basic at ₹199 differed from Standard at ₹499 only by one extra screen, few families would pay for Standard. And discrimination can feel unfair: in 1999, Coca-Cola’s chief spoke of vending machines that would charge more on hot days, and the public anger forced the company to drop the idea. A fence that looks like punishment costs more than it earns.

5 · In the interview.“I’d keep three clear tiers, each with one fence, test what each group would pay, and count how many current customers would drop to any new cheaper option before launching it.”
