Blockchain: a shared record that nobody can quietly change

Twenty families in a Pune housing society run a savings group, a bhishi. Each month every family puts in ₹5,000 and one family takes the ₹1 lakh pot. Mrs Kulkarni, the secretary, keeps the account book. In the third year, a family says it paid in March; the book says it didn’t. Did it forget, or did someone change the page? Nobody can prove either. Now imagine that every family keeps its own copy of the book, every entry is read out and written in all twenty copies, and each page carries a seal made from the page before it. To cheat, you would have to rewrite the same page in more than half the books, and every page after it, without anyone noticing. That is the whole idea of a blockchain. Making it work takes a seal that shows any change, a fair rule for who writes the next page, and a reason good enough to justify the effort.

1. A block: one page of entries

Everyday picture. One page of the society book: the date, three entries, and the seal from the previous page at the top.

How it works. A block holds a batch of transactions, a time stamp, the fingerprint of the previous block, and its own fingerprint ({{REF:block}}). The fingerprint is a hash: a short code worked out from the block’s contents, the same kind of fingerprint apps use to store passwords safely. The same contents always give the same hash, and changing even one character gives a completely different one. Real SHA-256 outputs: “Hello” gives 185f8db3…, “hello” gives 2cf24dba…. One capital letter, a new fingerprint.

2. The chain: why old pages can’t be changed quietly

Everyday picture. Each page’s seal is pressed partly over the previous page’s seal. Tear out page 12 and rewrite it, and the seal on page 13 no longer lines up.

How it works. Block 102 contains block 101’s fingerprint. If someone edits block 101, its fingerprint changes, so block 102 now points at a fingerprint that doesn’t exist ({{REF:chain}}). To hide the edit, the cheat must redo 102, then 103, and every block after it, and do it on more than half of all the copies, faster than honest writers add new blocks. On a large network, that is practically impossible. This property is called being tamper-evident: changes are not impossible, but they are always visible.

Real example. Bitcoin’s ledger has run since January 2009 with no one able to rewrite an old block. PM decision: tamper-evidence is valuable only when people fear the record keeper might change the record. If everyone trusts the keeper (a bank, the RBI, the railways), a normal database with an audit log gives the same comfort for a tiny fraction of the cost.

3. Consensus: who gets to write the next page?

Everyday picture. If any family could write the next page whenever it liked, two families could write different pages at once, or one could write only entries that suit it. The society needs a fair rule for choosing the writer, and a way for everyone to check the page.

How it works, proof of work. Bitcoin runs a lottery that costs electricity to enter. To write the next block, a computer must find a number (a nonce) that, added to the block, makes its hash start with a set number of zeros. There is no shortcut: you guess, hash, and check. On a laptop, making block 102’s hash start with four zeros took 31,312 guesses (the average is 164 = 65,536). Anyone can check the winner’s answer with one hash. Bitcoin sets the target so the whole network finds one block about every 10 minutes; in 2025 that meant roughly 1023 guesses per block ({{REF:consensus}}). Cheating means out-guessing everyone else combined, which needs more than half the world’s mining power: a 51% attack.

How it works, proof of stake. Instead of burning electricity, writers lock up a deposit of coins. The network picks the next writer at random, weighted by deposit, and others check the block. A writer who signs two different blocks or lies loses part of the deposit (slashing). Ethereum switched from work to stake on 15 September 2022 and cut its electricity use by more than 99.9%.

Real example, and the cost. Cambridge University estimates Bitcoin uses around 150 terawatt-hours a year, about as much as a mid-sized country, for roughly 4–5 lakh transactions a day. That works out to roughly 900 kilowatt-hours per transaction, close to what an average Indian home uses in a year (estimate). A UPI payment uses a tiny fraction of one kilowatt-hour. Small chains have suffered real 51% attacks: Ethereum Classic had blocks rewritten several times in 2020. PM decision: the consensus method decides cost, speed and energy. Bitcoin handles about 7 payments a second; UPI handles thousands. If a product needs speed and low fees for everyday payments, a public proof-of-work chain is the wrong base.

4. Wallets and keys: “not your keys, not your coins”

Everyday picture. A letterbox outside your flat. Anyone who knows the address can drop a letter in; only the person with the key can open it and take things out.

How it works. A crypto wallet holds a private key, and from it an address that others can pay into, a public/private key pair (two linked keys: what the private key signs, anyone can check with the public one). To spend, the wallet signs the payment with the private key; every node checks the signature with the public key. There is no password reset: the key is usually backed up as a seed phrase of 12 or 24 words. Lose it, and the coins are gone for ever. Share it, and they can be taken for ever. Chainalysis has estimated that around a fifth of all bitcoins are lost or stuck in wallets nobody can open. Many people let an exchange hold their keys instead ({{REF:custody}}). That is easier, but it means trusting the exchange: in July 2024 hackers took about $230 million from the Indian exchange WazirX, and users’ withdrawals were frozen for months.

PM decision: key recovery is the biggest user-experience problem in crypto. Every design choice (a seed phrase, recovery through friends, an exchange holding the key) trades freedom from middlemen against the chance of losing everything.

5. Smart contracts: a vending machine made of code

Everyday picture. A vending machine at a railway station. Put in ₹20, press B4, and a bottle drops out. No shopkeeper decides; the rules are built into the machine.

How it works. A smart contract is a small program stored on a blockchain (usually Ethereum) that holds money and moves it when conditions are met. Worked example, an escrow ({{REF:escrow}}): Nisha, a designer in Kochi, agrees to make a logo for a client in Dubai for 500 dollar-tokens (a stablecoin: a token meant to stay worth one dollar). The client locks 500 tokens in the contract. When Nisha delivers and the client clicks “approve”, the contract pays her. If the client goes silent for 14 days after delivery, it pays her anyway. If the client raises a dispute, it waits for an agreed arbitrator. Nobody can run off with the money in between.

Where it breaks. Code does exactly what it says, bugs included, and on a blockchain you can’t quietly patch it. In June 2016 an attacker used a flaw in “The DAO”, a smart-contract investment fund, to drain about 3.6 million ether, then worth around $50 million. Ethereum’s community reversed it only by splitting the whole chain in two. And a contract only knows what is on the chain: a delivery, a match result or a price must be fed in from outside (the oracle problem), and whoever feeds it in must be trusted. PM decision: smart contracts need audits before launch, limits on how much money they hold, and a plan for disputes, because “code is law” also means “bugs are law”.

7. Public or private: who may join, and who may write

Everyday picture. A notice board in a railway station, which anyone may read and anyone may pin to, against a housing society’s minutes book, which only members see and only the committee writes in.

How it works. Two questions sort every blockchain. Who may read and join: anyone, or only approved members? And who may write and check new blocks: anyone, or only approved members? A public, permissionless chain (Bitcoin, Ethereum) lets anyone do both; that is what makes it hard to censor, and also what makes it slow and costly. A private, permissioned chain (built on tools such as Hyperledger Fabric or R3 Corda) lets only named organisations join and write. It is fast and keeps data private, but it only works if members trust the group that approves them.

Anyone may write and checkOnly approved members write and check
Anyone may readPublic, permissionless: Bitcoin, Ethereum. Open, slow, costly per transactionPublic, permissioned: a registry anyone can inspect but only approved bodies update
Only members readPrivate, permissionless: rare in practicePrivate, permissioned: banks sharing trade papers; a consortium of exporters. Fast, private

PM decision: a private chain answers most business needs, but ask the awkward question: if a handful of known members run it, why not one shared database with signed entries and an audit log? The honest answer must be “because no member will let another run it”, as TradeLens found out.

8. When a blockchain is not the answer

A blockchain is a slow, costly database with one special power: it lets people who don’t trust each other, and don’t trust any one keeper, share a record nobody can quietly change. Five questions test whether you need that power ({{REF:bctree}}). Most proposals fail at the second or third.

In the interview · How blockchain comes up
  • “Should we use blockchain for X?” Ask who writes, who they don’t trust, and whether one keeper would do. Name the oracle problem: the chain can’t check that the data was true when entered.
  • “Explain blockchain to a ten-year-old.” A class notebook that every student copies, where each page is sealed with the one before, so nobody can change an old page without everyone noticing.
  • “Why hasn’t the e-rupee taken off?” UPI already moves money free and instantly; the e-rupee must offer something UPI can’t, such as money programmed for one purpose.
