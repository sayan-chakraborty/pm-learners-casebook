Try it first. Think of the last time you paid for a streaming app for one thing, a cricket season or one series. Did you keep paying afterwards? What would have kept you? Now structure “improve Hotstar to increase loyalty” out loud for five minutes.

What worked · what was missing
  • Asked for the stage and the goal first, and got a clear one: loyalty, in a crowded market.
  • Split users by how they behave, not by age, which is the right kind of split for an engagement question.
  • Linked each pain point to a feature and put the features in one table before choosing.
  • Never found where loyalty leaks. Hotstar’s biggest loss is the lakhs of people who join for cricket and leave when it ends. None of the seven features touches them.
  • Chose the smallest segments. Movie critics and explorers are a few percent of users; loyalty is decided by the mass of casual and sports viewers.
  • “RICE” without numbers. Letters (H, M, L) can’t be multiplied. The forum is rated High effort yet ranked first; in RICE, high effort pulls a score down.
  • No goal metric. Five metrics, none named as the one that shows loyalty (people still watching and paying months later).
  • Didn’t check what exists. Actor and character lookup already exists in India as Prime Video’s X-Ray.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

By loyalty, do we mean people who keep paying and keep watching, month after month? Which subscribers: paying directly, or also those who get Hotstar with a Jio recharge? And is there a moment when loyalty drops? I’ll assume India and paying subscribers.

Q

Yes, renewals. Direct payers. Many people join in March for the IPL.

Why this move: “loyalty” can mean likes, visits or payments. Pinning it to renewals turns a vague brief into something you can count. The question about timing is what surfaced the IPL.

A
② Find the leak

Then I’d compare groups of people by the month they joined. Say 100 people join in January and 100 join in March for the IPL. By September, perhaps 65 of the January group still pay, against 28 of the March group ({{REF:retcurves}}). The March group isn’t unhappy with the app. They came for one thing, and it ended.

Why this move: following each joining group separately shows a cliff that the total number of subscribers hides, because new joiners keep replacing the ones who leave.

A
③ Size

Illustrative: if 3 crore people subscribe for the IPL and 40% renew after June, that is 1.2 crore kept. Lifting renewal to 50% keeps 30 lakh more. On a ₹349 quarterly plan: 30,00,000 × ₹349 = about ₹105 crore a quarter, with no extra content cost, because the shows are already paid for.

Why this move: a rupee figure shows the interviewer the prize is large, and the “no extra content cost” part explains why keeping a subscriber is worth more than a new feature for a few enthusiasts.

A
④ Who

The person to design for is Ravi, 26, a sales executive in Indore. During the IPL he opens the app four days in five, always for the match, sometimes for highlights. He has never started a serial or a film on Hotstar. On 1 June he has no reason to open it, so at renewal he doesn’t.

Why this move: the candidate designed for critics and explorers, who already love content. Ravi is the one who leaves, so he is the one whose behaviour has to change.

A
⑤ Bets

Three ideas, the ones that score highest once RICE is done with numbers. 1. Pause, don’t cancel: when Ravi taps cancel, offer to pause for up to three months at ₹0, keeping his profile, and restart for the next tournament. 2. “After the IPL” watchlist: during the season, learn what else he might like from small signals (he watches the Mumbai team, he skips ads with film trailers) and show a short list of shows at the final over of the final. 3. Cricket all year: a calendar of the next matches (India tours, other leagues), with reminders he can switch on in one tap.

Why this move: each idea attacks the June cliff differently. The first makes returning easy, the second gives a reason to stay, the third makes cricket less seasonal.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): share of IPL joiners who are still watching every week, 60 days after the final. Inputs: non-cricket titles started during the season; pause uptake; reminders switched on. Safety check (guardrail): revenue per subscriber, because a free pause could tempt loyal all-year payers to pause too. Trade-off: pushing serials during the IPL takes screen space from match ads, which pay the rights bill.

Why this move: counting people still watching, not only paying, catches the silent leavers early: people who stop watching cancel at the next renewal.

How the answer hangs togetherLoyalty became renewals. Joining groups showed where renewals fail: after the IPL. The ₹105 crore sum sized the prize, Ravi made it human, and every bet aims at his June. The North Star (still watching 60 days later) and the guardrail (revenue per subscriber) measure exactly that cliff and the risk of the fix.
Framework · Retention curves and cohorts

1 · The situation.Hotstar’s total paying base looks healthy in April. But the people paying in April are not the people who paid last September. To see who stays, you have to follow each joining group on its own.

2 · An everyday example.A gym on 1 January. Sixty people join in the New Year rush. By March, twenty still come. In June, twelve are left, and those twelve keep coming for years. Meanwhile the owner sees 200 members on the register and feels fine, because each month’s new joiners hide those who quietly stopped.

3 · The framework.A cohort is a group of users who started in the same period, such as everyone who subscribed in a given week. A retention curve plots the share of one cohort still active after 1, 2, 3… periods. Three shapes matter. A curve that flattens means the product has found people it keeps. A curve that falls towards zero means nobody stays: fix the product before buying growth. A curve with a cliff means something outside the product ended, like the cricket season. Put cohorts in rows and months across, and colour the cells: the table ({{REF:cohort}}) shows at a glance whether newer cohorts keep people better than older ones.

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. Choose what “retained” means: here, watched at least once in the week, not merely paying.
  2. Group users by the week or month they joined, and mark the ones who joined for an event.
  3. Draw one curve per group and name its shape: flattening, falling to zero, or cliff.
  4. Compare a cohort with the same cohort a year earlier (IPL 2026 joiners against IPL 2025 joiners), never with a different season.
  5. Tie each fix to a point on the curve: the pause and the watchlist aim at the June cliff.

Common slip · comparing the IPL cohort with the January cohort and calling IPL joiners “low quality”. They behave differently for a known reason; compare like with like.

4 · When it misleads.Bundled users can make a curve look flat. A Jio recharge renews JioHotstar automatically, so a cohort of bundled users “stays” even if nobody opens the app. Count watching, not paying. Small cohorts also mislead: a test campaign that brought in 200 people can swing ten points either way by chance.

5 · In the interview.“Before choosing features, I’d look at retention by joining month; if the IPL cohort falls off a cliff in June, that is the loyalty problem to solve.”
