Back to Ahmedabad · A sketch answer for the final-night question

The eight seconds. A slow start on the busiest night of the year is a solved kind of problem. It has a playbook: servers switched on before the toss, logged-in phones skipping login, a player that starts on a low rung and steps up, and a ladder of features to turn off before the match is touched. Eight seconds to start, then a blurry picture that sharpens, is the system working as designed. A freeze in the middle of the last over would be far worse, and that is what start time and rebuffering ratio are watched for, city by city.

The June cancellations. This is the bigger business problem. In the illustrative cohort, about two in three IPL joiners leave within a month of the final. Lifting that renewal by ten points is worth about ₹105 crore a quarter, with no new content cost. The fixes are product work: a pause instead of a cancel, an “after the IPL” watchlist built during the season, and a calendar of the next cricket.

So which worries more? The June cancellations, because they repeat every year and cost money quietly. The buffering matters as a condition: nobody renews an app that failed them during the final. Goal (North Star): IPL joiners still watching weekly 60 days after the final. Safety checks (guardrails): start time and rebuffering ratio on final night; revenue per subscriber. Trade-off: promoting serials during matches earns tomorrow’s renewals but takes screen space from tonight’s ads.

Key idea · Pay once, then earn between the big moments

Streaming and music businesses pay a large, fixed sum for what people want, then need as many people as possible, for as long as possible, to pay for it through plans, ads or bundles. Big moments (a final, a new season) bring people in. What happens between the moments decides the business. When you design, price or measure anything here, ask: what brings people in, what keeps them once it ends, and who will move to a cheaper option if we offer one?
