Case 6.2 · Prime Video views fall 20% Root causeHardIIM B Sigma PM Casebook 2026–27, pp. 80–81
Try it first. A “view” is counted when someone watches 70% of a title. Views per day have fallen 20% over a month, everywhere in India. Write down every way that number could fall without a single person cancelling. Five minutes.
{{CASE:B80}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Excellent clarifying questions: the exact definition of a view, how big, since when, sudden or gradual, which platforms, which regions and groups.
  • Played the problem back in one sentence before starting.
  • A sharp answer on releases: a change rolled out slowly would cause a slow fall.
  • Didn’t use the definition. Views = viewers × titles started × share watched to 70%. A 20% fall can hide in any of the three, and two of them don’t need anyone to leave.
  • External checks were guesses, not data. The interviewer had to reveal the cause. Splitting light and heavy viewers would have found it.
  • The fix doesn’t match the cause. People left for a free app. Paying per season is still paying.
  • Pay-per-title already exists. Prime Video’s rental store has sold single films in India since 2022.

Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing ✓ · Exact definition ✓ · Size and timing ✓ · Where it shows ✓ platform, region, group · Recent changes ✓ · Is the data trustworthy ✗ · Split of the metric ✗. A model opening. The one skipped question, “has the way we count a view changed?”, is worth asking whenever a fall is smooth and everywhere.

Check · “We need an innovative way of pricing such as sachet pricing” Doesn’t hold up

Two problems. First, Amazon already sells single films in India through the Prime Video rental store (since October 2022) and offers cheaper Prime plans such as Prime Lite. Second, Amazon’s real answer to free rivals was free: its ad-supported miniTV, merged with MX Player into Amazon MX Player in 2024, and ads on Prime Video itself from June 2025. A viewer who left for free content won’t come back to pay for a season of Lupin.

Key idea · A slow fall everywhere points outside the code

A bug or a bad release usually hits one platform or one app version, and hits suddenly. A fall that is gradual and the same on every device, region and group usually means the world changed (a rival, a season, a habit) or what you offer changed (fewer new releases, longer shows), or the way you count changed. Check those three before opening the app’s logs.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

A view is 70% of a title watched, and views per day are down 20%, gradually, over a month, on every platform and in every region. Has anything changed in how we count a view, for example a new version of the player that logs progress differently?

Q

No change in counting.

Why this move: a counting change is the cheapest explanation to check and the most embarrassing one to miss. It takes one question.

A
② Split the number

Views per day = people watching that day × titles each person starts × share of starts watched to 70%. Illustrative: 1 crore viewers × 1.5 starts × 60% = 90 lakh views. A 20% fall means 72 lakh. That happens if viewers fall to 80 lakh, or starts fall to 1.2, or the finish rate falls to 48% ({{REF:viewtree62}}). Which part moved?

Q

Daily viewers are down about 15%. Starts per viewer dipped a little. The finish rate is flat.

Why this move: each part has different causes. Fewer viewers means people went elsewhere; fewer starts means a weaker catalogue or home screen; a lower finish rate means longer shows or a player problem. Asking which part moved saves ten guesses.

A
③ Rule out

Since viewers are down everywhere and gradually, it isn’t a crash or one bad release. There was no IPL, no price change and no new rule. What about our own catalogue: were there fewer new releases this month than last year?

Q

The release calendar was normal.

Why this move: a thin month of releases is the most common internal cause of a slow fall in viewing, and it would look exactly like this.

A
④ Find where

Then which viewers are missing? I’d split by how often people watch. If the 15% is mostly light viewers, people who watched one or two evenings a week, they are the easiest to lose to something free and good enough.

Q

Yes: mostly light viewers. They are watching films on a new free app.

Why this move: the split by viewing habit turns “users left” into “one kind of user left, for one kind of reason”, which is something you can design for.

A
⑤ Cause

For many light viewers, Prime Video came free with Prime, which they bought for delivery. They didn’t cancel Prime; they just stopped opening Prime Video. So revenue hasn’t fallen yet. The risk comes at renewal: if Prime’s video part feels unused, Prime feels less worth ₹1,499.

Why this move: naming who pays and why tells you how urgent this is and what to protect. Here it is Prime renewal, not a video subscription.

A
⑥ Fix

Meet free with free, and make choosing quick. 1. Bring Amazon’s free, ad-supported films into Prime Video’s home screen for light viewers, so the free option is inside our app. 2. A “tonight in under two hours” row: films and short series picked for people who watch once or twice a week. 3. For new blockbusters, keep renting single titles; don’t invent season passes that ask the same people to pay more.

Why this move: every fix aims at the light viewer and the reason they left (free, easy), rather than at a pricing idea that asks them for money.

A
⑦ Measure

Goal (North Star): Prime members who watch at least once a week. Inputs: days watched per light viewer; time from opening the app to pressing play. Safety check (guardrail): Prime renewal rate, and complaints about ads. Trade-off: more free, ad-supported titles bring light viewers back but can make paying for ad-free viewing feel less worth it.

Why this move: the goal counts watchers, the unit that fell; the guardrail watches Prime renewal, the money that is really at risk.

How the answer hangs togetherSplitting views into viewers × starts × finish rate showed that people, not minutes, went missing. Splitting people by habit showed they were light viewers lured by free films. So the fixes are free and quick to choose, the goal counts weekly watchers, and the guardrail protects Prime renewal, which is where this problem would finally cost money.
