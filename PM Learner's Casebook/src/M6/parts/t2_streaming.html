System design V: one IPL final, to crores of phones at once

7:28 pm, final night. In the next five minutes, several crore people will open JioHotstar, log in, perhaps renew a plan, and ask for the same few seconds of video at the same moment. Priya’s phone on the hostel steps has a patchy 4G signal; her father’s smart TV in Rajkot is on fibre; a truck driver near Nagpur is on a ₹199 data pack. Each of them should get the best picture their connection can carry, the match should never stop, and the ads in every over-break must be sold and shown. Follow one ball from the stadium to those three screens.

The load, in numbers

Suppose 6.5 crore phones and TVs watch at once, the March 2026 record. At an average of 1.5 megabits per second each, that is 6,50,00,000 × 1.5 Mbps ≈ 100 terabits per second, more than many countries’ entire internet traffic, all carrying the same few seconds of video. Live is harder than on-demand in three ways. Everyone wants the same moment, so nothing can be sent the night before. The crowd arrives in minutes, not over an evening. And a failure is public and remembered: the wicket everyone missed.

1. Encoding and the bitrate ladder

Everyday picture. A dairy sells the same milk in 200 ml pouches for a single person and 1-litre packs for a family. Nobody gets different milk; they get the size that suits them.

How it works. The stadium feed arrives at the platform as one very high-quality stream. Encoders (machines that compress video) turn it into several versions at once, the bitrate ladder: each rung is a picture size and a number of bits per second ({{REF:ladder}}). The data a viewer uses is simple arithmetic: megabits per second × 3,600 seconds ÷ 8 = megabytes an hour. At 1.5 Mbps, 1.5 × 3,600 ÷ 8 = 675 MB an hour; a three-and-a-half-hour match is about 2.4 GB, most of a ₹199 pack’s daily data. At 0.4 Mbps it is 180 MB an hour. Newer codecs (the compression methods, such as HEVC and AV1) squeeze the same picture into 30–50% fewer bits, but older phones can’t play them, so platforms encode in more than one.

Real example. JioCinema and then JioHotstar showed IPL in 4K on TVs while keeping low rungs for phones on weak signals; data-saver options let viewers cap quality to save their packs. PM decision: which rungs to offer and what the default is. A higher default looks better but eats viewers’ data and the company’s delivery bill; a lower default is cheap but looks poor on TVs. Many apps pick by device: low on phones, high on TVs.

2. Segments and adaptive bitrate

Everyday picture. Milk goes out in crates, not in one giant tanker to each house. And a good milkman, seeing the lift broken, brings two small packs instead of one heavy can.

How it works. Each rung is cut into segments of 2–6 seconds. The player first downloads a small list (the manifest) of all rungs and segments, then fetches one segment at a time, using standard formats called HLS and DASH. After each segment, it checks how fast it arrived and how much video is waiting in its buffer. If a 4-second segment at 1.5 Mbps arrived in 2 seconds, the network could carry about 3 Mbps, so the player steps up. If segments start arriving slower than they play, it steps down before the buffer empties. This is adaptive bitrate (ABR) ({{REF:abr}}). The buffer is the safety margin: 12 seconds of stored video gives the player 12 seconds to react when Priya’s train enters a tunnel.

Real example. Research on streaming (Conviva and university studies, 2011–13) found that a 1% increase in time spent frozen cut viewing of a live event by several minutes. That is why players prefer a blurry picture to a pause. PM decision: how cautious the player is. A cautious player (big buffer, slow to step up) rarely freezes but looks soft; an aggressive one looks sharp but freezes more. For live sport, the usual choice is caution.

3. CDNs and edge caching

Everyday picture. A dairy doesn’t send a truck from the plant to every home. Milk goes to chilling centres, then to neighbourhood booths; each booth serves a few hundred homes. If the booth runs out, it fetches more from the chilling centre, not from the plant.

How it works. A CDN (content delivery network) is thousands of servers placed near viewers, in every big city and often inside the internet providers’ own buildings. The first viewer in Nagpur to ask for a segment makes the Nagpur server fetch it from further up; every other viewer in Nagpur gets the copy it already holds. The share of requests answered from the nearby copy is the hit rate, and it decides how much reaches the source (the origin) ({{REF:cdn}}). With 6.5 crore viewers each asking for a segment every 4 seconds, about 1.6 crore requests arrive a second. At a 95% hit rate, 5% reach the next layer: 8 lakh a second. At 99%, only 1.6 lakh. Four points of hit rate, five times less load. Platforms add an origin shield, a middle layer that collects the misses so the origin sees each segment asked for only a few times.

Real example. Netflix places its own cache servers (Open Connect) inside internet providers, including Indian ones, and fills them overnight with the shows people are likely to watch the next day. Large live platforms use several CDN companies at once and move traffic between them if one struggles. PM decision: how much to spend on delivery, and where. Caches inside providers cost money and negotiation but cut delays and bills; using two CDNs costs more but removes a single point of failure on final night.

4. Live latency: why your father sees the wicket first

Everyday picture. Milk from a village reaches a city home in hours: collection, chilling, packing, transport, the booth, the doorstep. Each step is short; together they add up.

How it works. Glass-to-glass latency is the delay from the camera lens to your screen ({{REF:latency}}). Production and graphics take a few seconds; encoding a few more; the player waits for whole segments to be ready; the CDN adds a little; and the player keeps a buffer of two or three segments for safety. With 6-second segments, that adds up to about 30 seconds. Satellite TV is usually 5–10 seconds behind the stadium. That gap is why Priya’s father phones about a wicket she hasn’t seen. Low-latency streaming cuts segments into parts of about a second and shrinks the buffer, reaching 5–8 seconds, but a smaller buffer means more freezes on weak networks.

PM decision: delay against smoothness. For a match with neighbours cheering and WhatsApp buzzing, spoilers hurt; for a film, delay doesn’t matter at all. Many platforms offer lower delay on good connections and keep a bigger buffer on weak ones. Anything interactive, such as live predictions or voting, must allow for viewers being 30 seconds apart.

5. The toss-time spike

Everyday picture. Before Diwali a dairy books extra tankers and staff a week ahead. If it waited until the morning queues formed, the extra tankers would arrive after the customers had gone.

How it works. Viewers don’t arrive smoothly: millions join in the five minutes before the toss, and again when a star walks out to bat ({{REF:spike}}). Each new viewer logs in, loads the home page, perhaps pays for a plan, then starts the video, so the first wave hits login, payments and the home page, not the video. Autoscaling (adding servers automatically as load rises) takes minutes, too slow for a surge that arrives in seconds. So the platform pre-warms: it starts servers before the match for the crowd it expects, from past matches and the teams playing. It also serves the home page from caches, lets a phone that is already logged in skip login, and, if payments pile up, lets viewers start watching while the payment finishes.

Real example. Hotstar’s engineers described in public talks how they scaled ahead of big matches using forecasts, because waiting for autoscaling left them short at the toss, and how they had switches to turn off heavy features when load passed set levels. PM decision: how much spare capacity to pay for. Over-provision and you pay for idle servers on a dull match; under-provision and the final breaks in front of crores. The forecast (which teams, which stage, which time) belongs to the product team as much as to engineering.

6. Ads inside a live match

Everyday picture. A dairy that slips a local shop’s leaflet into each milk packet, a different leaflet for each street, all in the half-hour before dawn.

How it works. At the end of an over, the production team sends a marker saying “break starts now, 30 seconds”. Each viewer’s stream is then filled with ads chosen for that viewer, stitched in on the server so the ads look like the match to the phone: server-side ad insertion. The hard part is the timing. If 6.5 crore players all ask “which ads for me?” in the same second, the ad system faces 6.5 crore decisions at once. So platforms spread the questions over the seconds before the break, and prepare likely ads in advance.

PM decision: how personal to make the ads against how reliable the break must be. A fully personal choice for every viewer earns more per ad; a few broad groups (city, language, device) are far easier to serve at peak. If the ad system can’t answer in time, the fallback must be a pre-chosen ad or the match, never a black screen.

7. Graceful degradation: what to switch off first

Everyday picture. In a milk shortage, the dairy stops making cream, curd and ghee before it cuts a single family’s daily milk.

How it works. The team agrees a ladder in advance ({{REF:degrade}}): as load rises, features are switched off in order, from the least to the most important, each with a switch (a kill switch) that works in seconds. The two numbers watched live are the ones viewers feel: start time (seconds from pressing play to picture) and rebuffering ratio (share of watching time spent frozen).

PM decision: the order of the ladder is a product decision: which feature matters least to viewers and to revenue tonight? The PM writes it down before the match, because at 9:45 pm there is no time to debate it.

8. On-demand is different

Everyday picture. Bread for tomorrow can be baked and delivered to shops tonight. Only fresh milk has to move at dawn.

How it works. For films and series, nobody needs the same second at the same time, so platforms place copies in advance: overnight, when networks are quiet, caches are filled with what people will probably watch tomorrow (a new season, today’s Top 10). Viewing is lopsided: a small share of titles gets most views, so caching the most popular few per cent covers most requests. Downloads let viewers save episodes on home Wi-Fi and watch on the train without mobile data. PM decision: which titles to place where, and whether to push downloads for markets with costly data, such as mobile-first India.

9. DRM and piracy

Everyday picture. A sealed milk pack with a batch number: if adulterated milk turns up, the batch shows where it came from.

How it works. Video segments are encrypted; a player gets the key from a licence server only after the app checks the viewer’s plan and device. This is DRM (digital rights management), built into Android, iOS and TVs (Google’s Widevine, Apple’s FairPlay). DRM can’t stop someone filming a screen, so platforms add an invisible watermark, a tiny pattern unique to each account, woven into the picture. When a pirated IPL stream appears on Telegram, the watermark shows which account it came from, and that account can be cut off within minutes. PM decision: stronger protection (highest quality only on the most secure devices) versus reach (older phones and cheap TVs). Rights holders often decide it in the contract: 4K only with the strongest DRM.

In the interview · How streaming systems come up
  • “How would you handle 6 crore concurrent viewers?” Bitrate ladder and ABR, CDNs with a high hit rate, pre-warming before the toss, and a degradation ladder that never touches the match.
  • “Why is the app 30 seconds behind TV?” Segments and the safety buffer; shrinking them lowers delay but raises freezes on weak networks.
  • “Which metrics would you watch during the final?” Start time and rebuffering ratio by city and network, plus login and payment success in the first half-hour.
