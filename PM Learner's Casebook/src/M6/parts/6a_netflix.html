Netflix: always something good to watch, found in a minute

Walkthrough (illustrative persona). Meera, 29, a software tester in Bengaluru, pays ₹199 a month for Netflix Basic and watches on her TV. At 9:40 pm she opens the app. The home screen is built for her alone: a row of Korean dramas because she finished one last week, a “Top 10 in India today” row, and a poster for a thriller that shows its female lead, because Meera tends to click on those. She presses play. The picture appears in about two seconds, a little soft, and sharpens within ten. When the episode ends, the next one starts in five seconds unless she stops it. She watches three ({{REF:nf-flow}}).

Core loop: why people come back, and why they stay

Each evening follows a small loop: open, see something picked for you, press play, finish, see the next suggestion. The bigger loop is the business ({{REF:nf-loop}}). More members bring more money; more money pays for more shows in more languages; more hits bring more viewing and more data about what people like; better picks mean less browsing, so fewer people cancel, and the base grows again. The loop only works at a size where a ₹200-crore series is shared by crores of members.

Business model, with numbers

Netflix earns almost entirely from subscriptions, plus a fast-growing ads plan in about a dozen countries (not India). It expects revenue of about $51 bn in 2026, with an operating margin of 31.5%; April–June 2026 revenue was $12.6 bn, up 13%. Advertising should roughly double, to about $3 bn, in 2026. Content is the largest cost, spread over the years each show is watched. In 2025 Netflix stopped reporting member numbers, to point investors at revenue and profit instead. In India it chose low prices over ads: a ₹149 phone plan and bundles with Jio and Airtel.

Moat, in plain words. It can spend more on shows than almost anyone because the cost is shared by hundreds of millions of homes. It owns its big originals worldwide, so Squid Game exists only here. And years of viewing data make its picks better than a newcomer’s.

Strengths

  • Global scale spreads content cost
  • Recommendations that cut browsing
  • Very profitable; can outspend rivals

Weaknesses

  • Little live sport, the biggest draw in India
  • Priciest TV plans in India
  • Slow growth outside cities

What I’d build next

1. During the IPL, non-cricket viewers in a house fight for the TV → “match-length” picks: films under two hours and one-night stories, promoted at 7:30 pm on phones → hours per member in April–May; cancellations in May.

2. Growth outside big cities is slow → more Tamil, Telugu and Malayalam originals, with good subtitles and dubbing into eight languages → sign-ups outside metros; share of viewing in Indian languages.

3. People join for one show and leave → a small discount for paying for six months, offered on the day a big series ends → share of members on longer plans; churn in the month after big releases.
