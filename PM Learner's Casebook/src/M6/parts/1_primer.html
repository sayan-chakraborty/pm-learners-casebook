Streaming, music & entertainment

Ahmedabad, IPL final, 7:28 pm

Two minutes before the first ball, crores of phones open JioHotstar at once. Priya, 24, sitting on the steps outside her hostel, gets a spinning wheel for eight seconds, then a blurry picture that sharpens as the first over begins. Her father in Rajkot is watching the same match on television, about 30 seconds ahead of her. He phones to shout about a wicket she hasn’t seen yet. The next morning, lakhs of people who paid for a plan in March to watch the IPL start deciding whether to keep paying now that the cricket is over.

Try it: if you ran JioHotstar, which would worry you more, the eight seconds of buffering on final night or the people who cancel in June? Why? Think for five minutes before reading on.

Paying once for a show, earning from it many times

A streaming service pays a huge, fixed sum for its shows, matches and songs, and then tries to earn that money back from as many people as possible: through monthly plans, ads, bundles with a mobile recharge, or all three. One more viewer costs it almost nothing in content, so size wins. In India the audience is enormous but pays little, and the biggest draw, cricket, lasts two months a year. Almost every PM question in this sector comes back to three things: what to pay for content, how to get people to pay or watch ads, and how to keep them once the thing they came for is over.

Who does what, and who pays whom

Six groups share the money ({{REF:money}}). Rights owners, such as the BCCI for the IPL, film studios and record labels, own what people want to watch or hear, and sell the right to show it. The streaming app buys those rights, delivers the video and sells plans and ads. Viewers pay a monthly fee, or pay nothing and watch ads. Advertisers pay for every thousand people who see their ad. Telecom operators such as Jio and Airtel pay the app to include it in a recharge, because “free JioHotstar with this pack” sells bigger recharges. And delivery networks carry the video to your phone, charging for every gigabyte.

There are five ways to charge. SVOD (subscription video on demand) means a monthly plan with no ads: Netflix’s original model. AVOD (advertising video on demand) is free to watch and paid for by ads: YouTube. Hybrid is a cheaper plan with ads, now the default at Prime Video India. TVOD (transactional video on demand) is paying per film, like renting a DVD used to be. A bundle means someone else pays: your Jio recharge, or Amazon Prime. Games add a sixth: free to play, with a few players buying extras.

The unit economics, worked in rupees

Take one person on a ₹199-a-month plan for a year: ₹2,388 ({{REF:subyear}}). The government takes 18% GST, about ₹364. Payment fees take about ₹40. Then comes the share of content cost: shows and films are paid for in advance and spread over the years they are watched, and at Netflix this is the biggest single cost, roughly 40% of revenue. After streaming, marketing and staff, about ₹460 is left (illustrative).

That last point is the key to the whole sector. Content is a fixed cost (the same total whatever the number of customers). Suppose a service spends ₹2,000 crore a year on content. With 50 lakh subscribers, each one “costs” ₹2,000 Cr ÷ 50 lakh = ₹4,000 a year in content, far more than they pay. With 4 crore subscribers, the same content costs ₹500 each ({{REF:fixedcost}}). The business only works past a certain size, which is why streaming companies merge, bundle and spend heavily to grow.

Cricket: the most expensive show in India

In June 2022 the BCCI sold IPL rights for 2023–27 in separate packages for the first time. Viacom18 (now part of JioStar) paid ₹23,758 crore for the digital rights to 410 matches; Star (also now JioStar) paid ₹23,575 crore for television. That is ₹23,758 Cr ÷ 410 = about ₹58 crore a match, for phones alone. If 20 crore people watch a match, each viewer must bring in ₹2.90 just to cover the rights. At ₹70 per 1,000 ad views, one ad earns 7 paise, so each viewer must see about 40 ads per match ({{REF:iplmaths}}).

Three numbers about viewers are often confused ({{REF:reach}}). Reach is how many different people watched at some point: 70 crore for IPL 2026 on JioHotstar. Concurrency is how many watch at the same moment: the record is 6.5 crore. Paying subscriptions are a third number again, and many of them come bundled with a recharge. Ads are sold on reach and views; servers are built for concurrency; revenue per user comes from subscriptions. Headlines that say “82 crore viewers” are usually reach, not people watching at once.

Five turning points

1. Cheap data (2016). After Jio launched, a gigabyte cost a few rupees instead of a few hundred. Video on the phone became an all-day habit for hundreds of millions of people. 2. Covid (2020). Cinemas closed; films such as Gulabo Sitabo released straight on streaming, and many families paid for a plan for the first time. 3. Cricket goes digital first (2022–23). The IPL’s digital rights sold for almost as much as the TV rights. JioCinema then streamed IPL 2023 free to everyone, at scale, and taught the market that cricket on the phone could be paid for by ads. 4. One giant (2024–25). Reliance’s Viacom18 and Disney’s Star India merged into JioStar, and JioHotstar launched in February 2025 with almost all big cricket, Disney, HBO content and TV serials in one app. 5. Real-money games banned (2025). The Online Gaming Act banned games played for money, fantasy cricket included, from 1 October 2025. Dream11 and others turned to free games; ad spending on them vanished.

Five mechanics that aren’t obvious

1. Sport buys sign-ups; series keep them. People join for the IPL and leave when it ends ({{REF:churnyear}}). A service that can’t give them a reason to stay rents its audience for two months a year. 2. Churn and return. Many viewers now subscribe, watch one thing and cancel, then come back for the next big show. The monthly count looks steady while different people fill it. Services fight this with annual plans, bundles and a steady flow of releases.

3. Music pays per play. A video service pays for a show once. A music service pays record labels and publishers for every song played: about two-thirds of what it earns ({{REF:royalty}}). More listening means more cost, so music apps push podcasts and audiobooks, which cost less per hour.

4. A few hits, a long tail. A handful of titles take most viewing in any week; thousands of others are watched a little. The hits bring people in, the tail gives them something to do between hits, and recommendations connect the two. 5. Piracy is the free rival. A popular film appears on pirate sites within hours of release, and a match is restreamed on Telegram. Services add invisible watermarks to trace leaks and, above all, keep prices low enough that paying is easier than hunting for a pirated copy.

6. A show is judged by what each hour of viewing cost. Streaming services compare very different titles with one number: cost per hour watched (what the title cost ÷ total hours people spent watching it). A ₹100 crore big-budget series watched for 2 crore hours costs ₹100 Cr ÷ 2 Cr = ₹50 an hour. A ₹12 crore small-town comedy watched for 1.5 crore hours costs ₹8 an hour ({{REF:cph}}). The cheaper show did six times better on this measure, which is why services keep commissioning modest Indian series such as Panchayat. Two cautions: a big title may also bring in new subscribers, which hours don’t show, and a cheap title may be watched mostly by people who would have stayed anyway.

In the interview · Questions this sector loves
  • “How do you keep IPL subscribers after the season?” Learn what else they like during the season, line up the next reason to watch, offer a pause instead of a cancel.
  • “Should Netflix cut prices in India?” Say which segment, and test what they would pay first; a new cheap plan can pull existing members down to it.
  • “Views fell 20%. Why?” Split views into viewers × titles started × share finished, and find which part moved.
  • “How would you handle 6 crore people watching at once?” Prepare before the match, cache near viewers, and plan what to switch off first.
  • “Is YouTube a competitor to Netflix?” Yes, for time: both sell the same evening hours.
