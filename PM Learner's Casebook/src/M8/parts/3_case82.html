Case 8.2 · Sales reps who won’t update the CRM Root causeMediumIIM A Product Mindset 2026–27, pp. 311–312
Try it first. Think of a form your college or office makes you fill in every week, such as a timesheet or an attendance log. Do you fill it in on time? What would make you? Now structure “only 40% of reps use the CRM properly, even though it is compulsory” out loud, for five minutes.

What worked · what was missing
  • Asked what “actively use” means and split logging in from updating. The answer reframed the whole case.
  • Segmented before guessing, and got the key clue: the gap sits with reps of five years or more.
  • Cheap data before interviews: time per update, required fields, where reps give up, duplicate rate.
  • A leading and a lagging metric, with a smart reading of what it means if one moves and the other doesn’t.
  • Never named the real split. Sales ops chose the CRM and made it compulsory; reps pay the cost in typing; leadership gets the forecast. The case is about a buyer and a user who want different things.
  • Cutting to 3–4 fields is a negotiation, not a product call. Someone upstream uses each field (finance, the forecast, commission). The PM has to agree the cut with the buyer.
  • The strongest fix removes typing altogether. Emails, meetings and calls can be logged automatically; major CRMs (Salesforce, HubSpot, Zoho) already do this.
  • Tenured reps hold the biggest accounts, so their stale data costs the forecast the most. That sizes the problem and wasn’t said.

Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing ✓ log in vs update · Goal ✓ fresh pipeline data · Who ✓ segmented by tenure · Today and the trigger ✗ since when; a new CRM or new rule? · Edges ✗ mobile or desktop. RCA add-ons: exact definition ✓ · where it shows ✓ · recent changes ✗ · is the data trustworthy ✓ duplicates. The costliest skip: “since when”. If updates fell after a new mandatory field last quarter, the fix is to remove that field.

Check · “Tenured reps are generally more efficient in any tool, so it’s less likely to be pure friction” Overstated

Experienced reps usually carry more accounts and bigger deals, so they have more updates to make every week. The same 12 clicks cost them more time in total, not less. They also gain least from the CRM: they already know their deals by heart, so a screen of their own pipeline tells them nothing new. Habit and trust matter, as the candidate says, but the data the interviewer then shared (12 clicks, irrelevant fields) showed friction was real, and the candidate rightly fixed it first.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

First, what exactly is “use”: logging in, or updating deals? Second, since when: has it always been like this, or did it drop after a change, such as a new required field? Third, can I trust the 40%: is it measured from the CRM’s own logs?

Q

70% log in daily; 40% update their deals in a typical week. It has been flat since the CRM was made compulsory a year ago. The numbers come from the CRM’s logs.

Why this move: “flat since the mandate” rules out a recent bug or release, and points to how the tool fits reps’ work. Checking the source of the number takes one sentence and avoids chasing a tracking error.

A
② Segment

The gap is with reps of five years or more. Say they are 30% of reps but own 55% of the pipeline value, because they handle the biggest accounts. So the stale data sits exactly where the money is. If the company forecasts ₹15 crore a quarter and missed by ₹1.2 crore last quarter, that 8% error is mostly their deals (illustrative).

Why this move: segmenting found who, and weighting by pipeline value turns “some reps don’t update” into “the forecast is blind on its biggest deals”, which is why leadership cares.

A
③ Who gains

Now the key question: who pays the cost, and who gets the value? Sales ops chose the CRM and made it compulsory. The rep pays: 20 updates a week × 4 minutes = 80 minutes of typing. The value flows up to sales ops, the sales head and finance as reports and a forecast. Nothing comes back to the rep ({{REF:valueflow}}). A tenured rep, who knows every deal by heart, gets the least of all. So the mandate is the only thing holding usage up.

Why this move: naming the buyer–user split explains why the same tool works for new reps (they learn from it) and fails for tenured ones (it only takes from them). It points the fix at the rep’s payback, not at more rules.

A
④ Test causes

I’d check the three causes with data we already have ({{REF:tree82}}). Time-on-task: 12 clicks and about 4 minutes an update, with most drop-offs at the “competitor” and “contact role” fields. Screens opened: tenured reps almost never open the CRM’s own reports, so they get nothing back. Duplicates: 18% of accounts. Then 8 short interviews with tenured reps to hear the reasons in their words.

Why this move: logs show what reps do; interviews show why. Doing the logs first means the interviews test specific ideas instead of collecting opinions.

A
⑤ Fix

Three changes, in order. Remove the typing: log calls, emails and meetings automatically, and suggest the stage change for one tap, so an update takes 30 seconds, not 4 minutes ({{REF:clicks82}}). Give something back: follow-up reminders before a deal goes quiet, a “deal at risk” alert, and commission earned so far on the rep’s home screen. Agree the fields with the buyer: sit with sales ops and keep only the fields that feed the forecast or pay; move the rest to optional. Deduplication comes next quarter.

Why this move: each fix changes the rep’s side of the trade. Less effort and a clear payback make updating worth doing without a mandate, and agreeing fields with sales ops keeps the buyer on side.

A
⑥ Measure

Leading (early sign): share of open deals updated in the last 7 days, for tenured reps; target from 40% to 70% in a month. Lagging (the result): forecast error, from 8% to about 4% over two quarters ({{REF:lead82}}). Safety check (guardrail): each month, managers check 50 deals against what the rep says in person, so one-tap updates don’t become one-tap guesses. Also watch time reps spend in the CRM each week: it should fall.

Why this move: the leading metric shows within weeks whether reps use the fix; the lagging one shows whether it mattered to the business; the spot-check stops fast updates from being wrong ones.

How the answer hangs togetherSegmenting found the tenured reps, and weighting by pipeline showed their stale deals drive the ₹1.2 crore miss. The buyer–user split explained why they don’t update: they pay 80 minutes a week and get nothing back. So the fix cuts the cost to 10 minutes and gives them reminders and alerts, with fields agreed with sales ops. Updates show early whether it worked; forecast error shows whether it mattered; the spot-check keeps the data honest.
Framework · Buyer vs user (B2B)

1 · The situation.Sales ops bought the CRM and made it compulsory. Karan, a rep, has to feed it 80 minutes a week. His managers get a forecast; he gets nothing he didn’t already know. He does the minimum, so the forecast is wrong, so the buyer’s reason for buying fails too.

2 · An everyday example.A school uniform. The school chooses it, parents pay for it, and the child wears it every day. If the blazer is hot and itchy, the child leaves it in the bag whenever a teacher isn’t looking. The school can make it compulsory, but it can’t make it worn well. A uniform that is comfortable gets worn without anyone checking.

3 · The framework.In most B2B software, different people play different roles in one purchase. The user works in the product every day. The buyer (sometimes called the economic buyer) owns the budget and signs. Influencers shape or block the decision: IT and security, legal, finance. Often a champion, someone inside the customer who wants the product, pushes it through. Each role gives something (money, effort, approval) and wants something back. Trouble starts where value flows one way: someone gives effort and gets nothing ({{REF:valueflow}}). A mandate can force the effort for a while, but the data will be poor and the tool will be dropped at the first chance.

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. List every role around the product: reps, sales ops, sales head, finance, IT.
  2. For each, write what they give and what they get. Reps give 80 minutes a week and get nothing.
  3. Find the one-way arrows. Here: effort flows up from reps; value never comes back.
  4. Design the user’s payback: less effort (auto-capture) and something useful (reminders, alerts, commission).
  5. Agree with the buyer what they truly need, so cutting the user’s effort doesn’t break the buyer’s reports.

Common slip · designing only for whoever signs: more manager dashboards, more required fields. The buyer is pleased at the demo, and the users quietly stop feeding the tool.

4 · When it misleads.Sometimes one person is both. In a 15-person start-up the founder picks the CRM, pays for it and uses it; splitting roles adds nothing. Sometimes the users are the first buyers: Slack and Zoom spread because teams started using them before anyone in IT signed a contract, so the user’s experience was the sale. And it misleads when you stop at buyer and user and forget the blockers: a tool reps love and managers want can still die in a four-week security review.
