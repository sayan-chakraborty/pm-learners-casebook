E. Cost per task, and the quality–latency–cost triangle

1. Cost per resolved task

Everyday picture. A mechanic who charges ₹300 an attempt but fixes your scooter only every second visit costs you ₹600 a fix, plus the day you lose.

How it works. The price of one model call is the wrong number to budget with. What matters is cost per resolved task: the cost of an attempt divided by the share of attempts that succeed, plus what failures cost. Worked example ({{REF:costchain}}): an answer takes 3 model calls at about ₹2 each = ₹6 an attempt. It solves 60% of tickets, so ₹6 ÷ 0.6 = ₹10 per solved ticket. The other 40% go to a person at about ₹40 each, adding 0.4 × ₹40 = ₹16. Real cost: ₹6 + ₹16 = ₹22 a ticket, against ₹40 when people handle everything. The saving is ₹18 a ticket, not the ₹34 the ₹6 figure suggests. Raising the solve rate from 60% to 75% is often worth more than halving the price of a model call.

2. Latency: the median and the slow tail

Everyday picture. A bus that is usually on time but once a week is 40 minutes late. You remember the late day.

How it works. Latency is how long the user waits. Two numbers matter: time to first word (answers that stream in word by word feel faster) and time to the full answer. Look at the spread, not the average. The median is the time half the answers beat; p95 is the time 95% beat, so 1 in 20 is slower ({{REF:latency}}). Worked example: the copilot’s median is 1.2 seconds but its p95 is 6 seconds, mostly on long tickets with many replies, because the model has more to read. An agent handling 60 tickets a day hits the slow tail about 3 times a day. What users tolerate depends on the job: an agent will wait 3 seconds for a draft; a customer in live chat wants the first words within a second or two; an overnight report can take minutes.

3. The triangle: quality, speed, cost

Everyday picture. A wedding caterer: excellent food, fast service, low price. You can usually have two.

How it works. Bigger models and extra steps (re-ranking, checking) raise quality but add time and cost. Smaller models are fast and cheap but miss more. The PM’s job is to pick a point on the triangle for each use, not one for the whole product ({{REF:triangle}}). Worked example: A, a large model with a re-ranker, is right 92% of the time but takes 4.5 seconds and ₹3 a ticket. B, a small model, takes 0.9 seconds and ₹0.30 but is right only 81% of the time. C sends every ticket to the small model first and passes it to the large one when the small one is unsure: right 90% of the time, 1.6 seconds on average, and 0.7 × ₹0.30 + 0.3 × ₹3.00 = ₹1.11 a ticket ({{REF:routing}}).

4. The levers

Route by difficulty: easy tickets to a small model, hard ones to a large one. Cache repeated answers: if 30% of questions are the same 50 (“how do I reset my password?”), answer them from a saved reply without calling a model at all. Prompt caching: the fixed part of every prompt (instructions, tool descriptions) can be stored by the model provider and charged at a fraction of the price when reused. Send less text: 5 chunks instead of 20 is 1,500 words instead of 6,000 in every request, cheaper and often more accurate. Batch work that can wait: overnight summaries through batch services cost about half. Stop loops early with stopping rules.

Why this changes pricing. In classic SaaS, one more user cost the vendor almost nothing, so a flat price per seat was safe. With AI, every question has a cost, and heavy users can cost more than they pay. That is why vendors now charge per use or per result, or give each seat an allowance.

The PM decisions. A cost-per-resolved-task target, set before launch. The latency the job needs (draft, live chat or overnight). Which set-up to use for which task. And a guardrail on quality, so cost cutting doesn’t quietly lower the solve rate, which raises the real cost.

In the interview · Cost and latency
  • “Our AI bill doubled. What do you do?” Break it down by task: calls per task, words per call, solve rate, loops. Then route, cache and trim before cutting quality.
  • “Which model should we use?” Different ones for different jobs; show the three set-ups with quality, time and cost, and route by difficulty.
  • “Why is p95 more important than the average?” The slow 1 in 20 is what users feel and complain about, and an agent meets it several times a day.
