Try it first. Picture a sales rep in Pune who makes 15 calls a day, some on Zoom, most on her phone. What would make her trust an AI that listens to those calls and fills in the CRM? Now structure “launch this to mid-market sales teams in India” out loud, for five minutes.

What worked · what was missing
  • Asked add-on or standalone, and the objective: adoption among existing customers first, revenue next.
  • Separated user, buyer and influencer, including IT and legal because call data is sensitive.
  • A narrow pilot: one team, one workflow, three uses, with three questions it must answer.
  • Named the real risks: reps feeling watched, summaries they don’t trust, consent, clumsy CRM links.
  • Per-user pricing ignores the AI bill. Each call hour costs money to transcribe and summarise, so a rep on calls all day can cost more than they pay.
  • A free pilot with no pass mark often never converts. The buyer should agree before the pilot what result means “we pay”, and who pays.
  • India-specific gaps: many sales calls happen on mobile phones and WhatsApp, where a meeting bot can’t join; calls mix Hindi and English; recording needs consent under the DPDP Act.
  • No product-led option, even to reject: free AI note-takers spread rep by rep, and those reps are already in the customer’s teams.
  • No numbers: minutes saved per rep, the pilot’s targets, or the size of the add-on.

Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing ✓ · Goal ✓ adoption first · Who ✓ mid-market sales teams · Today and the trigger ✗ how many customers; what reps use now · Edges ✗ languages, phone vs video calls. GTM add-ons: standalone or add-on ✓ · competitors ✗ · budget ✗. The costliest skip: edges. A bot that only joins video calls misses most of an Indian field-sales rep’s day.

Check · “Avoid usage-based pricing at the start because buyers may worry about unpredictable costs” Directionally right

Buyers do dislike bills they can’t predict; a sales head has a fixed budget for the year. But a flat per-rep price has its own problem when every call hour costs the vendor money: the reps who love the product most cost the most to serve. The usual answer is a mix. Charge per rep, include a generous allowance of call hours, and charge for extra hours over it, with a cap the customer sets. Microsoft, Salesforce and most AI vendors moved to some version of this in 2025–26.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

A few facts first. How many existing customers do we have, and how many reps in a typical one? What does our core tool cost per seat? And what do reps use today for call notes: nothing, free AI note-takers, or a rival such as Gong or the AI inside Salesforce or Zoho?

Q

About 300 mid-market customers with 25 to 150 reps each. The core tool is ₹1,500 a seat a month. Some reps already use free note-takers on Zoom calls.

Why this move: the base and the price set the size of the prize, and knowing reps already use free tools tells you the need is real and the price has a ceiling.

A
② Who

Users are the reps; the buyer is the sales head or sales ops; IT and legal can block it over recordings. Two Indian facts shape the first customers. Many sales calls happen on mobile phones or WhatsApp, where a meeting bot can’t join. And calls mix Hindi and English, so the transcripts must be tested on real calls. So I’d start with customers whose reps sell over video calls, such as software, IT services and B2B edtech firms. That is where the product works on day one. Phone calls come later, through a calling app that records with consent.

Why this move: picking customers where the product already works gives the pilot a fair chance, and naming the gap now puts phone-call capture on the roadmap before it becomes a surprise.

A
③ Pilot

Ten accounts, about 40 reps each, for six weeks. Before it starts, each buyer signs off a pass mark: at least 60% of invited reps active every week by week 4; CRM fields filled after calls up from about 55% to 90%; reps saving 25 minutes a day, by their own log and time in the CRM. The buyer also names who will pay and agrees the price up front. If the pass marks are met, the order goes ahead. Target: 6 of 10 accounts convert ({{REF:pilot83}}).

Why this move: a pass mark agreed in advance turns “let’s see how it goes” into a decision, and naming the payer early stops a successful pilot from dying in a budget meeting.

A
④ Price

Start from cost. Transcribing, summarising and updating the CRM costs about ₹25 per call hour. A flat ₹1,200 a rep works for a light rep with 10 hours a month (cost ₹250), but a heavy rep with 60 hours costs ₹1,500, a ₹300 loss; break-even is 48 hours ({{REF:seatuse}}). So: ₹1,200 a rep a month including 30 call hours, then ₹40 an extra hour, with a monthly cap the admin sets. The buyer gets a predictable bill; heavy use pays for itself. All figures illustrative.

Why this move: a price that ignores what each use costs can make the best customers the least profitable. The allowance keeps the budget simple and the cap removes the fear of a surprise bill.

A
⑤ Channels

Sales-led for the launch: account managers take pilots to sales heads in our existing base, with case studies from the first six. But I’d also open a free door for individual reps: five calls a week at no charge, inside the tool they already use. When five reps at one customer use it, the account manager gets an alert and calls the sales head. That turns reps into a reason for the buyer to take the meeting.

Why this move: sales-led fits a product that needs a security review and a budget owner; the free door adds pull from users. Together they land in one team and expand across the account.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): weekly active reps with at least three calls summarised. Inputs: calls captured per rep; share of summaries sent without edits; CRM fields filled automatically. Business: pilot-to-paid and add-on revenue. If 30% of 300 customers adopt it for 60 reps each: 90 × 60 × ₹1,200 × 12 = about ₹7.8 crore a year. Safety checks (guardrails): consent recorded on every recorded call; reps’ complaints of being watched; gross margin per account over 70%.

Why this move: weekly use by reps comes first because it drives every other number. The margin guardrail keeps heavy use from eating the profit, and the consent check protects the whole launch.

How the answer hangs togetherClarifying gave the base (300 customers) and the ceiling (free note-takers). Indian calling habits chose the first customers: those on video calls. The pilot’s pass marks and a named payer turn pilots into orders. The cost of a call hour set the price, a seat with an allowance, so heavy users stay profitable. Weekly active reps is the North Star because nothing else moves without it; the margin and consent checks guard the business and its trust.
Framework · Sales-led vs product-led growth, with seat vs usage pricing

1 · The situation.The sales assistant can reach customers two ways: account managers take it to sales heads, or reps try it free and pull it into their company. And it can be priced per rep or per call hour. Both choices depend on the same facts: who feels the value first, who must approve, and what each use costs.

2 · An everyday example.Two gyms. One signs a wellness contract with a company’s HR head after three meetings, and 500 staff get access. The other gives anyone who walks in a free week, and some stay and pay. Two mobile plans: one is a flat fee per SIM with unlimited data; the other charges per GB used.

3 · The framework.Sales-led growth (SLG) means people sell: account managers find the buyer, run demos and pilots, and negotiate a contract. It suits products that are expensive, need a security or legal review, or deliver value only when a whole team uses them. Product-led growth (PLG) means the product sells itself: users sign up free, get value alone, and bring in colleagues; the company pays when a team hits the free limit. It suits products where one person feels the value on day one. Most B2B firms end up with both ({{REF:slgplg}}): free use creates pull, and sales turns that pull into a company-wide contract. The target for either is the ideal customer profile (ICP), the kind of company that gets value fastest and pays; here, mid-market firms whose reps sell over video calls. Pricing follows the same logic. Seat pricing charges per person: predictable, but blind to how much each person uses. Usage pricing charges per unit of work (a call hour, an action, a resolution): it tracks cost and value, but the bill is hard to predict. A hybrid charges per seat with an allowance and a price for extra use.

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. Ask who feels the value first. A rep feels it on her first call, which argues for a free door for reps.
  2. Ask who must approve. Recording calls needs IT, legal and a budget owner, which argues for sales to close the company deal.
  3. Work out the cost of one use: ₹25 a call hour.
  4. Compare seat revenue with cost for a light and a heavy user; find the break-even (48 hours).
  5. Pick the model: sales-led with a free door, and a seat price with an allowance of 30 hours and a cap.

Common slip · copying another product’s model. Slack’s product-led spread worked because one team gets value alone; a tool that needs the whole company’s data or a legal review can’t spread that way.

4 · When it misleads.Product-led growth can gather lakhs of free users and no contracts, if nobody in sales follows up and the free plan is good enough forever. Usage pricing can scare buyers off before they start: a sales head who can’t say what next year will cost may not sign at all. And seat pricing breaks for AI that does the work itself: if an agent resolves half the tickets, the customer needs fewer seats, and a per-seat vendor earns less for doing more. That is why support AI such as Intercom’s Fin charges per resolution.
