AI that looks things up, takes actions and plugs into other software

A language model on its own knows only what it read during training. It has never seen your company’s refund policy, can’t check a customer’s invoice and can’t change a plan. Three ideas close that gap. RAG lets the model look things up in your own documents before it answers. Agents let it take actions through tools, one step at a time. MCP is a common standard for plugging those tools in. Then come two questions every PM must answer before launch: how do we know it works (evals), and what does each answer cost and how long does it take? One running picture helps with all of them: Priya, a new support agent at a Pune software company, on her third week.

A. RAG: answering from your own documents

Why the model alone isn’t enough. Ask a general chatbot “what is our refund window for annual plans?” and it will answer confidently from patterns in its training, which never included your policy. Its knowledge also stops at a training cut-off, and it will invent a plausible answer rather than say it doesn’t know. RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) fixes this the way Priya would: look it up first, then answer from what you found ({{REF:rag}}). It is the pattern behind the support copilot, Microsoft 365 Copilot and most company chatbots.

1. Preparing the library: collect, clean, chunk

Everyday picture. Before an exam you don’t reread the textbook; you make notes, one topic per card, each card complete enough to make sense alone.

How it works. The team collects every source the copilot may use: help articles, policy PDFs, past tickets that were resolved well. It cleans them, removing website menus, duplicate copies and old versions. Then it cuts them into pieces called chunks, usually a few hundred words each, with a small overlap so no sentence is cut in half. Worked example: a 3,000-word refund policy becomes 12 chunks of about 250 words, each sharing 50 words with the next ({{REF:chunk}}). Too small, and a chunk loses its conditions (“within 14 days” without “for annual plans only”). Too big, and each chunk is about many things, so it matches every question a little and none well, and passing it to the model costs more words.

2. Embeddings: finding pieces by meaning

Everyday picture. In a good library, books on similar topics sit on the same shelf, so the librarian can walk to “refunds” even if you asked about “getting my money back”.

How it works. An embedding model turns each chunk into a long list of numbers, often about a thousand, that places it in a “meaning space”: texts about similar things get similar numbers. The question is turned into numbers the same way, and the search returns the chunks whose numbers point in the most similar direction (cosine similarity: the smaller the angle between two arrows, the closer the meaning). These numbers are stored in a vector database (a store built to find the nearest lists of numbers fast), with labels such as product, date and who may see it. Worked example: “Can I get a refund if I cancel my annual plan after 10 days?” scores 0.86 against the annual-refund chunk, 0.71 against monthly cancellations and 0.12 against password resets ({{REF:simmap}}).

3. Keywords, meaning, and a second opinion: hybrid search and re-ranking

Everyday picture. To find a book you use both the subject shelves and the exact title in the catalogue. Then you flip through the five likeliest and keep the best.

How it works. Meaning search is weak on exact strings: an error code such as “ERR-4012”, a “GSTIN”, a plan name. Keyword search finds those exactly but misses rephrasing. Hybrid search runs both and merges the results. A re-ranker, a second, slower model, then reads the top 20 candidates next to the question and reorders them, and only the best 5 go forward. Worked example: on 200 real test questions, the right article was in the top 5 for 74% with meaning search alone, 80% with hybrid, and 86% (172 of 200) after re-ranking ({{REF:hybrid}}).

4. The grounded prompt

Everyday picture. An open-book exam where the teacher says: answer from the book, and write the page number.

How it works. The app builds a prompt from the question, the top chunks with their titles and dates, and clear instructions: answer only from these passages, cite the passage for each fact, and if the passages don’t contain the answer, say so. This is a grounded prompt. Showing the sources lets the agent or customer check the answer in one click.

Without grounding
“Customer asks: can I get a refund on my annual plan after 10 days?”

Model: “Yes, you can request a full refund within 30 days.” (invented; sounds right)
Grounded
“Answer only from these passages; cite [1] or [2]; if they don’t answer it, reply ‘not found’.
[1] Refund policy §2, updated 1 Sep 2026: Annual plans: full refund within 14 days of purchase…
[2] Help article 214: Monthly plans can be cancelled any time…”

Model: “Yes. Annual plans get a full refund within 14 days of purchase, so a cancellation on day 10 qualifies [1].”

5. Freshness and permissions

Everyday picture. A library that still lends last year’s timetable causes missed trains; one that lets anyone read the staff files causes worse.

How it works. When a document changes, its chunks must be rebuilt and re-indexed; otherwise the copilot keeps quoting the old version. Worked example: the refund window changed from 7 to 14 days on 1 September, but the index refreshed weekly, so for six days the copilot said 7: about 6 × 30 refund tickets = 180 wrong answers ({{REF:fresh}}). Re-indexing whenever an article is published fixes it. Permissions matter as much: every chunk carries labels (which customer, which team, internal or public) and the search filters by them before anything reaches the model. A line in the prompt saying “don’t reveal other customers’ data” is not a lock; a model can be talked out of it.

6. Where RAG breaks

The same refund question can fail in four ways ({{REF:ragfail}}). The search can return the wrong piece; the right piece can be out of date; two sources can disagree (the policy says 14 days, an old ticket reply said 7); or the answer can be spread across pieces that weren’t all retrieved. Each has a different fix, which is why teams test retrieval and answering separately.

The PM decisions. What goes in the library, and who owns each source: if nobody owns the refund article, nobody updates it. Refresh: on publish for policies, daily for tickets. Answer or abstain: the match score under which the copilot says “not found” (say 0.6); too high and it rarely helps, too low and it guesses. Show sources by default, so people can check. Official over informal: policies outrank old ticket replies.

In the interview · RAG
  • “Our chatbot gives wrong answers. What do you check?” First whether search found the right passage (retrieval), then whether the answer stuck to it (faithfulness), then whether the source was current.
  • “Why not fine-tune the model on our documents instead?” Documents change weekly and need permissions and citations; RAG updates by re-indexing, fine-tuning needs retraining and can’t cite.
  • “What’s a key decision you’d own?” The abstain threshold and the list of sources, with an owner for each.
