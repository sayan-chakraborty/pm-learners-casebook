Connect the dots

The same few ideas ran through the cases, the products and the technology. The person who pays for business software is rarely the person who uses it, so the product has to give the user something back. And AI turns software that cost almost nothing to run into software where every use has a price, so both the design and the pricing have to start from the cost of one task.

Back to Gurugram: a sketch answer to the cold open

“Who is the real customer for a CRM, Karan or his boss?” Both, for different reasons. His boss (the buyer) pays and wants an accurate forecast. Karan (the user) decides whether that forecast is accurate, one update at a time. Today he gives 40 minutes a day and gets nothing back, so he skips. The ₹1.2 crore miss is the bill for ignoring the user. The fix is on his side: log calls and emails automatically, suggest the stage change for one tap, and give him reminders and a “deal at risk” alert. Then agree with sales ops which fields the forecast truly needs.

“If an AI tool does half an agent’s work, should the vendor still charge per agent?” Work out the cost first. If the copilot saves 15% of an agent’s time and costs about ₹2 a ticket, a seat price with an allowance works: the head of support can budget it, and heavy use is paid for beyond the allowance. If the AI resolves tickets on its own, per seat breaks: the better it works, the fewer seats the customer needs. Then charge per resolved ticket, as Intercom does, and keep the cost per resolved task (₹22 in the worked example) well under the price. That is also the answer to the finance head. The bill doubled because use doubled while the seats stayed flat. The fix is routing, caching and shorter prompts, measured against a solve-rate guardrail.
