Guesstimate 8.2 · Storage for 200 GB of free Google Drive in Singapore GuesstimateEasyIIM B Sigma PM Casebook 2026–27, p. 139
Try it first. How much of your own Google storage do you use today, out of the free 15 GB? Would you fill 200 GB if you had it? Build the storage Google needs for Singapore from that, for five minutes.
{{CASE:B139}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Asked the right questions: who counts (internet users), whether everyone uses the full quota, and uptake.
  • Didn’t assume a full quota, and added headroom for growth.
  • A short, clear equation that anyone can follow.
  • 60% average use is far too high. Free storage is used very unevenly: most people use a few GB and a few fill it. The average is nearer 8–10% ({{REF:skew82}}).
  • 80% internet use is low for Singapore, which is about 96%, and the 5.5 million population is out of date (6.1 million in June 2025).
  • Stored data isn’t disk needed. Cloud firms keep extra copies of every file in case a disk or a building fails, so the disk needed is higher than the data.
  • The 10% buffer has no reason attached. Why 10 and not 30?

Guesstimate kit check: 1 Unit ✗ data, not disk · 2 Approach ✓ · 3 Tree ✓ · 4 Numbers with reasons ✗ 60% use · 5 Check ✗ · 6 Range ✗. Card 4 cost the most: 60% use makes the answer about three times too big before copies are counted.

Stronger answer
A
1 · Unit

Disk space Google must set aside for Singapore’s users after a year or two of the offer, including the copies it keeps. That is what a capacity planner would buy.

Why this move: a free quota is a promise, not a purchase. Stating the unit as disk makes it clear that copies and headroom belong in the answer.

A
2–3 · Approach and tree

Demand side: users × average stored × copies × headroom. Singapore had 6.1 million people in June 2025, and about 96% use the internet: about 5.8 million users. With maximum uptake, all of them get the quota.

Why this move: using current, official numbers takes one weak step out of the tree before the real debate, how much each person stores.

A
4 · Numbers

Use is skewed ({{REF:skew82}}). Of 100 people, about 70 store under 5 GB (say 2 GB on average), 20 store 5–50 GB (about 20), 8 store 50–150 GB (about 90) and 2 nearly fill it (about 180). Average: 1.4 + 4 + 7.2 + 3.6 ≈ 16 GB; I’ll round up to 20 GB, because phone backups keep growing. Data stored: 5.8 million × 20 GB = 116,000 TB. Copies: storing each file safely in two places takes about 2 × the space, so 232,000 TB. Headroom: disks are bought 6–12 months ahead, so add 20%. Total: about 280,000 TB ({{REF:gtree82}}).

Why this move: the skewed-use picture replaces a single guessed percentage with groups anyone can picture, and each extra step (copies, headroom) has its own reason.

A
5 · Check

Google Photos holds about 9 trillion photos and videos for 1.5 billion users: roughly 6,000 items each. At about 1.5 MB an item, that is around 9 GB per user. A 20 GB average in a market with a far bigger free quota is in line with that.

Why this move: a real per-user average from a similar Google product tests the one number the whole answer depends on.

A
6 · Range

150,000 to 500,000 TB of disk, most likely about 280,000, which is roughly half the casebook’s answer. The swing factors are average use (10 to 40 GB) and how many copies Google keeps.

Why this move: the range shows the two corrections pulled in opposite directions, lower use and extra copies, and names what to measure in a pilot market.

How the answer hangs togetherCard 1 set the unit as disk, which brought in copies and headroom. Skewed use cut the stored data to a third of the casebook’s, while the copies doubled it back part of the way. Google’s own photos-per-user figure checked the key assumption, and the range says which two numbers a planner should measure first.
The reusable trick

For any “free allowance” question, size real use as a few groups rather than a single percentage; the average usually lands near a tenth of the allowance. Then turn data into infrastructure: × copies kept, × headroom for growth.

Real-life example · One more to try: how much storage do a year of UPI payments need?

Author’s practice question, not from a casebook. Try it with the cards before reading the sketch. Unit: disk for one year of payment records, across everyone who keeps them. Tree: UPI handled about 20 billion payments a month in 2025–26 (NPCI), so about 240 billion a year. A payment record (IDs, amounts, time, status, a few checks) is about 1 KB. So 240 billion × 1 KB = 240 TB for one copy. Copies: the payer’s bank, the payee’s bank, NPCI and the payment app each keep one (4), and each keeps it safely in about three places: 240 TB × 4 × 3 ≈ 3 PB a year. Check: that is under 0.2% of Google Photos’ yearly growth ({{REF:gtreeupi}). Money data is small; photos and video are big. What moves it: logs and fraud-check data stored with each payment, which can be several times the record itself.
