Guesstimate 8.1 · Storage needed by Google Photos GuesstimateMediumIIM B Sigma PM Casebook 2026–27, p. 137
Try it first. Open your phone’s gallery. How many photos did you take last month, and how many arrived on WhatsApp? Which of them get backed up? Build a year of Google Photos storage from that, for five minutes.
{{CASE:B137}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Good scoping questions: photos only, clicked and received, Android only.
  • Segments of users (heavy, medium, light) instead of one average.
  • Noticed that received photos are smaller than clicked ones, and said the answer covers one year only.
  • The file sizes were swapped. The 75% that are received got the 2 MB size, and the 25% that are clicked got 0.2 MB. That makes the answer about 2.4 times too big ({{REF:split81}}).
  • Five photos a week for a heavy user is low; many people take that many in a day.
  • Every Android user was counted, but not everyone backs up to Google Photos. Google publishes its monthly users: 1.5 billion.
  • No check against Google’s published photo counts, which would have caught the size of the error.

Guesstimate kit check: 1 Unit ✓ photos, one year · 2 Approach ✓ demand side · 3 Tree ✓ · 4 Numbers with reasons ✗ sizes swapped, no reasons · 5 Check ✗ · 6 Range ✗. Card 4 cost the most: one swapped number more than doubled the answer, and card 5 would have caught it.

Stronger answer
A
1 · Unit

New photos added to Google Photos in one year, counted once, as stored after Google’s compression. Videos are out, as agreed. I’ll leave extra copies for the data-centre question.

Why this move: “storage needed” could mean new data this year, everything ever stored, or disks bought. Naming one keeps the tree and the check comparing like with like.

A
2 · Approach

Demand side, starting from Google Photos users rather than all Android users. Google says 1.5 billion people use Photos each month (May 2025). Not every Android owner backs up, and some iPhone users do, so the published number is the better base.

Why this move: a published user count removes the least certain step, “what share of Android users back up”, from the tree.

A
3–4 · Tree and numbers

Clicked photos: heavy users (20%) take about 30 a week, medium (50%) 10, light (30%) 3. That is 6 + 5 + 0.9 ≈ 12 a week, or about 600 a year. Received photos: many people back up their WhatsApp folder too; say one received photo is kept for each clicked one, so 600 more. Sizes: a clicked photo about 2 MB after compression, a forwarded one about 0.2 MB. Per user: 600 × 2 MB + 600 × 0.2 MB = 1,320 MB, about 1.3 GB a year. Total: 1.5 billion × 1.3 GB ≈ 2,000 petabytes (2 exabytes) a year ({{REF:gtree81}}).

Why this move: every rate has a reason a listener can test against their own phone, and the sizes sit on the right groups: big for the camera, small for forwards.

A
5 · Check

When Google ended free unlimited storage in November 2020, it said 28 billion photos and videos were added every week: about 1.5 trillion a year. My tree gives 1.5 billion × 1,200 = 1.8 trillion photos a year, a few years later. That fits. The casebook’s 2.5 trillion photos is plausible too; its size per photo is what was off.

Why this move: a published count checks the number of photos, the part of the tree most likely to be wrong by a large factor.

A
6 · Range

1,000 to 4,000 PB a year, most likely about 2,000. The swing factors are how many received photos people back up (none to two per clicked photo) and whether photos are stored full size (about 4 MB) or compressed.

Why this move: naming the swing factors tells a storage planner what to measure next, and shows the interviewer you know which guesses matter.

How the answer hangs togetherStarting from Google’s own user count removed a shaky step. Putting 2 MB on clicked photos and 0.2 MB on forwards fixed the error that more than doubled the casebook’s answer. Google’s 2020 count of photos added each week confirmed the tree’s size, and the range names the two habits that would move it.
The reusable trick

When items come in two kinds with very different sizes, multiply each kind by its own size before adding. Then say out loud which kind is the many and which is the heavy: the many are often the light ones.
