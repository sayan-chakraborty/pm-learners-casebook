The cloud trio: AWS vs Google Cloud vs Azure

Walkthrough (illustrative persona). Meera, 31, runs engineering at a Bengaluru clothing brand that sells online. On a normal evening ten servers handle the site. For the Diwali sale at 8 pm she expects ten times the traffic. She doesn’t buy servers. She sets a rule in AWS: when the servers get busy, add more; when it’s quiet, remove them. At 8:02 pm traffic jumps, the rule adds 30 servers in about three minutes, product pages are served from read copies of the database, and orders go to the main copy. At midnight the extra servers switch off. Her bill shows 40 servers for four hours, charged by the second ({{REF:flow_cloud}}).

Core loop: why customers spend more each year

A customer starts with servers and storage, then adds a managed database, then analytics, then AI. Each added service makes the next one easier to adopt on the same cloud and harder to move away from, because data is expensive to take out (providers charge for data leaving their network) and engineers learn one provider’s tools. Big customers sign commitments: “we’ll spend $50 million over three years” in return for a discount. That locks in spending and gives the provider a backlog of future revenue to plan data centres against.

Business model, with numbers

Pay for what you use: per second of server time, per GB stored, per million AI tokens. In April–June 2026 AWS earned $42.2 billion, up 37%, with $16.6 billion of operating profit (a 39% margin). Google Cloud earned $24.8 billion, up 82%, the fastest growth of the three, driven by AI work. Microsoft doesn’t report Azure’s revenue on its own; Azure grew 43%, and the wider Microsoft Cloud, which includes Microsoft 365, earned $59.3 billion ({{REF:cloudmoney}}). AWS remains the largest by market share (about 28% against Azure’s 20% and Google’s 15%, mid-2026). All three are spending record sums on data centres for AI; Amazon raised its 2026 plan to about $220 billion.

Moat, in plain words. Scale: each provider buys chips, power and land cheaper than anyone else. Switching costs: moving years of data and rebuilding systems on another cloud takes months. Each also has a different pull. AWS has the widest range of services and the most engineers who know it. Azure sells into companies that already run Microsoft software, and hosts OpenAI’s models. Google leads on data analytics (BigQuery) and its own AI (Gemini, TPUs).

2025–26 moves. AI capacity is the race: record data-centre spending, own chips, and deals to host models from OpenAI and Anthropic. A long AWS outage in its main US region in October 2025 took down many apps worldwide and reminded customers why spreading work across regions matters.

Strengths

  • Customers stay for years
  • Very high margins at scale
  • AI demand still growing fast

Weaknesses

  • Bills that surprise customers
  • Huge spending before demand is proven
  • One region’s outage hits thousands of firms

What I’d build next

1. Start-ups in India overspend because nobody watches idle servers → a weekly “money left on the table” report in rupees, with one-click fixes → cost per active customer; small-customer retention.

2. Teams can’t tell which AI model is good enough for the price → run the customer’s own test questions on three models and show quality, speed and cost side by side → AI spend retained after the first month.

3. Banks must prove they can survive a region failing → a guided “fail over to Hyderabad” drill with a report for the RBI audit → regulated customers running in two regions.
