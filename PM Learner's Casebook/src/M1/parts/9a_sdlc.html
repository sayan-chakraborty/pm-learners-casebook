How software is built: from an idea to an app update

September 2026, PhonePe’s merchant team has two jobs on its board. The first has a date nobody can move: from 15 October, large merchants pay 0.4% on UPI payments above ₹2,000, so fee calculation, invoices and settlement reports must be live on day one. The second is open-ended: “help merchants like Hitesh keep payments on their business QR”, where nobody yet knows what will work. Same team, same engineers, and two very different ways to plan the work.

The software development life cycle (SDLC)

Every piece of software, from a button to a bank, goes through the same six stages. The SDLC is simply their names. What changes between teams is how long each stage lasts and how often they loop.

PRD (product requirements document): the one document that says what is being built, for whom, why, and how success is measured.

User story: one requirement from the user’s side: “As a merchant, I want to see the fee on each payment, so that I can check my settlement.”

Acceptance criteria: the tests a story must pass to count as done: “Fee shown to the paisa; zero on payments of ₹2,000 or less.”

MVP (minimum viable product): the smallest version that tests whether the idea works with real users.

Waterfall and Agile: two ways to walk the stages

Waterfall walks the six stages once, in order: all requirements, then all design, then all the code, then all the testing. Agile walks all six in a small loop every two weeks or so, shipping a working slice each time and learning from users before the next slice. Think of building a house (you can’t pour the foundation after the roof) against cooking for guests (you taste and adjust as you go).

Scrum and Kanban: the two common ways to run Agile

Scrum suits a team building new features on a steady rhythm. Kanban suits work that arrives unpredictably, such as payment-failure fixes or merchant support escalations. A WIP limit (work in progress) caps how many tasks can sit in a column, which stops a team from starting ten things and finishing none.

Light maths: when will it ship?

Teams size stories in story points (relative effort, not hours) and track velocity, the points finished per sprint. Say the merchant fee dashboard is 120 points and the last three sprints delivered 18, 22 and 20. At the average of 20, that is 120 ÷ 20 = 6 sprints, or 12 weeks. But velocity wobbles: at 22 it is 5.5 sprints, at 18 it is 6.7. So the honest answer is “11 to 14 weeks”, not a date. If the business needs 8 weeks, the PM’s lever is scope: cut the dashboard to 80 points (80 ÷ 20 = 4 sprints) by dropping charts and keeping the fee table.

When it breaks
  • Agile in name only: daily stand-ups, but releases once a quarter. Nothing is learned between them.
  • Points as a productivity target: tell teams to raise velocity and estimates quietly inflate, so velocity rises while output doesn’t.
  • Fixed deadlines in an open backlog: regulatory work treated as “we’ll pick it up next sprint” misses the date. Plan it backwards from the deadline.
Key idea · The PM decisions this drives
  • Which method fits which project (fixed vs open).
  • What is in each sprint, ranked in the backlog.
  • What “done” means: acceptance criteria, not “the code is written”.
  • When late, cut scope, not testing.
In the interview

“How do you work with engineers?” “I own the why and the what: the problem, the user story and the acceptance criteria. Engineers own the how. I bring them in at requirements, not after design, because they often see a cheaper way to reach the same outcome.” “The sprint is slipping. What do you do?” “Protect the date or the scope, not both. I’d rank what’s left by user impact, cut from the bottom, and keep testing intact, because in payments a bug costs more than a delay.”
