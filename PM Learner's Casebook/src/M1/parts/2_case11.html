{{CASE:B55}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Asked for the objective. The answer, “branches are overcrowded”, turned a vague “design an app” into a sharp brief: take load off branches.
  • Asked how customers bank today before proposing anything.
  • Picked one segment with a reason (regular customers, for volume) and parked vendors for later.
  • Prioritised with named criteria, proposed an MVP order, and made the top metric (waiting time at the branch) match the goal.
  • Never asked about phones, network or language. In rural India many people share a phone: 18% of India’s internet users go online on someone else’s device, and about 80% of them are rural (IAMAI–Kantar 2025).
  • Scored RICE in words (“High-Moderate”), so the ranking is still a gut call. With rough numbers the order changes ({{REF:rice}}).
  • Reinvented what exists. “Vendors collecting cash” is the business correspondent model: 17.36 lakh bank agents already do it (Jul 2026). Offline UPI exists as UPI 123Pay.
  • Metrics mixed goal and vanity. Downloads and DAU/MAU don’t show fewer branch trips, and nothing watches for fraud through agents.
Check · “Rural India faces ~30% UPI transaction failure rates” Doesn’t hold up

No public source gives a rural figure near 30%. Nationally, technical declines (failures caused by banks or systems) fell from 8–10% in UPI’s first year to under 1% by 2025, and NPCI’s target is below 0.25%. Weak signal does cause timeouts and “pending” payments, and those feel like failures. The honest version: “Poor network makes payments slow or pending more often in villages; I don’t have a number, so I’d check our own data by district.”

Check · “Offline UPI in our app, using SMS read/write permissions” Doesn’t hold up

Google Play allows the SMS read and send permissions only for the phone’s default SMS app and a short list of approved uses, so a bank app would struggle to get them. The need is real, and it is already met: UPI 123Pay lets feature-phone users pay by voice call or missed call, up to ₹10,000 a payment. The better product move is to promote 123Pay in the bank’s alerts, not to rebuild it.

The scoping kit · five questions before any answer

A good doctor doesn’t write a prescription the moment you say “headache”. They ask where it hurts, since when, and whether anything changed: new food, new medicine, less sleep. The scoping kit is your version of those questions. It takes about a minute and stops you solving the wrong problem. In this case, question 2 alone changed the brief from “an app” to “emptier branches”.

Same questions, everyday setting: planning a friend’s birthday, you’d ask what they’d enjoy (goal), who is coming (who), what’s already booked (today), and the budget and date (limits).

1What exactly is it?So you and the interviewer mean the same thing.“A mobile app for X Bank’s existing rural customers. Right?”✗ half: asked about KYC, not the product
2What’s the goal?Growth, usage, money, cost or trust lead to different answers.“Is there a particular objective behind the app?”✓ asked: the key question here
3Who is it for?Farmers, labourers and shopkeepers bank differently.“In which geographies does our bank operate?”✓ asked, then chose a segment
4What happens today?Shows the real pain and why now.“How does the consumer currently use our services?”✓ asked
5What are the limits?Phones, network, language and rules shrink the design.“What phones do they have? Which languages?”✗ skipped

For a design case, add one or two: which journey stage? · which platform first (smartphone, feature phone, both)? · any legal limits (who may handle cash)? · say the success metric at the end.

Rule of thumb: don’t ask everything. Ask 3–4, say one assumption out loud (“I’ll assume about half our rural customers have a smartphone”), and begin.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

Three quick questions. What’s the goal: fewer branch visits, more digital use, or lower cost? Who are our rural customers, and what phones do they carry? Any languages or rules I should design around?

Q

Goal: emptier branches. Farmers, labourers, small shopkeepers. About half have a smartphone, often shared. Pick the languages you like.

Why this move: “emptier branches” is now the test for every idea. A feature that doesn’t remove a trip is out, however clever.

A
② Pick user · ③ Find the pain

I’ll design for the account holder who visits most. I’ll assume 1 crore rural customers. A trip costs them a morning: 8 km each way, a two-hour queue, about ₹200 of lost wages. And many trips only ask “has my money come?”, a question the bank already knows the answer to ({{REF:journey}}).

Why this move: sorting visits by reason shows which ones an app can remove.

A
④ Ideas

One idea per reason for visiting. Money-arrived alerts: a voice message in the local language when wages or scheme money land, plus a missed-call balance check. Scheme checker: which schemes you qualify for, and the documents to bring. Branch token: book a time slot. Agent finder: the nearest bank agent for cash deposits. Every feature needs a voice or missed-call version for the half without a smartphone.

Q

Why not the offline UPI idea?

A

UPI 123Pay already works on feature phones, and Android won’t let a bank app send SMS in the background. I’d promote 123Pay inside our alerts instead of rebuilding it.

A
⑤ Prioritise

Scoring with RICE, per quarter, with rough numbers: alerts reach 40 lakh people, impact 1, confidence 80%, effort 2 person-months: 40 × 1 × 0.8 ÷ 2 = 16. The agent finder reaches more people but needs agent training and cash limits, so effort is 8 and it scores under 4. Alerts first, then the scheme checker and tokens; agents in phase 2.

Why this move: numbers, even rough ones, let the interviewer argue with your inputs instead of your instinct.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): branch visits per rural customer per month, from about 1.5 to 1.1 in six months (illustrative). Safety check (guardrail): fake “money arrived” scam messages and complaints about missed alerts. Trade-off: messages cost money: 40 lakh people × 4 alerts × ₹0.15 = ₹24 lakh a month, far less than the branch staff time saved. Next: pilot in two districts before a state-wide launch.

Framework · RICE: rank ideas with four rough numbers

1 · The situation.The candidate had three good ideas and had to pick an order. Without numbers, “High-Moderate” against “High” is a matter of taste, and the interviewer can only nod or disagree.

2 · An everyday example.A free Saturday and four jobs at home: fix the leaking tap, paint the balcony, set up the family’s new phone, clean the fan. You naturally ask: how many people does it help, how much, how sure am I it’ll work, and how long will it take?

RICE partThe questionSaturday jobsRural bank app
ReachHow many people, in a set time?Tap: all 4 at home. Phone: 1 (grandma)Alerts: 40 lakh customers a quarter
ImpactHow much for each? (3 huge, 2 high, 1 medium, 0.5 low)Tap: 1, saves water. Phone: 3, she can video-callAlerts: 1, removes a trip for some
ConfidenceHow sure are the numbers? (100%, 80%, 50%)Tap: 80%, done it before. Paint: 50%Alerts: 80%, simple tech; agents: 50%
EffortHow much work? (person-months)Tap: 1 hour. Balcony: 2 daysAlerts: 2; agent finder: 8

3 · The framework.Score = Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. A high score means a lot of benefit per unit of work. Popularised by the product team at Intercom, it works because each part is argued separately: someone can challenge your reach without rejecting the whole idea.

4 · When it misleads.RICE would tell a family to fix the squeaky door (quick, everyone hears it) before the gas leak in the kitchen (one room, slow to fix). Anything about safety, law or a promise already made goes first and is never scored. RICE also breaks when reach is counted in different units (users a day for one idea, customers a year for another), or when confidence is inflated for a favourite idea.

5 · In the interview.“Let me put rough numbers on reach, impact, confidence and effort, so we can argue about the inputs.” Show one calculation out loud, give the others as results, and say what you do first regardless of the score.
