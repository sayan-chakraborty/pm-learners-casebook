Payments & fintech

Surat, 14 October 2026, 9 pm

Hitesh Shah (an illustrative persona) sells sarees wholesale from a shop in Surat’s textile market. Retailers from across Gujarat pay him on a PhonePe QR code taped to the counter, usually ₹20,000 to ₹60,000 at a time, about ₹25 lakh a month. For ten years UPI has cost him nothing. Tonight a WhatsApp forward from his trade association says that from tomorrow, merchants like him pay a fee: 0.4% on every UPI payment above ₹2,000.

He does the maths on a calculator: ₹25 lakh × 0.4% = ₹10,000 a month, ₹1.2 lakh a year. His first idea is the one thousands of traders will have: “I’ll ask buyers to pay my personal UPI ID instead. Person-to-person transfers are still free.”

Try it: you are the PM for PhonePe’s merchant app. What changes for Hitesh on 15 October, and what would you build? Take five minutes before reading on.

How money moves in India, and who gets paid

7:40 pm, a kirana in Pune. Asha buys ₹500 of groceries, scans the QR code at the counter and types her UPI PIN. Before she has put her phone away, the shop’s soundbox says “₹500 received”. PhonePe was on her screen the whole time, yet PhonePe never held her ₹500 and earned nothing from the payment. It is still one of India’s most valuable fintech companies.

Five rails, one country

A payment rail is the shared road that money messages travel on, like a railway line many trains can use. India has five that matter to a PM. They differ in who runs them, how big a payment they suit, and above all who pays for each payment, which decides what products can be built on top.

How one UPI payment moves

Follow Asha’s ₹500. Four organisations pass messages before any money moves, and the app she looks at is only the first of them.

UPI ID (or VPA, virtual payment address) is a nickname for a bank account, such as kirana@ybl. A TPAP (third-party app provider) such as PhonePe or Google Pay builds the screens but cannot join UPI alone; it plugs in through one or more PSP banks (payment service providers). NPCI, a not-for-profit owned by banks, runs the switch. The issuer is the payer’s bank and the acquirer is the merchant’s. Because every UPI app talks to every bank, any app can pay any QR code. That single fact, called interoperability, makes switching apps free for users and explains why UPI apps fight so hard for habit.

Who pays for a “free” payment?

Every payment has costs: servers, fraud checks, customer support, bank systems. On a card, the merchant pays for all of it through the MDR (merchant discount rate: the share of each payment a merchant pays to accept it). The MDR is split along the chain, and the biggest slice, the interchange, goes to the bank that issued the card.

Since January 2020 merchants have paid zero MDR on UPI. The government paid banks a small subsidy instead (₹1,500 Cr in FY25, at 0.15% on small-merchant payments up to ₹2,000). Zero MDR is why a chaiwala takes UPI and not cards. It is also why UPI apps lose money on payments. That changes on 15 October 2026, when large merchants start paying 0.4% on UPI payments above ₹2,000.

How a free app makes money: follow one loan

If payments earn nothing, the habit must be sold. The biggest thing it sells is credit. The app becomes a lending service provider (LSP): it finds the borrower, shows the offer and handles the screens, while a bank or NBFC (a non-bank lender licensed by RBI) lends its own money and carries the risk. Here is one ₹1 lakh personal loan, followed rupee by rupee.

Two RBI rules shape every lending feature you will design. First, the loan must go straight into the borrower’s bank account, never through the app’s or a third party’s account. Second, an app may promise to cover the lender’s losses, a default loss guarantee (DLG), but only up to 5% of the loans it sourced. A DLG earns the app a bigger fee and also makes it share the pain when borrowers default.

Turning points

Demonetisation (Nov 2016) created the users. With 86% of cash by value withdrawn overnight, people who had never paid digitally had to. Paytm’s wallet grew first, because UPI apps were still clunky; UPI caught up within two years because it moved bank money directly, with no wallet to top up.

Zero MDR (Jan 2020) decided the business model. Once merchants paid nothing, no app could earn from payments, so the winners became the apps that could sell something else to their users: loans, insurance, investments, soundbox rentals.

Paytm’s bank (Jan 2024 to Apr 2026) showed the regulatory risk. RBI stopped Paytm Payments Bank taking deposits after repeated compliance failures, and cancelled its licence in April 2026. Paytm survived by moving its users’ UPI IDs to four partner banks, a lesson in why PMs in fintech sit close to compliance teams.

Check · “PhonePe must cut its share to 30% by January 2027” Overstated

The rule says an app above 30% of UPI volume (rolling three months) must stop onboarding new users. It does not force existing users off. NPCI has pushed the deadline back three times, most recently to 31 December 2026, and as of September 2026 it is not in force. Watch it anyway: a freeze on new users would hand growth to Paytm, Navi and CRED.

In the interview · Five questions this sector loves
  1. How does PhonePe make money if UPI is free? The habit is distribution for loans, insurance and bills.
  2. Design a payments product for rural users, teenagers or the elderly. Start from who holds the bank account and the phone.
  3. Payment failures went up. Why? Split by bank and by step before guessing.
  4. How would you reduce fraud without hurting good users? Name the safety check you will watch.
  5. What happens to UPI apps when MDR returns or the 30% cap bites? Who pays, who switches, who grows.

In the interview · “How does PhonePe make money?” in 60 seconds

“Almost nothing from the payment itself. UPI has been free to merchants since 2020, so the 45.9% share is a distribution asset, not a revenue line. Money comes from what the habit sells: its lending arm earned ₹945 Cr in FY26, plus insurance, broking, bill payments and soundbox rentals. Two things could change the maths: the 0.4% fee on large merchants from 15 October, if PhonePe gets a fair slice of it, and the 30% cap, which would stop it adding users. I’d watch revenue per monthly transacting user, not payment volume.”
