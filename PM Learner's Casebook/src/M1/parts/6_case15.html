Case 1.5 · Promotions in Google Pay MetricsMediumIIM A Product Mindset 2026–27, p. 294
Try it first. Before any metric, answer: why does Google want partner coupons inside Google Pay at all? Then name the one number you would report to its head.
{{CASE:A294}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Confirmed the feature in plain words before measuring it: scratch cards, cashback, partner coupons. A model “confirm the thing”.
  • Named both sides: users and partner brands.
  • Wrote the metric as a formula, which is a driver tree in words.
  • Gave partners their own formula and metrics, including churn.
  • Never asked why Google runs the promotions: more payments, partner revenue, or both? Without that there is no North Star, just 15 metrics of equal weight.
  • Never asked whether the coupons cause anything. Would the user have bought from Lenskart anyway? Only a holdout group answers that.
  • No cost or safety checks: coupon farming (₹1 payments to friends to win cards), “not interested” taps, trust.
  • The formula starts one level too low: “number of payments” is really payers × payments per payer, two levers owned by different teams.
Check · “Successful uses = payments × % tie-in scratch cards × % used” Directionally right

It is a proper driver tree: each factor can be owned and moved by a team. Two caveats. The factors aren’t independent (people who pay more also redeem more coupons), so improving one can shift another. And the output is a count of redemptions, which says nothing about whether each redemption was extra business for the partner.

Stronger answer
A
① Why?

Why does Google Pay carry partner coupons? My guess: partners fund the rewards, so Google gets rewards that keep people paying on Google Pay without paying for them itself, and partners get customers. Is the priority more payments on Google Pay, or partner revenue?

Q

Both, but more payments on Google Pay comes first.

Why this move: the answer decides the North Star. Without it, every metric looks equally important.

A
② Who gains · ③ North Star

Users want to be rewarded for paying here. Partners want new customers cheaper than ads. Google wants Google Pay to be the default way to pay. So my Goal (North Star) is monthly payers who make 10 or more payments, a habit measure. Promotions are one input to it, alongside payment success and new users.

A
④ Driver tree

Illustrative numbers: 10 crore monthly payers × 25 payments = 250 crore payments. 20% earn a scratch card = 50 crore cards. 30% of cards are partner coupons = 15 crore coupons. 2% are redeemed = 30 lakh redemptions a month. The weak link is redemption: 98% of coupons go unused, so I’d fix relevance before signing more partners ({{REF:tree}}).

Why this move: multiplying the chain out shows which factor is weakest, and that factor becomes the team’s focus.

A
⑤ Prove cause

Do coupons cause anything? Keep 5% of users on no partner coupons for a quarter. If they make 24.5 payments a month and everyone else makes 25, coupons add 2% more payments. For partners: if 1.2% of users shown a Lenskart coupon buy, against 1.0% of similar users not shown one, only 1 in 6 redemptions is new business. That is what Lenskart should pay for ({{REF:holdout}}).

A
⑥ Guardrails

Safety check (guardrail): coupon farming (very small payments between the same few people), “not interested” taps, and support complaints. Trade-off: better targeting means fewer coupons for some users, which can feel like a cut in rewards. Next: report incremental purchases per partner every month, not raw redemptions.

Framework · North Star, input metrics and the driver tree

1 · The situation.The candidate listed 15 metrics. A team can’t steer by 15 numbers; it needs one that captures the value it creates, and a map of the levers that move it.

2 · An everyday example.A cricket team’s goal is matches won. The captain can’t order “win more”, but can work on the things that add up to it: runs per over, wickets, catches. And runs themselves split further: balls faced × strike rate.

North StarMatches wonMonthly payers making 10+ payments
Input metricsRuns per over, wickets, catchesPayments per payer, coupon redemptions, payment success
Driver treeRuns = balls faced × strike rateRedemptions = payments × card rate × partner share × redemption rate
Weak linkDropped catches98% of coupons unused

3 · The framework.A North Star metric is the one number that best captures the value users get, and that predicts the business’s future. Input metrics are the few numbers teams can move directly that feed it. A driver tree writes the link as arithmetic, a multiplication or a sum, so you can see which branch is weakest and who owns it ({{REF:tree}}).

4 · When it misleads.Choose a North Star that can be gamed and it will be. If it were “number of payments”, users would split one ₹500 payment into five of ₹100 to win more scratch cards, and the number would rise while nothing real improved. Driver trees mislead when the branches affect each other: showing more coupons (partner share ↑) can lower the redemption rate, because people ignore a cluttered rewards page.

5 · In the interview.“I’ll pick one number that shows users are getting value, then break it into the three or four levers that drive it, and find the weakest one.” Say the North Star in a sentence before listing any other metric.

Framework · Holdouts and incrementality: did we cause it?

1 · The situation.Lenskart sees 30,000 coupon redemptions and is delighted. But many of those buyers were already planning to buy glasses. Lenskart should pay only for the purchases the coupon caused.

2 · An everyday example.A coaching centre advertises that its students average 90% in the board exam. But the students who join coaching are often the most motivated ones anyway. The fair test: compare them with similar students who didn’t join.

PartCoaching centrePartner coupon
Treatment groupStudents who joinedUsers shown the Lenskart coupon
Holdout (control)Similar students who didn’t joinSimilar users, picked at random, not shown it
Raw result90% average1.2% of users bought
Incremental effect90% − 84% = 6 points1.2% − 1.0% = 0.2 points: 1 in 6 buyers

3 · The framework.A holdout is a random slice of users kept away from a feature so they show what would have happened without it. Incrementality is the difference between the treated group and the holdout: the effect the feature caused. Because the slice is random, the two groups are alike in everything except the feature.

4 · When it misleads.Holdouts cost something: those users miss rewards, and the business gives up whatever the feature earns on them. Too small a holdout gives noise: 5% of 10 crore users is 50 lakh people and plenty; 5% of a 2,000-user pilot is 100 people and tells you nothing. And holdouts run for too short a time miss slow effects, such as users getting tired of coupons after two months.

5 · In the interview.“Redemptions tell us coupons were used, not that they caused anything. I’d keep a random 5% on no partner coupons and compare purchases and payments against them.”
