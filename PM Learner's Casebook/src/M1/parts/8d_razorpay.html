Razorpay: the plumbing behind India’s online checkouts

Walkthrough (illustrative personas). Neha sells skincare from her own website. Arjun, a customer in Jaipur, buys a ₹899 serum on his phone. The checkout sheet, the jump to his UPI app and the “order placed” page are all Razorpay, though Arjun never sees the name. Neha sees it every evening, on the dashboard where her day’s payments, failures and settlements live. Razorpay’s customer is Neha; Arjun’s experience is its product.

Business model in one line. A fee on each payment (2% on the standard plan, much less for large merchants), plus banking, payroll and lending products that earn more per merchant. FY25 revenue was ₹3,783 Cr (+65%), with a net loss of ₹1,209 Cr, mostly stock-based pay and a one-off tax on moving its holding company back to India. Moat, in plain words: being wired into thousands of merchants’ systems (checkout code, webhooks, reconciliation, payroll). Leaving means rebuilding that wiring. 2025–26 moves: the move back to India (May 2025); a confidential IPO filing (12 Jun 2026); agentic-payments launches with NPCI, OpenAI and Anthropic.

Strengths

  • Developer-first products that other fintechs copy
  • A ladder of products with rising switching costs
  • Early in AI-agent payments

Weaknesses

  • Thin gateway margins; zero-MDR UPI and rivals (PayU, Cashfree, Juspay) squeeze price
  • Net loss in FY25; IPO timing depends on showing profit
  • Every product rests on bank partners and RBI licences

What I’d build next

1. Neha loses orders every time a card payment fails → when a card fails, offer UPI in the same sheet with the amount pre-filled → orders recovered per 100 failed payments.

2. Small D2C brands run short of cash before a sale season → advances repaid as a fixed share of daily settlements, priced on their Razorpay history → advances taken and loss rate.

3. Merchants fear AI agents placing wrong or duplicate orders → per-agent spending caps and an “agent orders” view on the dashboard → disputes per 1,000 agent payments.
