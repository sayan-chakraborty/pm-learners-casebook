System design I: how the internet and APIs work

Meena opens PhonePe on 4G in Indore and taps “Check balance”. Two seconds later she sees ₹18,240. In between, her phone found PhonePe’s servers among billions of machines, set up a secret channel, proved who she is, and PhonePe asked her bank, through NPCI, for a number. A PM who can walk through those two seconds can hold a real conversation about why the app is slow, why a payment is “pending”, and what an engineer means by “the bank’s API is timing out”.

What happens when you open a screen

The app on the phone is the client: it asks. Computers in a data centre are the servers: they answer. Every screen that shows live data is a conversation between the two, in a fixed order.

IP address: a server’s numeric address on the internet, like 104.18.2.35.

DNS (domain name system): the internet’s phone book; it turns api.phonepe.com into an IP address. Answers are cached, so the lookup is usually skipped.

HTTPS: the web’s request-and-reply format (HTTP), sent inside an encrypted tunnel (TLS). The padlock in a browser means TLS is on.

Load balancer: a traffic police officer that sends each request to a healthy server, so one crash doesn’t take the app down.

Token: a temporary pass the app got at login; every request carries it, so the server knows it is Meena without asking for her password again.

Latency: waiting time. A round trip on 4G is often 50–100 ms, so steps that each need a round trip add up.

An API call, opened up

An API (application programming interface) is a menu of requests one system accepts from another, with a fixed format for each. REST is the most common style: each thing is a web address (/orders, /payments), and a verb says what to do with it: GET to read, POST to create. Here is the real shape of the call Neha’s website makes to Razorpay when Arjun taps “Pay ₹899”.

Webhooks vs polling: how a system hears that something happened

After Arjun pays, Neha’s server needs to know. It can poll, asking Razorpay every few seconds “paid yet?”, like a child asking “are we there yet?”. Or Razorpay can send a webhook, a message to an address Neha registered in advance: “payment.captured, order_Q7xK, ₹899”. The first is simple and wasteful; the second is efficient but needs care.

When it breaks
  • A slow partner makes you slow. If a bank’s API takes 8 s, your screen takes 8 s. Timeouts and clear “pending” states matter more than faster code.
  • Retries double-charge unless each request carries a unique ID that the server remembers (idempotency: a repeated request has the effect of one).
  • Unsigned webhooks let a fraudster tell a shop “paid” when nothing was paid.
In the interview

“Our app feels slow. What would you check?” “Where the time goes, step by step: finding the server, the connection, our servers, then partners such as banks. I’d look at p95, not the average, split by partner and network type. Usually one slow partner or a chatty screen making ten calls is the culprit.”

Walkthrough: 2 seconds inside a UPI payment

Back at the Pune kirana. Between Asha’s thumb on “Pay” and the soundbox speaking, about ten messages cross five organisations, all using the API ideas above. Knowing that path lets a PM answer the question every payments team hears weekly: “The money left my account but the shop didn’t get it. Where is it?”

ReqPay: UPI’s API request for “pay this”; every UPI app, bank and NPCI speak the same message format.

Switch: a router for payment messages. NPCI’s switch sees every UPI payment and holds none of the money.

Timeout: how long a system waits before giving up. UPI allows 15 s for pay requests and 10 s for status checks.

Idempotency: sending the same request twice has the effect of sending it once. Each UPI payment carries a unique ID, so a retry can’t debit twice.

Pending (“deemed”): debit done, credit not confirmed. A status check or a reversal settles it later.

p95 latency: the time within which 95% of requests finish.

Why the average lies

Take 100 payments: 90 finish in 1.2 s, 8 take 3 s and 2 hang until the 15 s timeout. The mean is (90×1.2 + 8×3 + 2×15) ÷ 100 = 1.62 s, which looks healthy. But the 95th payment in line (p95) takes 3 s and the 99th (p99) takes 15 s. Users feel their own payment, not the average: the unlucky two stand at the counter staring at a spinner. At 791 mn payments a day, 2 in 100 would be 1.6 crore stuck payments daily (illustrative; real UPI failure rates are lower).

Success rates multiply

A payment succeeds only if every hop succeeds, so the hop success rates multiply. Five hops at 99.9% each give 0.9995 ≈ 99.5%: one payment in 200 fails, although no single hop looks bad. Let one issuer bank slip to 99.0% and the chain drops to 0.9994 × 0.99 ≈ 98.6%, or one in 72. That is why payments teams track success rate per bank, and why an app’s success rate can fall with no change to the app.

When it breaks · “Money gone, shop not paid”

If step ⑤ debits Asha but the reply to ⑦ or ⑧ is lost, the payment is pending. The app must not say “failed” (Asha pays again and is out ₹1,000) or “success” (the kirana hands over goods unpaid). RBI’s rule: a failed UPI payment that debited the customer must be reversed by T+1, or the bank pays ₹100 a day.

Key idea · The PM decisions this drives
  • What the pending screen says, and how often it re-checks status.
  • Retry rules: re-check status freely, never resend a payment with a new ID.
  • Routing: PhonePe spreads users across PSP banks (@ybl, @ibl, @axl), so a failing bank can be routed around.
  • Dashboards: success rate and p95 per bank, not one average.
In the interview

“UPI failures are up on our app. Why?” Split by hop before you guess: one issuer bank, one PSP bank, or all of them? Failures clustered on one bank point outside your app. Failures everywhere point at your app, or at NPCI.
