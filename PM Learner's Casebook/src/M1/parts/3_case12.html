Try it first. Before reading on, answer one question: who legally borrows the money in a “credit for teenagers” product in India?
{{CASE:B38}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Spotted the legal problem in the first answer: teenagers can’t take credit alone, so there must be a guardian account.
  • Split teens by age with a reason (16–18 spend on their own) and picked one group.
  • Gave the guardian real controls: limits, approvals, blocked categories.
  • Named honest trade-offs (control vs freedom, default risk, two groups to convince) and put default rate in the metrics.
  • Never asked who lends, or why. A bank wanting future customers and a fintech wanting interest income build very different products.
  • Didn’t follow the law through. A contract with a minor is void in India (Indian Contract Act, 1872, s. 11), so the teenager can’t be the borrower. The credit has to be the parent’s.
  • Listed features without the job they do. A transport pass and pocket money aren’t credit; why does a 17-year-old need to borrow at all?
  • No North Star. Four metric families, none marked as the one that decides success.
Check · “Introduce a Teen Credit Score” Overstated

Credit bureaus such as CIBIL score people with loans or cards in their own name, which minors can’t have, so there is no official score to “improve”. An in-app score is still a good idea if it is called what it is: a habit meter that rewards staying within the limit and repaying the parent on time. It becomes useful at 18, when the bank can use that history to offer a first card.

Real-life example · What Indian teens can use today

Prepaid teen cards (FamApp, formerly FamPay; Junio) hold money loaded by parents. In June 2022 RBI barred loading such prepaid cards from credit lines, which ended “card-like credit” products built this way. Two newer routes: since July 2025, banks may let children aged 10+ run their own savings accounts within bank-set limits; and UPI Circle (2024) lets a parent give a child UPI spending of up to ₹15,000 a month from the parent’s account. Google Pay (“Pocket Money”, Dec 2025) and Paytm built teen features on it.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

Two questions first. Who is lending, and what do they want from this? And since a minor can’t sign a loan in India, can I assume the parent is the borrower and the teen spends within limits the parent sets?

Q

It’s a bank. It wants to win customers before they turn 18. Yes, assume the parent borrows.

Why this move: the law and the lender reshape the brief. “Credit for teens” becomes “a parent’s money the teen can use, and a way to learn credit before 18”.

A
② Two users · ③ Their jobs

The 17-year-old’s job: “Let me handle my own money without asking every time.” The parent’s job: “Let my child be independent without me worrying about overspending or scams.” The parent can veto everything, so I’ll win the parent’s trust first ({{REF:twosided}}).

A
③ What holds them back

Today they use cash pocket money. What pushes them away from it: asking is awkward, and cash gets lost. What pulls them in: paying by UPI like friends. What holds them back: parents fear overspending and scams, teens fear being watched. My features must shrink those fears, more than they add shine ({{REF:forces}}).

Why this move: in a two-person product, fear is usually a bigger barrier than missing features.

A
④ Features

Allowance on UPI Circle: the parent’s account, a ₹3,000 monthly limit, the teen pays by scanning any QR. “Borrow ahead”: when the allowance runs out, the teen can spend up to ₹500 more, taken from next month’s allowance, once the parent approves in one tap. That is practice credit: a limit, a due date, a repayment, and no loan to a minor. Weekly summary for both, framed as progress, not surveillance.

A
⑤ Risks

Scammers target teens with “free game credits” links. So payments to a new payee above ₹1,000 need the parent’s approval, and the parent can pause spending with one switch.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): active parent–teen pairs, meaning the teen paid at least once that week. Illustrative sizing: 20 lakh of the bank’s customer families have a 16–18-year-old; 10% sign up in year one = 2 lakh pairs, spending ₹60 Cr a month through the bank. Safety check (guardrail): scam losses and the share of teens who hit the limit every month, a sign of money stress. Trade-off: more parent control means less teen use; default to alerts, not approvals. Next: at 18, offer a first credit card built on the allowance history.

Framework · Two-sided users and personas

1 · The situation.The teen uses the product, but the parent pays, sets limits and can delete the app. Designing for the teen alone produces features the parent will never switch on.

2 · An everyday example.A school canteen has two users: children who eat and parents who pay. Children want samosas; parents want vegetables and a monthly bill with no surprises. Successful canteens offer a prepaid card and let parents block fried food.

QuestionSchool canteenTeen payments
Who uses it?The child, at lunchThe teen, at shops and online
Who pays or decides?The parent, monthlyThe parent, who funds and can pause it
What each wantsTasty food · healthy food, no surprise billsFreedom · safety and visibility
Who can veto?The parentThe parent

3 · The framework.List every party, what each one wants, and what flows between them. Then write one persona per important party: a short, specific portrait of a real kind of user (“Riya, 17, Class 12 in Pune, spends ₹2,500 a month on food, travel and books; her father checks every SMS alert”). Personas stop you designing for “users” in general.

4 · When it misleads.Personas become fiction when they are invented at a desk: “Riya loves fintech and budgeting” describes nobody. Base each one on something observed (ten parent interviews, the bank’s own data). And two-sided thinking can spiral: five parties with five personas each fills 20 minutes and designs nothing. Two or three parties are usually enough.

5 · In the interview.“There are two users here with different goals, and one of them can veto. Let me take the parent first.” Then give each a one-line persona and move on.

Framework · Jobs to be done, and the four forces

1 · The situation.The candidate listed features (transport pass, gamification). None of them explains why a family would switch from cash pocket money, which works fine today.

2 · An everyday example.Nobody wants a drill; they want a hole in the wall to hang a photo. And even when a better drill exists, people keep the old one: the new one is expensive (anxiety) and the old one is in the cupboard (habit).

3 · The framework.A job to be done (JTBD) is the progress a person wants in a situation: “When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].” For the teen: “When I’m out with friends, I want to pay my share myself, so I don’t have to call Dad.” Whether they switch depends on four forces: two pushing towards the new product and two holding them back. Switching happens only when push plus pull beats anxiety plus habit.

ForceBuying a new drillTeen payments
PushThe old drill can’t go through concreteAwkward asks, lost cash
PullThe new one is cordlessPaying like friends, feeling grown-up
Anxiety“Will I use it enough for ₹6,000?”Scams, overspending, surveillance
HabitThe old drill is right there“Dad just pays”

4 · When it misleads.Jobs written too broadly become useless: “help teens manage money” fits every fintech app ever built. Keep the situation specific (“out with friends, bill arrives”). And the four forces explain switching, not staying: a family that tried the app and left after a month has a retention problem, which needs its own diagnosis.

5 · In the interview.“The teen’s job is to pay their share without calling a parent; the parent’s job is to allow that without worry. The parent’s anxiety is the biggest force against us, so my first features reduce it.”
