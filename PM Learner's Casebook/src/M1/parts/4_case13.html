Try it first. Pick the app you use most. In 5 minutes, name one group of users who might leave it, and the single moment that would make them leave.
{{CASE:B116}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Described the product and its rivals first, so both sides were talking about the same PhonePe.
  • Asked for the goal, and when the interviewer turned it back, chose retention with a reason: payments have little loyalty.
  • Gave each persona a pain point and dropped one group openly, with a reason.
  • Aimed a bold idea at the biggest pain (failed payments at a counter) and gave it its own metrics.
  • Two numbers don’t hold: the 66% share and “nearly 100% success”. And “PhonePe has a higher success rate than GPay” can’t be checked: NPCI publishes success rates by bank, not by app.
  • Never ranked the three ideas. Cashback is the most expensive way to buy loyalty, and rivals copy it within a week.
  • Defined retention loosely. Retained over which window: next week, next month? Measured on payers or app openers?
  • No safety check. A “fail-proof” payment that tells the receiver “paid” early invites fraud; nothing in the metrics would catch it.
Check · “66% of Indians fall under this bracket” (students and young professionals) Overstated

About 68% of Indians are aged 15–64, the whole working-age band (UN World Population Prospects 2024). The candidate’s groups, roughly 15–40, are closer to 4 in 10 Indians. Better still, size the market you care about: UPI users, not the population.

Check · “A suspense wallet gives nearly 100% transaction success” Doesn’t hold up

Three problems. (1) Most failures happen at the payer’s bank (the issuer): if Asha’s bank is down, her money can’t leave her account, and a middle wallet doesn’t change that. (2) Telling the shop “paid” before money arrives means PhonePe is lending against a pending payment, and fake “paid” screens are already a common scam. (3) Holding customers’ money in a wallet needs an RBI licence as a payment aggregator or prepaid-instrument issuer. The pain behind the idea is real, and the Stronger answer keeps it.

Stronger answer
A
C · Comprehend

PhonePe starts 45.9% of UPI payments, and earns from what those payers buy next: loans, insurance, bills. Switching is free, because any UPI app scans any QR. So I’d pick one goal: keep monthly payers paying next month. Is that fair?

Q

Retention is fine. Go ahead.

A
I · Identify · R · Report

Who leaves? Probably students and first-jobbers: they chase cashback, carry no cash, and pay small amounts many times a day. Their need: “When I pay at a counter, it must go through, or I must be able to show it will.”

Why this move: one sharp need beats four broad personas. It tells you what to build.

A
C · Cut

Of their pains (failed payments, weak offers, splitting bills), failures hurt most. One failed payment in front of a shopkeeper is remembered longer than ten good ones.

A
L · List

Three ideas. Smart switch: if the user’s bank is failing right now, suggest their second linked bank or UPI Lite before they tap Pay. Pending proof: a clear pending screen with “money back by tomorrow if not credited”, and the shop’s soundbox says “payment pending”, not silence. Split and remind: split a bill and auto-remind friends.

A
E · Evaluate

A successful payment is a basic: nobody thanks you when it works, and everyone leaves when it fails ({{REF:kano}}). Smart switch protects that basic and is cheap, since PhonePe already tracks each bank’s health. Size it, illustratively: if 1% of PhonePe’s 11.25 bn monthly payments fail at the user’s bank, and a third are saved, that’s 3.75 crore payments a month. Pending proof comes next; splitting is a nice-to-have.

Why this move: ranking by “basic vs nice-to-have” plus a rough size is what turns a list into a decision.

A
S · Summarise

Bet: build Smart switch first. Goal (North Star): share of this month’s payers who pay again next month. Safety check (guardrail): double debits and complaints about unwanted prompts. Trade-off: prompting a second bank can feel like nagging, so show it only when the user’s bank is failing. Next: test on 5% of users against a control group.

Framework · CIRCLES: a route map for “improve X”

1 · The situation.“How would you improve your favourite app?” is so open that candidates either ramble or jump to a feature in the first minute. The interviewer wants to see how you get from a product to one well-chosen bet.

2 · An everyday example.Your hostel asks you to improve the college canteen. You’d first ask what it is for (cheap, fast lunches), who eats there (hostel students, day scholars, staff), what annoys them, which annoyance matters most, some fixes, which fix is worth it, and then you’d propose one.

3 · The framework.CIRCLES is seven moves ({{REF:circles}}): Comprehend the product and goal, Identify the customer, Report their needs, Cut to one, List solutions, Evaluate trade-offs, Summarise. Its value is the order: understanding before ideas, and a decision at the end.

4 · When it misleads.Recited mechanically, CIRCLES eats the clock: a candidate describes the product, lists four personas with ages, prioritises two, and the interviewer says “we have five minutes left” before the first idea. Spend about two minutes on C-I-R together, and most of your time on L and E.

5 · In the interview.Don’t name it. Say: “I’ll pick a goal and a user, find their biggest pain, then compare a few fixes.” The structure shows without the acronym.

Framework · Kano: basics, performance, delighters

1 · The situation.The candidate offered cashback, reminders and a fail-proof payment as if they were the same kind of thing. They aren’t: one fixes anger, one adds value step by step, one surprises.

2 · An everyday example.A hotel room. Hot water is a basic: no one praises it, everyone complains without it. Wi-Fi speed is performance: faster is steadily better. A handwritten welcome note is a delighter: nobody expects it, and it makes the stay memorable. Next year, if every hotel leaves a note, it stops delighting.

3 · The framework.Sort each feature by how satisfaction responds when you do it better. Fix basics first, compete on performance, and use delighters sparingly, because they fade into basics as rivals copy them.

4 · When it misleads.The same feature sits in different places for different users: UPI Lite (no PIN for small payments) delights a student buying chai and worries a 60-year-old who wants a PIN for everything. And the labels are guesses unless you ask users; Kano’s original method is a short survey pairing “how would you feel if it had this?” with “if it didn’t?”.

5 · In the interview.“Payment success is a basic here. Nobody will stay for rewards if payments fail, so I’d protect the basic first and treat cashback as a delighter we can’t afford to depend on.”
