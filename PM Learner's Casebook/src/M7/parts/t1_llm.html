Large language models and prompting

An assistant that turns a patient’s folder into a one-page brief for the doctor, HealthifyMe’s AI coach and PhysicsWallah’s night-time doubt answers all run on a large language model (LLM): a program trained on a huge amount of text to predict what comes next. A PM building on one needs to know why it costs what it costs, why it sometimes makes things up, what its settings do and how to instruct it.

One new intern, eight ideas

Think of an LLM as an extremely well-read new intern who has read almost every public book and website, but has never worked at your clinic, doesn’t know today’s date and badly wants to give an answer.

1. Tokens: the unit of cost

Everyday picture. An SMS used to be priced per 160 characters, so a long message cost two or three. Language models are priced in the same spirit, per token: a word or a piece of a word.

How it works. Before the model reads anything, a tokeniser cuts the text into pieces from a fixed list. Common English words are one token each. Words in scripts the list was not built for get chopped into many small pieces ({{REF:tokens}}). The same advice in Hindi can take two or three times as many tokens on an older tokeniser. Newer tokenisers built with Indian scripts in mind, such as those from Indian labs like Sarvam AI, close much of the gap.

Real example: what the clinic brief costs. Each brief reads about 6,000 tokens of typed-up reports and writes about 400. At illustrative prices that is about ₹2 a patient, or ₹2,500 a month for a busy clinic, more than the ₹2,000 subscription ({{REF:costchain}}). Sending only new reports and using a smaller model for the typing-up cuts it to about ₹750.

PM decision. Every AI feature has a cost per use that never falls to zero, unlike most software. Price it per task, check the cost in each language you support, and ask engineers what the answer costs at 10 times today’s users.

2. Next-word prediction: why it sounds so fluent

Everyday picture. Your phone keyboard suggests “day” after “Happy birth”. It has seen that pattern often.

How it works. An LLM does the same, but with far more text behind it and the whole conversation in view. For every possible next token it calculates a chance ({{REF:nextword}}), picks one, adds it to the text and repeats. A 300-word answer is several hundred of these steps. Nothing in this loop looks anything up; it produces what is likely, which is usually, but not always, what is true.

PM decision. Fluency is not accuracy. Any fact that matters (a lab value, a dose, a fee) must come from a source you control, not from the model’s memory.

3. Attention: how it keeps track of meaning

Everyday picture. “The tablet didn’t fit in the box because it was too big.” You know “it” is the tablet. Change “big” to “small” and “it” becomes the box. You re-read the sentence without noticing.

How it works. The transformer, the design behind almost every modern LLM (published by Google researchers in 2017), lets each word look at every other word and decide how much each matters, which is called attention ({{REF:attention}}). This is what lets a model connect a patient’s allergy on page one with a medicine on page five. It is also the expensive part: the longer the text, the more pairs of words to compare.

PM decision. Longer inputs cost more and answer more slowly. Send the relevant pages, not the whole folder.

4. How a model is made

Everyday picture. A doctor’s training: years of reading, then internship on real cases, then seniors correcting their notes ({{REF:stages}}).

How it works. Pre-training: the model reads a vast amount of text and learns to predict the next token; this takes months on thousands of chips. Instruction tuning: it is trained on examples of requests and good answers, so it follows instructions. Feedback from people: people compare answers, and the model is rewarded for the helpful, honest and safe ones. What comes out has a training cut-off: it knows nothing after that date, and nothing about your clinic or your users unless you send it.

PM decision. Most teams should buy a model and shape it with instructions and their own data, not build one. Anything that changes (prices, rules, a patient’s reports) must be sent at the moment of the request.

5. Temperature: steady or creative

Everyday picture. Ask a friend for a greeting for a card. On a serious day they write the same safe line every time; in a playful mood they try something new, sometimes brilliant, sometimes odd.

How it works. Temperature changes how the model picks from its list of likely next tokens. At 0 it almost always takes the top one, so the same input gives nearly the same output. Higher values give less likely tokens a better chance: more variety, and more drift ({{REF:tempdial}}). A related setting, top-p, limits the choice to the most likely tokens that together make up, say, 90% of the chance.

PM decision. Set it per feature: near 0 for the clinic brief and anything tested against a fixed answer; moderate for coaching messages people see every day.

6. The context window: what’s on the desk

Everyday picture. The intern can only use the papers on the desk right now. Whatever is in the filing cabinet might as well not exist.

How it works. The context window is the maximum amount of text, in tokens, that the model can take in one request: instructions, documents, conversation so far and its answer. In 2026, large models take from about a lakh to over ten lakh tokens. The model remembers nothing between requests; “memory” in a chat app is the app re-sending earlier messages or a saved summary. And a bigger desk is not a cleaner one: researchers found that models find facts at the start and end of a long input more reliably than in the middle ({{REF:desk}}).

PM decision. Decide what the model sees: which reports, how much history, in what order. For a coach, a short saved summary of past weeks is cheaper and more reliable than re-sending every chat.

7. Hallucination: confident and wrong

Everyday picture. Ask a stranger for directions and some will point confidently the wrong way rather than admit they don’t know.

How it works. A hallucination is a fluent statement with no basis in the input or in fact. It comes straight from next-word prediction: the model produces what a good answer usually looks like, including numbers and names ({{REF:halluc}}). Two real cases: in 2023 lawyers in New York were fined after filing court cases invented by a chatbot; in 2024 a Canadian tribunal made Air Canada honour a refund policy its chatbot had made up.

PM decision. Decide which questions the product must refuse, where every claim needs a source, and how you will measure false statements before launch, not after complaints.

8. Prompting: writing the brief

Everyday picture. “Write something about diet” gets you a generic essay from any intern. “You are writing to Neha, 27, vegetarian, who logged 1,420 calories today; give one tip under 25 words, no medical advice” gets you something usable.

How it works. A prompt is the full set of instructions and material sent with each request. Good prompts have five parts: a role, the context (the user’s data), an example of a good answer, the steps or rules, and the format of the output. The instructions that the app sends every time, which the user never sees, are called the system prompt.

Before
Give diet advice to the user based on their food log.
After
Role: a friendly diet coach for Indian users.
Context: Neha, 27, vegetarian, goal 1,600 kcal; today 1,420 kcal, protein 38 g.
Example: “Good day! Protein is low: add a bowl of curd at dinner.”
Rules: one tip, under 25 words; never mention medicines, doses or illnesses; if she mentions a symptom, suggest she asks her coach.
Format: plain text, no emojis.

PM decision. The prompt is product copy and product policy at once. Keep it under version control, test every change against the eval set, and write the “never” rules as clearly as a legal clause.

In the interview · LLMs
  • “Why is our AI feature so expensive?” Count tokens in and out per task, times price, times uses; cut what you send before you cut the model.
  • “Why did the bot make up an answer?” It predicts likely text; ground it in your data, show sources, and let it say “I don’t know”.
  • “Should we build our own model?” Almost never at first: buy one, shape it with prompts and data, measure it with evals.
