Health, fitness & learning

Indore, the first Monday of January

Neha, 27, downloads a fitness app after a New Year resolution. Before it shows her anything, it asks 22 questions: weight, goal weight, diet type, allergies, wake-up time, medical conditions. She logs breakfast on Monday and Tuesday, forgets on Wednesday, and never opens it again. The same week, a green owl reminds her that her Spanish streak on Duolingo is at 212 days, and she does a two-minute lesson in the office lift to keep it alive. Four hundred kilometres away in Nashik, her mother, 58, waits in a clinic with a plastic folder of old reports, while the doctor, on his fortieth patient of the morning, flips through them page by page.

Try it: why does one app keep Neha for 212 days and the other lose her in three? If you ran the fitness app, what would you change first? Think for five minutes before reading on.

Selling a change that takes months

Health, fitness and learning apps all sell the same hard thing: a better version of you, weeks or months from now. Losing 5 kg, controlling blood sugar or clearing an entrance exam takes a long time, and most people give up long before the result arrives. So these businesses live or die on two things. First, keeping people going through the dull middle, when motivation has faded and the result hasn’t come yet. Second, getting scarce experts, such as doctors, coaches and teachers, to serve many more people than they could in person, without the quality falling. A wrong answer here costs more than in most apps: a bad film suggestion wastes an evening, while a bad dose or a false exam promise can hurt someone.

Who does what, and who pays whom

Four kinds of people pay ({{REF:money}}). Often it is the user: Neha paying ₹999 a month for a diet coach. In learning it is usually parents, who pay for a course their child will use. Employers pay for staff wellness programmes and health check-ups. Insurers pay hospitals and, more and more, apps that keep policyholders healthy. The money goes to three kinds of supply: experts (doctors, dietitians, trainers, teachers), who are scarce and expensive; places (clinics, gyms, labs, pharmacies, coaching centres), which cost rent every month whether full or empty; and content and AI (recorded lectures, workout videos, a chatbot), which cost a lot once and very little per extra user.

The three businesses look alike from far away but differ in the details that decide a PM’s choices.

The unit economics, worked in rupees

A coaching plan. Take a ₹999-a-month diet coaching plan ({{REF:coachwf}}). GST of 18% is inside the price, so ₹152 goes to the government. The biggest cost is the coach. A dietitian paid ₹60,000 a month who looks after 150 clients costs ₹60,000 ÷ 150 = ₹400 a client. Marketing, spread over the months a customer stays, takes another ₹250. About ₹110 is left. That is why every coaching app tries to let each coach handle more clients, with AI drafting the meal feedback and the coach checking it: at 250 clients a coach, the coach’s cost falls to ₹240 and the margin more than doubles.

A gym centre. A gym’s costs are mostly fixed: rent, trainers and upkeep of about ₹7.5 lakh a month for a mid-sized centre ({{REF:gym}}). If a member pays about ₹2,000 a month, ₹1,700 after GST, the centre needs ₹7,50,000 ÷ ₹1,700 ≈ 441 members just to break even. But the room only fits about 520 regular members before the 7 am classes are full. The margin sits in a narrow band, which is why chains move to franchises (someone else pays the rent) and sell app-only plans that need no floor space.

A test-prep course. Here the cost of making a lecture is fixed: a teacher records it once, and it can be watched by 10 or 10 lakh students. PhysicsWallah gives much of it away on YouTube. Out of 100 students who watch a free lecture, perhaps 20 install the app, 4 buy a ₹4,000 online batch and 0.4 join an offline centre at about ₹80,000 a year ({{REF:funnel}}). In FY26, offline enrolments were 4.7 lakh of PW’s 53.4 lakh paying students, about 9%, yet centres bring in a large share of the money.

Players and numbers

Most Indian players are still losing money or have only just stopped ({{REF:players}}). PhysicsWallah, which listed on the stock exchanges in November 2025, grew revenue 35% to ₹3,900 crore in FY26 and nearly broke even. Tata 1mg (online pharmacy, lab tests, teleconsults) earns ₹2,392 crore but still loses ₹276 crore. Cult.fit cut its loss from ₹889 crore in FY24 to ₹252 crore in FY26 and filed for an IPO in July 2026. Practo made its first full year of operating profit in FY25 on ₹234 crore of revenue. HealthifyMe shrank to ₹178 crore but nearly stopped losing money, and is moving into weight-loss drug support. Globally, Duolingo earned $298.5 million in April–June 2026 alone. The biggest failure is Byju’s, once valued at $22 billion and in insolvency since July 2024.

Turning points

Three changes shaped the sector ({{REF:timeline}}). Covid (2020–21) made video consults legal and normal (the Telemedicine Practice Guidelines of 25 March 2020) and moved every classroom online for a year; edtech firms raised huge sums on the belief that it would last. The Byju’s collapse (2022–24) showed it didn’t: when schools reopened, parents stopped paying for apps, and aggressive selling turned into refund complaints and unpaid loans. The winners since then mix online and offline, like PhysicsWallah’s centres. Digital public health rails (2021 onwards): the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission gives each person a 14-digit ABHA ID to which clinics and labs can link records, shared only with consent, so a patient no longer needs a plastic folder.

The non-obvious mechanics

Motivation is seasonal. Fitness sign-ups spike in January and again before the festive season, and most January joiners are gone by March ({{REF:cliff}}). A team that judges a new feature on January numbers will fool itself; a team that plans for the drop can catch people in week two, when resolve fades.

Results are slow, so apps sell progress. Weight goes up and down day to day and falls only a little over weeks; an exam is months away. So the apps give a signal that moves every day the user shows up: a streak, a ring filling, a lesson ticked ({{REF:outcome}}). This keeps people going, but it can drift from the goal: a user can keep a 200-day streak on the easiest lessons and learn very little.

Experts are the bottleneck. India has far fewer doctors, dietitians and good teachers than it needs, and their time can’t be copied. So products aim to stretch an expert’s time: a teacher recorded once for lakhs, a coach checking AI-drafted meal feedback for 250 clients, a doctor who reads a one-page summary instead of a folder. Free is the biggest rival. YouTube workouts, free lectures and free diet charts compete with every paid plan; paid products win on structure (a plan, a batch, a schedule), a human who checks in, and proof of results. Trust comes first. Health data is sensitive personal data under the DPDP Act and needs clear consent; online pharmacies face rules on prescriptions; coaching centres may not promise ranks. An app that breaks trust once rarely gets a second chance.

In the interview · Questions this sector loves
  • “Users drop off in the first week. Fix it.” Find the first moment of real value and get people there faster; count who still comes back in week two.
  • “Design an AI feature for doctors, coaches or teachers.” Decide what the AI may do alone, what an expert must check, and what it must never do.
  • “Engagement fell 15%. Why?” Split the metric, find the segment, check recent releases and experiments; read any test result with care.
  • “How would you measure success?” Pair a daily habit metric with an outcome metric, so a streak can’t hide that nobody is getting healthier or learning.
