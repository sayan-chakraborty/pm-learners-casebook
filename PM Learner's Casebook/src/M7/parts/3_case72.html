Case 7.2 · HealthifyMe: users dropping off early ImprovementMediumIIM B Sigma PM Casebook 2026–27, p. 97
Try it first. Think of an app you installed with good intentions and stopped using within a week. What happened on the day you stopped? Now structure “users are dropping off very early; fix it” out loud, for five minutes.
{{CASE:B97}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Asked when it started and what “engagement” means, and got a precise answer: onboarding completion and first-week retention are down.
  • Listed what the app already does (calorie scanner, coaching, store) before suggesting anything new.
  • Two concrete fixes with a risk each: skipping may leave profiles empty; syncing raises privacy worries.
  • Never used the data on offer. The interviewer named two falling metrics; the candidate went to “my personal experience” instead of asking at which onboarding screen people leave.
  • The “when” question went unanswered and wasn’t repeated. A drop that began with a new release points somewhere very different from a slow slide.
  • Picked the wrong group. Goal-driven enthusiasts who “track calories regularly” are the least likely to quit in week one. The people leaving early are newcomers.
  • No first moment of value. Skipping the questions only helps if the user then gets something useful fast; otherwise the drop moves from day 0 to day 2.
  • “RICE and Value vs Impact vs Effort” were named but not used, and no metric or safety check was given.

Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing ✓ · Goal ✓ early retention · Who ✗ new users, not enthusiasts · Today and the trigger ✗ asked “when”, didn’t get it, let it go · Edges ✗ Android or iPhone, India or global. Improvement add-on: which journey stage ✓ onboarding, from the interviewer. The costliest skip: “today and the trigger”. If the drop started with a longer sign-up form last month, the fix is to undo that, not to redesign onboarding.

Check · “Sync with Apple Health / Google Fit … removes all the initial manual effort” Overstated

Two problems. First, Google stopped letting new apps sign up for the Google Fit APIs in May 2024 and supports them only until the end of 2026; on Android the route is now Health Connect. Second, a sync fills in steps, weight and sleep, which a phone already knows. The slow questions are the ones no phone knows: goal weight, vegetarian or not, allergies, medical conditions. Syncing helps, but it removes perhaps a quarter of the effort, not all of it.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

Two things first. Did this start on a date, for example after a release, or has it slid slowly? And can I see the onboarding funnel: how many installs reach each screen, log a first meal and come back on day 2 and day 7? I’ll assume India, Android and iPhone both, and new users only.

Q

A slow slide over two quarters. Here is the funnel.

Why this move: a sudden drop means “find the change”; a slow slide means the experience no longer fits new users. Asking for the funnel puts data where the candidate put a hunch.

A
② Find the step

Out of 100 installs, 55 finish the questions, 38 log a first meal, 20 come back on day 2 and 12 are active on day 7 ({{REF:onb}}). The biggest single loss is the 45 who never get past the questions. But the second loss matters too: of the 38 who log a meal, only 20 return the next day. So there are two leaks: the form, and a first day that doesn’t give enough reason to come back.

Why this move: naming two leaks keeps the answer from stopping at “shorten onboarding”. A shorter form feeding into the same weak day 1 would only move the drop one day later.

A
③ Who

The person to design for is Neha, 27, in Indore: a new user in January who wants to lose 5 kg before a wedding in April. She is keen on day 1, busy on day 3, and has never counted calories. She doesn’t know what 1,600 calories looks like on a plate.

Why this move: the early leavers are beginners, not enthusiasts. Beginners need the app to do the work, so the design aims at effort, not features.

A
④ Aha moment

Next I’d find what separates people who stay from people who leave. Say users who log three meals in their first three days are still active on day 30 at 45%, against 12% for everyone else ({{REF:aha}}). That gives a target: get Neha to three logged meals by day 3. It doesn’t prove the cause, since keen people both log and stay, so I’d test it: make logging easier for a random half and see whether their day-30 number rises.

Why this move: a named first moment of value turns “improve engagement” into a single target the whole team can aim at, and the random test protects against mistaking keenness for cause.

A
⑤ Bets

1. Five questions, not 22: goal, current weight, height, veg or non-veg, and one medical question. Ask the rest when they matter: “any allergies?” the first time she logs a dish with nuts. 2. Photo first: the calorie scanner already exists; make “snap your lunch” the very first action, with a one-tap fix if the guess is wrong. 3. A prompt at her lunch time: on day 2, at the hour she logged lunch on day 1, one notification: “Snap your lunch.” Health Connect and Apple Health sync is useful for steps and weight, as a later, optional step.

Why this move: each bet makes the next action easier or better timed. None needs Neha to be more motivated, because motivation is exactly what fades by day 3.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): share of new users who log three meals in their first three days. Inputs: share finishing the five questions; time from install to first logged meal (target under 90 seconds). Safety checks (guardrails): day-30 retention, so we aren’t just moving the drop; and the share of users reaching a healthy weekly weight loss, not a crash diet, since a shorter form asks fewer medical questions. Sizing: if 10 lakh people install in January and day-7 activity rises from 12% to 20%, that is 80,000 more active users; if 5% of them buy a ₹2,000 plan, 80,000 × 5% × ₹2,000 = ₹80 lakh from one month’s installs (illustrative).

Why this move: the North Star measures the aha moment directly and moves within days; the guardrails stop the team from gaming it, and the weight-loss check keeps the product honest about health.

How the answer hangs togetherThe funnel showed two leaks, the form and day 1. The aha moment (three meals in three days) linked them: shorten the form so Neha reaches her first meal fast, and prompt her on day 2 so she reaches three. The North Star measures that moment, the day-30 guardrail checks it lasts, and the ₹80 lakh sum shows why a small lift in week one is worth a quarter of work.
Framework · Behaviour change: B = MAP (Fogg)

1 · The situation.On Monday Neha is keen and pushes through 22 questions. On Wednesday she is just as busy but less keen, logging means typing every item of her lunch, and nothing reminds her. She skips. Nothing about her goal changed; the moment did.

2 · An everyday example.A morning walk. On 1 January you are keen. If your shoes are lost under the bed and it’s cold, you stay in. If the shoes are by the door and a friend rings the bell at 6:30, you go, even on a lazy day. Three things decided it: how much you wanted to, how easy it was, and whether something prompted you at the right moment.

3 · The framework.BJ Fogg, a Stanford researcher, puts it as B = MAP: a behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt come together at the same moment. Picture motivation going up and ability going across ({{REF:fogg}}). There is a curved action line: above it, a prompt leads to action; below it, the prompt is ignored or annoys. When motivation is high, people will do hard things. When it is low, only easy things happen. Motivation is hard to raise and it swings, so products usually win by making the action easier and prompting at the right moment. Fogg calls the smallest version of a habit a tiny habit: “log one item” rather than “log everything you ate”.

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. Name the one behaviour you want: “log lunch on day 2”, not “be healthier”.
  2. Ask where the user sits on motivation at that moment: lower than on day 1.
  3. List everything that makes the action hard (typing, finding the dish, guessing portion size) and remove the biggest one.
  4. Choose a prompt tied to something she already does (her lunch hour) rather than a random time.
  5. Test whether the easier version, with the prompt, beats the old one on the behaviour itself.

Common slip · reaching for motivation first: badges, pep talks, “you can do it!” messages. They help a little on good days and not at all on the day that matters.

4 · When it misleads.Prompts turn into nagging. Five notifications a day will push a user above the line once and then into switching notifications off, or deleting the app. Streaks can also backfire: a user who breaks a 60-day streak often quits altogether, because the thing they were protecting is gone. Duolingo’s answer, the streak freeze, lets a missed day pass without losing everything. And making something easy only helps if it’s the right behaviour: one-tap logging of “healthy meal” is easy, but tells the coach nothing.

5 · In the interview.“On day 3 her motivation is lower than on day 1, so I wouldn’t try to raise it; I’d make logging a one-photo job and prompt her at the time she usually eats.”

Framework · Activation and the aha moment

1 · The situation.HealthifyMe gets people to install. What it loses is the week after. The question is what a new user must experience early on to decide the app is worth keeping, and how quickly they get there.

2 · An everyday example.A new gym member. If in the first week a trainer writes her a plan and she finishes three sessions, she tends to stay for months. If she wanders around the machines alone twice, she stops coming. The gym can’t make her fit in a week, but it can give her the experience that shows her it will work.

3 · The framework.The aha moment is the first time a new user feels the product’s value for themselves. Activation is reaching it, measured as an action you can count: the activation rate is the share of new users who do it within a set time. Time to value is how long that takes. Well-known examples: Facebook found that users who added 7 friends in their first 10 days tended to stay; Slack found that teams which had sent about 2,000 messages rarely left. You find your own by comparing people who stayed with people who left, looking for an early action with a big gap between the two groups ({{REF:aha}}), that most users can reach, and that plausibly causes the value rather than just marking keen users. Then you redesign the first days around reaching it.

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. Take users who joined in one month; split them into those still active on day 30 and those gone.
  2. List early actions (logged a meal, set a goal, synced steps, chatted with a coach) and the day-30 rate for users who did and didn’t do each.
  3. Pick the action with a large gap that happens early and can be made easy: three meals in three days.
  4. Write the activation rate on the team’s wall and cut every screen that delays it.
  5. Test the cause: make the action easier for a random half; if their day-30 number rises, the aha moment is real.

Common slip · treating the aha action as a trick to force. Auto-logging a sample meal for Neha raises the activation number and teaches her nothing.

4 · When it misleads.A strong link can be pure selection: keen people log three meals and keen people stay, so pushing lazy users to log may not keep them. That is why step 5 matters. The moment also changes as the product changes: when HealthifyMe added coaching, “first coach message answered” may matter more than meals logged. And one aha moment rarely fits every segment: a diabetic user may value seeing her sugar trend far more than calories.

5 · In the interview.“I’d find the early action that separates users who stay from those who leave, say three meals logged in three days, then test that it causes retention, and rebuild the first week to get people there in minutes.”
