Back to Indore · A sketch answer for the cold-open question

Why Duolingo keeps Neha. Its daily task takes three minutes, so it fits a lift ride even on a tired day. The streak makes missing a day feel like a loss, the reminder arrives at the time she usually studies, and a streak freeze forgives a bad day. In behaviour terms: high ability, a good prompt, and not much motivation needed.

Why the fitness app lost her. It asked for 22 answers before giving anything back, then made each meal a typing job. On Wednesday her motivation dipped, the task was still hard and nothing prompted her. She never reached the moment where the app showed her something useful about her own eating.

What I’d change first. Cut the questions to five, make “snap your lunch” the first screen, and send one prompt on day 2 at her lunch time. Goal (North Star): share of new users who log three meals in their first three days. Safety checks (guardrails): day-30 retention, so the drop isn’t just delayed; and healthy weekly weight change, so an easier app doesn’t encourage a crash diet. Trade-off: fewer questions at the start mean rougher first advice; ask the rest when it becomes relevant.

And her mother’s folder. The same thinking applies: a one-page brief that saves the doctor minutes, with every value linked to its source, a doctor who confirms, and rules for what the assistant must never say.

Key idea · Make the next step small, and check it leads somewhere

People in this sector want results that take months, while the product has to earn their attention every day. The winners shrink each step (a three-minute lesson, a photo, a one-page brief) and time it well. Then they check that the small steps add up to the real outcome, with the same care they give to any other evidence: a test read properly, an eval by category, a guardrail tested both ways. When you design, measure or ship anything here, ask: what is the smallest next step, who checks the risky part, and how will we know the person is actually better off?
