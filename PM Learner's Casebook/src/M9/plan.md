## Cold open (top of the primer page)
Lucknow, a Saturday in April, the week before polling day. Ananya, 21, a first-time voter, has her new AirPods in and hasn't checked which booth she votes at. Her mother pays for groceries on BigBasket and is told she has 1,240 NeuCoins she never noticed. Her grandfather is stopped at a traffic check without his driving licence and shows it from DigiLocker on his phone. The city's turnout last time was 55%. Try it: "Ananya will spend ₹25,000 on Apple gadgets but may not spend 20 minutes voting. What would you build to change that, and how would you know it worked?"

## Frameworks (approved list only)
- **9.1 · Nudges (EAST: Easy, Attractive, Social, Timely; Behavioural Insights Team, 2014).** Everyday example: getting a family to take their vitamins (pills by the toothbrush; a sticker chart; "everyone at school does it"; a reminder at breakfast). Map to voting: pre-filled booth and route (Easy); a first-time-voter badge and a share card (Attractive); "73% of your neighbours voted last time" (Social); a plan-making prompt three days before and a reminder at lunch on polling day (Timely). Using it, step by step; common slip (nudging people who can't act: no voter ID, not registered). When it misleads: social norms backfire when the norm is low ("only 45% of young people vote"); government apps rating candidates raise neutrality problems; effects are often a few points, so size them honestly. Evidence to cite (verify): Gerber, Green and Larimer's "neighbours" mailer (2008), Facebook's "I voted" experiment (Bond et al., 2012), plan-making prompts (Nickerson and Rogers, 2010). In the interview: one-line script.
- **9.3 · Working backwards: PR-FAQ with a pre-mortem (Amazon).** Everyday example: writing the wedding-day toast before planning the wedding, then asking "it's the day after and it went badly: why?". Worked on Tata: a one-paragraph press release for the single loyalty programme, five customer FAQs and five internal FAQs (who funds a point earned at Titan and spent at Starbucks; what happens to old points; consent to share data across brands), then a pre-mortem set in 2028 listing why it failed (points worth too little, few places to spend, app nobody opens, brands refusing to fund it). Using it, step by step; common slip (a press release full of features instead of the customer's problem). When it misleads: it becomes a writing exercise that nobody tests; a pre-mortem that lists every risk without ranking them. In the interview: one-line script.

## Early catches to teach (from reading the cases)
- **9.1 voter turnout:** good clarifying questions got the causes and the segment (18–30, first-time voters). Catches: what exists already (in India, the Election Commission runs the Voter Helpline app, Know Your Candidate and SVEEP campaigns; the pattern is the same in Perugia); the North Star "turnout of app users vs non-users" is biased because keen voters install the app, so measure with a randomised reminder or a holdout; a government app scoring candidates risks looking partisan, so show facts from official affidavits, not a score; "my vote doesn't count" is named but no feature answers it; registration through an app needs identity checks and legal backing; most of the listed features give information, while the research says timely reminders, plan-making and social norms move turnout more.
- **9.2 Apple Home pricing:** strong on differentiation and on not fighting Alexa and Google on their strengths. Catches: the "30% Apple premium" from iPhone 15 vs Pixel 8 doesn't hold in India (launch prices were close, about ₹79,900 vs ₹75,999; verify); Apple's real speaker prices are far above +30% (HomePod mini about ₹10,900 and HomePod about ₹32,900 in India; verify), because Apple prices for its installed base, not for share; the goal of 20–30% share conflicts with a premium price, and the interviewer's goal should have been questioned; no cost floor or margin; the value of a speaker to Apple is partly the services it sells (Apple Music) and the devices it locks in, which justifies a lower price only if those are counted.
- **9.3 Tata loyalty:** a clean structure (group, brand, store points; migration of old points). Catches: this programme already exists as Tata Neu and NeuCoins (2022), and the candidate never mentions it; no economics (what a point costs, the earn and burn rates, breakage, the liability sitting on the books); who pays when a point earned at Titan is spent at Starbucks (inter-brand settlement) is the hard problem and isn't addressed; using the mobile number as the ID needs consent under the DPDP Act to share data across companies, and recycled numbers are a risk; no success metric or pre-mortem.
- **G9.1 food-delivery users:** "users" never defined (monthly active vs ever ordered); 70% of 18–40-year-olds with income and access is generous for South America; 40% propensity is a guess with no check; compare against a real market (e.g. iFood in Brazil, Rappi in Colombia) scaled per head.
- **G9.2 Swiggy orders per hour:** dividing a day's orders by 24 ignores that dinner hours carry several times the average; the answer has an arithmetic slip ("70,00"; 1.75 million ÷ 24 ≈ 73,000); 32.5 million users mixes up users with monthly transacting users; check against Swiggy's published monthly transacting users and orders.
