Try it first. Count the Tata brands you or your family paid last year: groceries, a watch, a flight, a coffee, a phone. Would one set of points across all of them change where you shop? Who should pay for the points? Structure the programme out loud, for five minutes.

What worked · what was missing
  • Confirmed the scope (one programme for all brands) and asked what happens to existing programmes.
  • Raised the old points early, before the interviewer did, and closed with a migration plan.
  • A clear three-level structure (group, brand, store) with campaigns set centrally and inherited by stores.
  • Saw that redemption is where the value is, including across brands.
  • This programme already exists. Tata Neu launched in April 2022 with NeuCoins (1 NeuCoin = ₹1) across Tata brands. The candidate never asked what exists or why it hasn’t worked as hoped.
  • No economics. What a point costs, how many are redeemed, how much must sales rise to pay for them.
  • “Points earned at Titan, redeemed at Starbucks” skips the hard part: who pays Starbucks for the coffee.
  • The mobile number as ID ignores recycled numbers, shared family phones and consent to share data across separate companies.
  • No goal or success metric; “loyalty” was never turned into a number.

GTM add-ons: standalone or built on existing assets ✗ · competitors ✗ · budget ✗. The costliest skip: today. Designing from scratch a programme that has run since 2022 misses the only useful question: what has it taught us?

Check · “Use the customer’s mobile number as the primary identifier” Directionally right

The number is the one thing every Tata brand already collects, so it is the right key for matching records. Three things need handling. Recycled numbers: operators reissue disconnected numbers after a few months, so a new owner could see someone else’s points; log-in needs an OTP and a check on recent activity before a big redemption. Shared phones: in many homes one number is used by the whole family; allow family members under one account. Consent: Titan, Tata Consumer and Air India are separate companies, so under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 a customer must agree, in plain words, before one brand’s purchase history is shared with another.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

Tata Neu and NeuCoins have run since 2022. Is this a redesign of Neu, or something new? And what has Neu taught us: do members earn but not spend, or shop at one brand only?

Q

Treat it as the redesign. Members join, but most shop at one brand only, and many have coins they never spend.

Why this move: a programme with years of data is a gift. “Most shop at one brand” tells you the goal; “coins never spent” tells you the points don’t feel valuable.

A
② Goal

Then I’d define loyalty as one behaviour: a member buying from a second Tata brand. A group programme only earns its cost if it moves money between brands; a Tanishq customer who stays loyal to Tanishq could be rewarded by Tanishq alone. Target the people most likely to cross: BigBasket and Croma shoppers who already buy often, shown the brand that fits their life (a coffee near the office, a hotel for a long weekend).

Why this move: “increase loyalty” can’t be measured or designed for. A single behaviour tells you whom to reward and what to count.

A
③ Economics

Say 1 crore active members spend ₹40,000 a year each: ₹40,000 crore of sales. A flat 5% everywhere, with 75% redeemed, costs 40,000 × 5% × 75% = ₹1,500 crore a year. At a 25% gross margin, sales must rise by ₹6,000 crore, 15%, just to break even ({{REF:cost93}}). Most of those points go to people who would have bought anyway. A 1% base plus a 5% bonus on the first ₹10,000 at a new brand, for 10 lakh members who try one, costs about ₹340 crore and needs a 3.4% rise. All figures illustrative.

Why this move: a point is a discount. Working out what it costs, and how much extra buying it needs, shows that a flat generous rate mostly pays people for what they would have done anyway.

A
④ Design

Earn: each brand sets its base rate from its own margin: 0.5–1% on groceries, 2–3% on fashion and electronics, more on hotels and flights. Burn: coins spendable at every brand, in any amount, at checkout, so a coin always feels like ₹1. Fund: the brand that issues a coin pays for it; the brand that accepts it is paid from a group pool, less a small fee; the group funds the new-brand bonus from marketing; and the co-branded HDFC card buys coins for card spends. Watch the flows ({{REF:settle93}}): coins earned on weekly groceries get spent on flights and hotels, so BigBasket pays into the pool every month. If its earn rate is set too high, it will quit.

Why this move: a group programme holds together only if every brand sees its own maths work. Earn rates tied to each brand’s margin and a clear settlement rule keep the brands in.

A
⑤ Move over

ID: the mobile number, with an OTP, a family account for shared phones, and a check before large redemptions to catch recycled numbers. Consent: one plain screen at sign-up: “Let Tata brands use your purchases to send you offers?”, which a member can turn off any time. Old balances: convert at their rupee value, not one-for-one: 1,000 old points worth ₹250 become 250 coins. Tell members 90 days ahead, show the conversion in the app, and don’t expire converted coins for a year.

Why this move: moving money people already own is where trust is won or lost. Converting at value is fair to members and to the brand paying for the points; a year’s grace prevents a wave of complaints.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): share of active members who bought from two or more brands in the last 90 days; say from 12% to 20% in a year. Inputs: coins redeemed as a share of coins earned; members who try a new brand with the bonus; repeat purchase at that brand within 90 days. Proof it pays: a random 5% of members who get no bonus, to measure the extra spend the bonus caused. Safety checks (guardrails): total cost of coins under 1% of group sales; consent opt-out rate; complaints about missing coins. Illustrative targets.

Why this move: the North Star counts the behaviour the programme exists for. The holdout proves the bonus caused extra buying, and the cost cap keeps the brands willing to fund it.

How the answer hangs togetherAsking what exists turned a blank-slate design into a redesign of Tata Neu with a known problem: members stick to one brand and don’t spend their coins. That made the goal one behaviour, buying from a second brand. The economics showed why a flat rate fails (₹1,500 crore, needing 15% more sales) and a new-brand bonus works (₹340 crore, 3.4%). Earn rates set by each brand’s margin, with a settlement pool, keep the brands in. The North Star counts cross-brand members, a holdout proves the bonus caused the extra buying, and the cost cap and consent checks are the guardrails.
Framework · Working backwards: a PR-FAQ, then a pre-mortem

1 · The situation.Everyone at Tata agrees “a group loyalty programme” is a good idea, and everyone pictures something different: a points bank, an app, a credit card, a VIP tier. Before building, the team needs one page that says what a customer gets, and a plain list of the ways it could fail.

2 · An everyday example.Planning a wedding. Before booking a hall, write the toast your friend will give on the day: “Everyone ate well, nobody waited, and the couple actually enjoyed it.” Then imagine it is the morning after and it went badly. Why? The caterer ran out, the band came late, the bride’s grandmother couldn’t climb the stairs. Now you know what to plan for.

3 · The framework.Amazon plans new products by working backwards from the customer. The team writes a PR-FAQ before any building: a one-page press release announcing the finished product in plain words, then questions customers would ask, then harder internal questions about cost, risk and how it works. If the release isn’t exciting to a customer, the idea isn’t ready. A pre-mortem, from the psychologist Gary Klein, is the opposite check: the team imagines the launch has already failed and writes down why, then ranks the reasons ({{REF:premortem93}}). People who would never criticise a plan in a meeting will happily explain a failure that “already happened”.

The press release, one paragraph (written for this exercise; the quote is invented)

Tata Neu: every Tata brand now pays you back where you want it. Mumbai, June 2027. From today, every purchase at BigBasket, Croma, Westside, Tanishq, Titan, Taj, Air India and Starbucks earns NeuCoins, and every coin is worth ₹1 at all of them, at the counter or in the app, in any amount. Your first ₹10,000 at a Tata brand you haven’t tried earns five times the usual coins. Your old points move over at their full rupee value, with a year to use them. “I earned enough on groceries this year to pay for my parents’ airport lounge,” says Meena Iyer, a teacher in Chennai. Join with your mobile number; you choose whether your brands can use your purchases to tailor offers.

Customer FAQ (sample): What is a coin worth? Where can I spend it? Do my old points expire? Can my family share one account? How do I stop brands sharing my data? · Internal FAQ (sample): Who funds a coin, and at what rate per brand? What does the programme cost a year, and what extra sales pay for it? How are old points valued? What stops fraud on recycled numbers? What does each brand’s P&L look like after settlement?

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. Write the press release for a customer, in one paragraph, with one quote from a real kind of person.
  2. Write the five questions a customer would ask first, and answer them plainly.
  3. Write the five internal questions a CFO or a brand head would ask; any you can’t answer is the work plan.
  4. Run the pre-mortem: “It’s 2028 and it failed. Why?” Everyone writes reasons alone first, then share.
  5. Score each reason for likelihood and damage, and fix the top three in the design before building.

Common slip · a press release full of features (“three tiers, dynamic offers, AI personalisation”). A customer reads for what she gets: “every coin is worth ₹1 everywhere”.

4 · When it misleads.A PR-FAQ can become a writing contest: a beautiful page that nobody tests with a single customer. And a pre-mortem that lists twenty risks without ranking them leaves the team where it started, worried about everything. Neither replaces data: Tata Neu’s own numbers on who earns and who spends are worth more than any imagined failure.

5 · In the interview.“Before designing the tiers, let me write the one-line promise a customer would read, that every coin is worth ₹1 at every Tata brand, and then imagine it’s 2028 and it failed: the top reasons are all about what a coin is worth and who pays for it, so that’s where I’ll start.”
