C. Staged roll-outs: a few people first

Everyday picture. Before the new biryani goes on every table, the chef serves it to the staff at lunch, then to two regular tables on a quiet Tuesday. If it comes back uneaten, only a few people were affected, and the chef learns why before Saturday night.

How it works. A staged roll-out gives a new version or feature to a small share of users first and widens it only if the numbers stay healthy. Both app stores support it. Apple’s phased release sends an update to automatic-update users over seven days: 1%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 50%, then everyone, and the team can pause it ({{REF:rollout}}). Google Play’s staged roll-out lets the team pick the percentage and halt at any time. Server changes and flags use the same idea: a canary (named after the birds miners took underground to warn of gas) is the small group that gets the change first. Many firms go in rings: staff, then beta testers who signed up for early versions, then 1% of the public, then everyone. At each step the team compares the new group with everyone else on a few numbers agreed in advance: crash-free sessions, payment success, app-store ratings, support tickets.

Worked example. An app with 1 crore users has 3 crore sessions a day. On the old version, 99.8% of sessions don’t crash: 0.2%, or 60,000 crashes a day. On day 3 of a roll-out the new version is at 5%, with 15 lakh sessions a day, and it is crash-free on only 99.1% of them: 13,500 crashes where 3,000 were expected. That looks small in absolute terms, but at 100% the same rate means 2.7 lakh crashes a day, 2.1 lakh more than today ({{REF:crash}}). The rule agreed before the roll-out (“pause if crash-free sessions fall by more than 0.2 points”) makes the call easy: pause, fix, resume.

Where it breaks. Some problems only appear at full scale: a server that copes with 5% of traffic falls over at 100%. Small groups can be unlike everyone else (staff have new phones and good Wi-Fi), so bugs on cheap phones on slow networks slip through. And a change that skips the stages can hurt everyone at once. On 19 July 2024, a faulty update from the security company CrowdStrike went to all its Windows customers together and crashed about 85 lakh computers worldwide (Microsoft’s estimate), grounding flights and disrupting banks and hospitals, including in India. A roll-out to 1% first would have caught it in minutes. PM decisions: the gate numbers and the pause rule, set before the roll-out starts; how long to wait at each step (a day for a big release, an hour for a small change); which users go first (staff and beta testers, not the biggest business customers); and the rule that “just a configuration change” goes through stages too.

In the interview · Staged roll-outs

“Our last release crashed on many phones. What would you change?” Stage every release (staff, 1%, 5%, and on), agree the pause rule in advance, and make sure the early groups include cheap phones and slow networks, where Indian users are.

D. Observability: seeing what the software is doing

Everyday picture. A courier company knows three kinds of things about its parcels ({{REF:three}}). A dashboard shows counts (12,400 delivered this hour, 3% late). Each hub keeps a diary (“14:02, parcel 8841 scanned, label torn”). And any single parcel can be tracked hub by hub, with the time it spent at each.

How it works. Metrics are numbers counted over time: requests a second, error rate, crash-free sessions, how long a screen takes to load. They are cheap to store and good for alerts. Logs are records of single events with details (which user, which bank, which error code); they are what an engineer reads to find the cause. Traces follow one request through every service it touches, with the time spent in each. On phones, crash reporting tools collect every crash with the phone model and the line of code, and real-user monitoring measures what users actually experience: how long the app takes to open, how long each screen takes to load, reported as the median and the 95th percentile (the time within which 95 of 100 loads finish, the number the slowest users feel).

Worked example: a slow payment screen. Users complain the payment screen hangs. The metric shows the screen’s 95th-percentile load time rose from 1.8 to 4 seconds this week. A trace of one slow request shows where the 4 seconds go ({{REF:trace}}): 0.1 s to send, 0.2 s to check the login, 0.3 s to fetch offers, 3.2 s waiting for one bank’s API to confirm, 0.2 s to draw the screen. The logs for that bank show a timeout error on its side since Tuesday. So the fix is not in the app: show a “confirming with your bank” state after 1.5 seconds, send users to other banks’ routes if that bank is slow, and raise it with the bank.

Alerts that mean something. An alert on every error would wake the on-call engineer all night. Teams instead set a target, such as 99.9% of payment screens loading within 2 seconds each month, which leaves an error budget of 43 minutes of slow screens a month. They alert when the budget is being used up too fast: if a day uses a fortnight’s worth, someone is paged ({{REF:burn}}). The same budget helps decide releases: with plenty left, ship; nearly used up, freeze risky changes and fix reliability first.

Where it breaks. Collecting everything costs money: logs from crores of users can cost more than the servers they describe, so teams sample. Logs can leak personal data (a full card number or Aadhaar number written into a log is a breach under the DPDP Act). Averages hide pain: a median of 1 second can sit next to a 95th percentile of 8 seconds for users on slow networks. And dashboards nobody watches are decoration. PM decisions: which few numbers define “working” for each feature (the ones the gates and alerts use); the targets and budgets; what may never be logged; and a rule that every new feature ships with its metrics and alerts, not a month later.

In the interview · Observability

“Users say the app is slow. What do you do?” Find which screen and which users (95th percentile by phone and network), trace a slow request to see where the time goes, then fix the slowest step, which is often another company’s API, not your code. “What’s an error budget?” The failure your target allows; when it runs low, slow down releases.
