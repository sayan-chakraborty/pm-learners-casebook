Consumer hardware, loyalty & public services

Lucknow, a Saturday in April, the week before polling day

Ananya, 21, will vote for the first time on Thursday. She has spent ₹25,000 on AirPods and an Apple Watch this year, but she hasn’t checked which booth she votes at, and she isn’t sure her name is on the list. In the kitchen her mother pays for groceries on BigBasket and the app tells her she has 1,240 NeuCoins she never noticed. Her grandfather is stopped at a traffic check without his driving licence; he opens DigiLocker and shows it on his phone, and the constable accepts it. The city’s turnout last time was 55%.

Try it: Ananya will spend ₹25,000 on gadgets but may not spend 20 minutes voting. What would you build to change that, and how would you know it worked? Think for five minutes before reading on.

Three businesses that run on one habit

A smart speaker, a loyalty programme and a government app look unrelated. They share one job: get a person to come back and do a small thing again, long after the first sale or the first download. Apple sells a speaker once but earns every month from the music, storage and warranty plans that ride on it. A loyalty programme gives away points today so that the customer picks Tanishq over another jeweller next year. A voter app is worth nothing if it is downloaded and never opened in voting week. In all three, the first transaction is the easy part, and the product is judged on what happens after it.

Consumer hardware: who does what, and who pays whom

Follow one HomePod mini from the factory to a living room in Lucknow. A contract manufacturer assembles it from chips, a speaker driver, a microphone array and a plastic mesh body. Apple ships it to India, pays import duty, and sells it through its own stores, its website and resellers such as Croma, Imagine and Amazon. The buyer pays the price on the box, called the MRP (maximum retail price, which in India includes GST). Of ₹15,900, the government takes ₹2,425 as GST, the shop and distributor keep a margin, the parts and assembly cost several thousand rupees, and Apple keeps what is left ({{REF:speakerwf}}).

Two ways to make money from a device. Apple keeps a high margin on the device itself and then sells services on top. Amazon did the opposite with the Echo: it sold the speaker near cost, often under ₹3,000 in a sale, because the speaker was a way to put Alexa in the home, and Alexa was meant to earn through shopping (“Alexa, reorder atta”). The first model works when people will pay for the brand and the device is good enough to stand alone. The second works only if the thing the device sells actually gets used. Voice shopping never took off, and reports in 2022 put the losses of Amazon’s devices unit at billions of dollars a year. The lesson for a PM: a device sold at a loss is a bet on what comes after, so measure what comes after.

The ecosystem. Apple’s real product is the set of devices working together ({{REF:ecoweb}}). AirPods switch from the iPhone to the Mac without pairing again. An Apple Watch works only with an iPhone. A HomePod plays what the phone was playing. Each extra device makes the others more useful, and together they make leaving expensive: moving to Android means replacing the watch and losing half of what the earbuds do. The number that tracks this is the attach rate: the share of owners of one device who also buy another (for example, 35 of every 100 iPhone owners buying AirPods). The money earned after the sale is services revenue per device: iCloud storage, Apple Music, AppleCare and a cut of App Store sales, paid every month.

Loyalty: how a point is born, and who pays for it

A loyalty point is a promise: “spend with us, and we owe you something later”. Say Priya spends ₹1 lakh a year across Tata brands and earns 5% back, ₹5,000 of points. That ₹5,000 is the earn rate at work. The moment the points are given, the company owes them to her, so accountants record them as a liability (money owed, sitting on the balance sheet). Some points are later spent, the burn or redemption; some expire unused, which is called breakage. If 70% are redeemed, the real cost is ₹3,500, or 3.5% of what she spent, not 5% ({{REF:pointlife}}).

The hard part is when points cross brands. In a single-brand programme the shop that gives the point also takes it back. In a group programme, Priya earns points at Titan and spends them at a Starbucks café. Starbucks gave away ₹400 of coffee and must be paid, so somebody runs a clearing account: the brand that issues a point pays money into a pool, and the brand that accepts it is paid from the pool, often less a small fee ({{REF:crossbrand}}). Airline programmes work the same way: a bank buys miles from the airline to give to its card users, which is why co-branded credit cards are the most profitable part of many loyalty programmes. Tata Neu’s card with HDFC Bank had passed 20 lakh cards by March 2025.

Public digital services: the state as a product builder

India built a set of public systems, often called digital public infrastructure (DPI): shared systems built once by the state that any bank, company or department can plug into ({{REF:stack}}). Aadhaar lets anyone prove who they are. UPI moves money between any two bank accounts. DigiLocker holds documents fetched straight from the body that issued them, such as the transport department’s driving licence or CBSE’s mark sheet. ONDC lets any shopping app show any seller’s goods. Private companies then build apps on top and compete for users.

Success is measured differently. A government doesn’t earn from DigiLocker. It succeeds if people can use it (reach), if officials and citizens accept what it shows (trust), and if a task becomes cheaper for the citizen (fewer trips to an office, fewer photocopies). The trap is to count downloads. A voter app downloaded by lakhs during a campaign may be opened by very few in voting week, and fewer still vote because of it ({{REF:enrol}}). The people who install a voter app are also the keen ones who would have voted anyway, which makes “app users vote more” a misleading number.

Players and numbers

Consumer hardware. Apple dominates the premium end in India (phones over ₹50,000), while Samsung, Xiaomi, Vivo and Oppo sell most phones overall. In smart speakers Amazon’s Echo sells most units, helped by frequent deep discounts; Google’s Nest Mini (about ₹4,500) and Echo Dot (₹5,499 list) cost a third of the HomePod mini’s ₹15,900. Loyalty. Airline and hotel programmes (Air India’s Maharaja Club, Marriott Bonvoy), bank card points and retail programmes (Tata Neu, Reliance’s One, Shoppers Stop First Citizen) all compete for the same wallet. Tata Digital, which runs Neu, BigBasket, Croma’s online store, Tata 1mg and Tata CLiQ, grew revenue 12% in FY26 but its loss widened ({{REF:tatadig}}). Public services. Aadhaar covers almost every adult; UPI carries about 2,000 crore payments a month; DigiLocker has 67.6 crore users. Turnout in the Lok Sabha election rose sharply in 2014 and has since stayed in the mid-60s ({{REF:turnout}}); young voters are the group that most often doesn’t turn up.

Check · “Points make customers loyal” Overstated

Most members of a loyalty programme are people who already shop there often, so a programme full of members proves little. Indians also carry several programmes at once, one per airline, bank card and store, so points rarely lock anyone in. Points change behaviour most near a reward. In a well-known study of a café’s stamp card (Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, 2006), customers bought coffee faster the closer they got to the free one, and a card that started with two “free” stamps out of twelve was completed faster than a card needing ten out of ten. The lesson for design: make the next reward feel close and easy to reach, and measure the extra buying against members who didn’t get the offer.

Turning points

Four changes shaped these businesses ({{REF:timeline}}). The iPhone and the App Store (2007–08) turned a phone into a platform that earns after the sale. Aadhaar (2010) and UPI (2016) gave India shared rails for identity and money, and DigiLocker (2015) did the same for documents. The Echo (2014) showed a company could sell hardware near cost to plant a service, and its stall showed the risk when the service isn’t used. Tata Neu (2022) tried to join one of India’s largest groups under one app and one currency; by 2025–26 it had narrowed its focus to the co-branded card, payments, loans and loyalty.

The non-obvious mechanics

A device can’t be patched like an app. Once a speaker ships, its microphones, chip and memory are fixed for years; only the software can change. So hardware PMs decide early which features need hardware (a better microphone for far-field voice) and which can arrive later by update. Points are money the company owes. A big balance of unspent points is a debt, and when a programme closes or changes, customers notice at once; in any merger of programmes, the old balances must move over at a fair rate. Generous earn, narrow burn is a trap. Points that can only be spent on a few items feel worthless, so breakage rises and the programme stops changing behaviour. A public app competes with Instagram for attention, but it has no ad budget and no reason to be opened most weeks, so it must be useful at the one moment that matters (the week before voting, the day of the traffic check). Neutrality matters more than engagement. A government app that ranks candidates or nudges one group more than another will be accused of taking sides, even when it doesn’t.

In the interview · Questions this sector loves
  • “Price our new device.” Ask who it is for and what the goal is (share or profit), find the cost floor, then price against what buyers compare it with, and count the services it brings.
  • “Design a loyalty programme.” Work out what a point costs and who pays for it when it crosses brands, then make the points easy to spend; old balances must move over at a fair rate.
  • “Design an app to raise turnout (or tax filing, or vaccination).” Find why people don’t act, remove the friction at the moment of action, and measure the lift against a group that got no nudge.
  • “Why did this super-app struggle?” Ask what brings people back each week; many brands under one login don’t give anyone a reason to open it.
