Case 9.2 · Pricing an Apple smart speaker PricingMediumIIM B Sigma PM Casebook 2026–27, p. 93
Try it first. A friend with an iPhone asks whether to buy an Echo Dot at ₹5,499 or an Apple speaker. What would the Apple speaker have to do, and cost, for her to choose it? Now price “Apple Home” out loud, for five minutes.
{{CASE:B93}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Confirmed the product and asked new or existing, and learnt it controls smart-home devices too.
  • Saw the tension between a low launch price for share and Apple’s habit of premium prices.
  • Asked for competitor prices and proposed two variants, as rivals do.
  • Refused to fight Alexa and Google on their strengths (shopping, search).
  • Didn’t challenge the goal. 20–30% share and a premium price pull in opposite directions; the candidate should have asked which one wins.
  • Missed who can buy it. An Apple speaker is set up with an iPhone, and iPhones are under a tenth of phones in use in India (industry estimates, 2025–26). The market is iPhone homes, not all homes.
  • The 30% premium is wrong twice: the phone comparison is off, and Apple’s actual speakers cost far more than 1.3 times the rivals.
  • No cost floor: ₹6,500 is less than what the speaker costs Apple once GST and the shop’s margin are paid.
  • The differentiator already exists. HomePod has recognised different voices in a home since 2019; Alexa (Voice ID) and Google (Voice Match) do it too.

Once the buyers are iPhone homes, the share target and the price both change.

Check · “Apple charges about 30% more than Google for comparable products, so price 30% over rivals” Doesn’t hold up

In India the iPhone 15 launched at ₹79,900 and the Pixel 8 at ₹75,999: about 5% apart, not 30%. More to the point, Apple’s own speakers are nowhere near 1.3 times the rivals. On Apple’s India store in September 2026 the HomePod mini is ₹15,900 and the HomePod ₹44,900, against about ₹4,500 for a Nest Mini and ₹5,499 for an Echo Dot ({{REF:ladder92}}). Apple doesn’t add a fixed markup to a rival’s price. It prices for people who already own an iPhone and value how its devices work together, and it accepts a small share of the market in return. A price copied from another product category tells you nothing about this one.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

Two things decide the price, so I’d ask them first. Which market: India, or everywhere? And the goal: 20–30% share and a premium price don’t usually go together, so if we must choose, is it share or profit? One fact I’d check: does it need an iPhone to set up, like Apple’s other speakers?

Q

India first. Yes, it needs an iPhone. Leadership says share, but it must make money.

Why this move: “share or profit” changes the answer from ₹6,500 to ₹15,000, so it has to be settled before any number. The iPhone question sets the size of the market.

A
② Who buys

Then the buyers are iPhone homes. Say 20 lakh smart speakers sell in India a year and one in ten goes to an iPhone home: about 2 lakh. Even winning a quarter of those is 50,000 speakers, about 2.5% of the whole market. So I’d restate the goal as share of iPhone homes that buy a speaker: 25–30% of them is ambitious and possible; 25% of all speakers isn’t. All figures illustrative.

Why this move: a share target only makes sense against the people who can buy the product. Restating it keeps the goal and the price from fighting each other.

A
③ Cost floor

Next, the lowest price that doesn’t lose money. Say parts, assembly, freight and warranty cost ₹6,300 for the mini. The shop keeps about 12% of the price before tax, and GST is 18% on top. So the break-even shelf price is ₹6,300 ÷ 0.88 × 1.18 ≈ ₹8,450. At ₹6,500, Apple loses about ₹1,450 on every speaker. Illustrative costs.

Why this move: in India the price on the box includes tax and the shop’s cut, so a device that “costs ₹6,300” can’t sell at ₹6,500. Working back from cost stops a launch that loses money on each unit.

A
④ Value

An iPhone owner compares it with an Echo Dot at ₹5,499 and with doing nothing. What she gets that an Echo doesn’t: it plays what her phone plays with a tap, sets up in seconds, keeps voice requests private, and runs Apple’s smart-home hub. What it earns Apple after the sale: more Apple Music and iCloud subscriptions, and a household more tied to Apple. I’d count that in rupees: say 30% of buyers add a ₹100-a-month music plan, about ₹1,000 of profit a speaker over three years ({{REF:worth92}}).

Why this move: the value is set by the buyer’s other options, and the services only justify a lower price if they are counted in rupees and actually happen.

A
⑤ Price

Now compare profit at four prices ({{REF:profit92}}). At ₹6,500 it loses money even with services. At ₹15,900 each speaker earns most, but fewer homes buy. At ₹12,900 total profit is about the same as at ₹15,900, and it reaches 55,000 homes instead of 35,000. So: “Home mini” at ₹12,900 and “Home” at ₹29,900, and a stereo pair of minis at ₹23,900. Launch with six months of Apple Music included, and sell at Apple Stores and Croma, where iPhone buyers already shop.

Why this move: the best price is where total profit peaks, and near the peak the curve is flat. When two prices earn about the same, pick the one that puts the device into more homes, because each home adds to the ecosystem.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): share of iPhone homes that bought a smart speaker this year and chose ours. Inputs: sales per week at each price; share of buyers who pair a second speaker; Apple Music trials that become paid. Safety checks (guardrails): gross margin per unit over 30% (₹12,900 gives about 34%); returns within 14 days under 4%; complaints that it doesn’t understand Indian accents or Hinglish. Trade-off: at ₹12,900 we give up some margin per unit for more homes; I’d review after two quarters. All figures illustrative.

Why this move: the North Star follows the restated goal, so the team isn’t judged on a share of homes it can’t sell to. The accent guardrail matters in India: a speaker that mishears is returned, however good the price.

How the answer hangs togetherAsking the goal and the setup requirement turned “20–30% of all speakers” into “a quarter of iPhone homes”. The cost floor (₹8,450) ruled out the candidate’s ₹6,500. The rivals and the services set the value; comparing profit at four prices showed ₹12,900 earns about what ₹15,900 does in 57% more homes. The North Star counts the homes Apple can actually reach, and the margin and returns guardrails keep the lower price honest.
Key idea · Price for the buyer who can buy, then count the whole life

A premium device is priced against what its own buyers compare it with, not against the whole market. Then look at the whole life of one unit: price, less tax and the shop’s cut, less cost, plus what it earns afterwards. The services are real only when they are counted in rupees; “it builds the ecosystem” on its own is not a reason to sell at a loss.
