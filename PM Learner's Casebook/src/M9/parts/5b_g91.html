Guesstimate 9.1 · Users of a food-delivery app in South America GuesstimateEasyIIM A Product Mindset 2026–27, p. 239
Try it first. Of ten adults you know in a big city, how many ordered food on an app in the last month? Would that be true in Lima or Bogotá? Build the number of users of one app in a country of 10 crore, for five minutes.
{{CASE:A239}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Asked the population and the product’s stage before starting.
  • A clean funnel: urban → age → access → propensity, each step multiplied.
  • Named the 41+ group it left out and said the answer is on the low side for that reason.
  • Defended the 40% with three reasons when asked.
  • “Users” was never defined. Ever installed, ordered this year, or ordered this month? Each differs by a large factor.
  • The market and the app were mixed up. 40% “use food-delivery platforms” counts all apps; the candidate then said competition lowers this app’s share, but never applied a share.
  • 70% of urban 18–40-year-olds with income and access is generous for much of South America, where many pay in cash.
  • No check. Brazil’s iFood publishes orders and customers, a ready comparison per head.

Guesstimate kit check: 1 Unit ✗ “users” undefined · 2 Approach ✓ demand side · 3 Tree ✓ · 4 Numbers with reasons ✓ for the 40%, when asked · 5 Check ✗ · 6 Range ✗. Card 1 cost the most: without a unit, 9.8 million could be the whole market or one app, and nobody can tell whether it is right.

Stronger answer
A
1 · Unit

I’ll estimate monthly users of this app: people who ordered at least once in the last 30 days. That is what delivery companies report, and it is the number that brings revenue. I’ll give the whole market’s monthly orderers on the way.

Why this move: “users” is the vaguest word in product numbers. Picking the reported definition makes the answer checkable.

A
2 · Approach

Demand side, from people to orderers, then this app’s share. I’ll take all adults over 15 in cities rather than only 18–40, and apply one rate for how many order.

Why this move: a wider age band with one ordering rate is simpler than a narrow band plus a note that it leaves people out.

A
3–4 · Tree and numbers

10 crore people; about 70% live in cities, as in most of South America, and about 78% of them are over 15: 5.5 crore. About 70% have a smartphone and a way to pay online: 3.8 crore. About a third of those order food at least once a month: 1.3 crore, the whole market. If this app is second of three, with about 40% of orders: about 50 lakh monthly users ({{REF:gtreeg91}}).

Why this move: every step has a reason a listener can argue with, and the market and the app are separate numbers, so a wrong market share doesn’t hide inside another rate.

A
5 · Check

Brazil’s iFood handles about 12 crore orders a month for about 21 crore people: 0.57 orders a person a month. My market: 1.3 crore people × about 2 orders ÷ 10 crore = 0.26, about half of Brazil’s. Brazil is the region’s most developed delivery market, so half sounds right for a typical country.

Why this move: orders per person lets you compare countries of any size, and a published leader gives an upper bound.

A
6 · Range

3 to 8 million monthly users of this app, most likely about 5 million; the whole market 10 to 20 million. The biggest swing is how often people order: in a Brazil-like country the market would be nearer 20 million.

Why this move: a range with its swing factor tells the listener which assumption to test first.

How the answer hangs togetherDefining users as monthly users of this app fixed what was being counted. The tree kept the market (1.3 crore) and the app (50 lakh) apart, so the share was a visible choice. Brazil’s orders per person confirmed the market is a sensible size, and the range names ordering frequency as the number to check.
The reusable trick

Estimate the market first, then apply the company’s share as a separate, named step. When a question says “users of an app”, a number that skips the share is the market wearing the app’s name.
