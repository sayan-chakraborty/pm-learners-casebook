QuickBooks: the books, and the money that moves through them

Walkthrough (illustrative persona). Neha runs a bakery in New Jersey with six staff and sells bread to a café chain. On Monday she sends the chain a $2,400 invoice from QuickBooks with a “pay now” link. The café pays by card; QuickBooks Payments deposits $2,330 in her bank the next day, keeping a fee. Her bank sends every transaction to QuickBooks automatically (the bank feed), and QuickBooks files the $600 flour bill under “cost of goods” on its own, because it has seen thousands of bakeries’ flour bills. On Friday she runs payroll: wages go to the staff and payroll taxes are filed. At year end her accountant opens the same books and prepares her tax return ({{REF:flow_qb}}).

Business model, with numbers. A subscription opens the door: plans run from about $38 to $275 a month in the US. The bigger money is in what moves through the books. A business on the Plus plan pays about $1,380 a year; add card payments, payroll and a small loan, and the same business may pay Intuit nearly three times that ({{REF:qb_money}}). This is why Intuit’s online business revenue grew 19% in FY2026 while customer numbers grew more slowly: more money moves through each customer, and more customers buy payroll and payments. Mid-sized customers on the Advanced plan use payroll and payments more than smaller ones (13 and 9 points more, Intuit says).

Moat, in plain words. Years of books are hard to move: every invoice, bill, payroll run and tax filing lives inside. About the accountants: once an accountant knows QuickBooks and manages 60 clients in it, she recommends it to the next 60. And the more money a business moves through Intuit, the more reasons it has to stay. Weaknesses: the price rose steadily through 2023–26, and many small firms complain about it; and the software is built around US tax and payroll rules, which makes it hard to take to other countries.

Real-life example · Why QuickBooks left India

QuickBooks sold in India for over a decade, then stopped: new sales ended in 2022 and the service shut down for Indian customers in 2023, with about 30,000 users asked to move. Intuit gave no detailed reason, but the pattern is clear. Indian small businesses need GST invoices and returns, e-way bills and TDS, which change often; they pay little for software; and local products were built for exactly these rules. Tally has been the default for Indian accountants for decades, Zoho Books sells cheaply to firms already on Zoho, and Vyapar and Khatabook serve the smallest shops on a phone. Intuit kept its large development centre in Bengaluru, building for other markets. The lesson: in accounting, the local tax rules are the product, and the accountant who recommends the software is the channel. A global product without both loses to local ones.

Intuit Enterprise Suite: moving up to mid-sized firms

Walkthrough (illustrative persona). Neha’s bakery grows. Five years on it is a group: the bakery company, a café company with four outlets, and a central kitchen company that makes dough for both. Each is a separate company for tax. On QuickBooks she runs three sets of books, and every month her finance manager spends four days copying numbers into a spreadsheet to see how the group is doing. Worse, the kitchen’s sales of dough to the bakery and café count as revenue twice if the three sets of books are simply added up ({{REF:ies_cons}}). This is the moment small-business software is outgrown.

What it does. Intuit Enterprise Suite (IES), launched in September 2024, keeps the familiar QuickBooks screens but adds what a group needs. Multi-entity accounting: several companies in one login, with shared customers and suppliers. Consolidation: one group profit and loss, with sales between sister companies removed. Dimensions: every line can be tagged by outlet, project or product line, so the group sees which café makes money. On top come project costing, payroll and HR, Mailchimp marketing, and AI agents that do parts of the month-end work. The alternative for these firms used to be an ERP (enterprise resource planning software: one system for accounts, purchasing, stock and people) such as Oracle NetSuite, Sage Intacct or Microsoft Dynamics, which takes months to set up and costs far more.

Business model. IES is sold, not signed up for. Account managers and partner accountants run demos, move the data from QuickBooks or an old system, and sign yearly contracts worth many times a small-business plan. Intuit reported its mid-market customers (Advanced and IES) grew 28% in FY2026, and revenue from them 38% in the last quarter, the fastest-growing part of the company. The PM risks are those of any move up-market: bigger customers expect features, support and uptime a small-business product never needed; a failed migration can lose a customer for good; and a sales team is expensive, so contracts must be large enough to pay for it.
