The customer funnel and brand lift

Before the October sale, a fashion app spends ₹5 Cr on a video campaign on YouTube and Instagram (illustrative). A week later the CFO asks whether it worked. Sales are up, but they are always up before a sale, and most people who saw the video won’t buy for weeks. Clicks tell you little: few people click a brand video. The app needs a way to measure what the campaign did to people’s minds, not just their fingers.

The funnel from “never heard of it” to “buys again”

Marketing thinks in a funnel of mental steps, each with its own measure ({{REF:mfunnel}}). A campaign usually aims at one stage. A brand video aims at the top (people remember you and think well of you); a sale-day search ad aims at the bottom (people buy now). Judging a top-of-funnel campaign by next-day sales is like judging a school by one week’s attendance: the effect is real but slow.

How a brand lift study works

A brand lift study measures the top of the funnel with a randomised experiment. Among the people the campaign targets, the ad platform (YouTube, Meta) randomly keeps some from seeing the ad: the control group sees whatever ad would have run otherwise. The rest, the exposed group, see the campaign. A few days later both groups get the same one-question survey inside the app: “Which of these fashion apps have you heard of?” or “Which would you use first for festive wear?”. Because the groups were split at random, any difference comes from the campaign ({{REF:lift}}).

Worked example. 5,000 people in each group answer. In the control group 30% name the app when asked about festive wear; in the exposed group, 42%. Absolute lift = 42% − 30% = 12 points. Relative lift = 12 ÷ 30 = 40%. Is it real? With 5,000 answers per group the margin of error on the difference is about ±2 points, so a 12-point gap is far outside chance. Then the business sum: if the campaign reached 1 crore people, 12% of them, about 12 lakh extra people, now think of the app first, at about ₹40 each (₹5 Cr ÷ 12 lakh).

When it breaks

People say one thing and do another: a lift in “first choice” answers may never turn into orders. Survey takers are a special group (people who answer in-app polls). The control group can still see the brand elsewhere, on billboards or TV, which shrinks the measured lift. And a short survey can’t capture effects that build over months.

In the interview

“I’d first ask which stage of the funnel the campaign targets. For a brand video I’d run a brand lift study with a random control group and measure recall or preference; for a sale-day ad I’d measure incremental orders. I wouldn’t judge a brand campaign by next-day clicks.”

PM decisions it drives. Which funnel stage a feature or campaign is meant to move, and therefore which metric proves it. How big the control group must be (larger groups give a tighter margin of error but hide the ad from more people). Whether a lift is worth its cost per person persuaded. And a caution: a lift study proves the ad changed minds; only a sales test proves it changed wallets.
