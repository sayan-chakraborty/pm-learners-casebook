Case 3.3 · Amazon’s return rate is rising Root cause analysisMediumIIM B Sigma PM Casebook 2026–27, pp. 70–71
Try it first. Your family’s electricity bill in May is 40% higher than in February, yet no appliance has become less efficient. Explain how. Then use the same idea on a rising return rate.
{{CASE:B70}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Asked sudden or gradual, and which season. “Gradual, with festive peaks” is the most useful fact in the case.
  • Narrowed by category and by region before brainstorming.
  • A clean list of five reasons, then fixes for each of the three chosen.
  • Saw the cost of a shorter return window and proposed a pilot.
  • Never asked what the rate is, or by how much it rose. From 18% to 20% is a different problem from 18% to 30%.
  • Missed the mix effect. In the festive season far more clothes are sold, and clothes are always returned more. The total rate rises even if no category gets worse.
  • Guessed the reasons instead of reading them. Every return has a reason code picked by the customer; the data would rank the five reasons in a minute.
  • The top pick is the costliest. Barcodes on every product in transit fix “wrong or defective items”, usually a small share of fashion returns, while size and fit is usually the largest.
  • Penalising customers hits good ones too. A customer who orders two sizes and returns one is often a loyal, high-spending buyer.

Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing: which rate (orders or value, returns ÷ delivered)? ✗ · Goal ✗ · Who ✓ (category) · Today and the trigger ✓ (gradual, festive peaks) · Edges ✓ (all India). RCA add-ons: size vs baseline ✗ · where it shows ✓ · recent changes ✗ · is the data trustworthy ✗. The two skipped add-ons would have exposed the mix effect straight away.

Check · “Wardrobing” as a top-three cause Directionally right

Wearing an outfit once and returning it does happen, especially with party and wedding wear in the festive season. But industry surveys consistently put size and fit first by a wide margin. Wardrobing deserves a targeted fix (tamper tags on high-value ethnic wear), not a return-window cut for all clothes.

Check · “Reduce the return window… a 15-minute return policy as a pilot” Overstated

Easy returns are a reason people buy clothes online at all. Shrinking the window cuts returns partly by cutting sales. A gentler tool is try-and-buy at the door, which Myntra launched in 2017 as a ₹99 add-on: the customer tries the item while the rider waits, so a misfit costs one trip, not two.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

I’d pin down three things. What exactly is the rate: returned units ÷ delivered units? From what level to what, over how long? And did anything change on our side: return rules, a new seller programme, a new delivery partner?

Q

Units. Overall it went from 11% to about 15% over the festive quarter. No policy changes.

Why this move: “11% to 15% in a festive quarter, with no rule changes” already hints that the season, not a broken process, may be behind much of it.

A
② Mix or rate?

First I’d split the rise into two parts. Clothes are always returned more than other things, and in the wedding season we sell far more clothes. Illustrative: clothes went from 30% to 40% of orders. If every category kept its old return rate, that shift alone would add 2.1 of the 3.7 points. The rest comes from clothes’ own rate rising from 25% to 28% and electronics from 8% to 10% ({{REF:mix}}).

Why this move: more than half the “problem” is simply selling more clothes, which is good news. Only the smaller part is a real deterioration.

A
③ Reason codes

For the part that is a real rise inside clothes, I’d read the reason each customer chose when returning, and cut it by brand, seller, size, payment type, and new versus repeat buyers. I’d also count bracketing: one order with the same item in two sizes, like Pooja’s kurta. Illustrative split of clothing returns: size and fit 55%, “didn’t like it” 20%, damaged or wrong item 10%.

Why this move: the data ranks the causes in minutes; guessing them risks spending effort on a small one.

A
④ Size it

Each return loses about ₹250 (two trips, a check, a discounted resale). 3 extra points on, say, 1 crore clothing units a month = 3 lakh extra returns. 3 lakh × ₹250 = ₹7.5 Cr a month. Size and fit is 55% of that, about ₹4 Cr, so it gets most of the effort.

Why this move: rupees turn a list of causes into a priority order anyone in the room can agree on.

A
⑤ Fix by root cause

Size and fit: a fit hint on each product, built from its returns (“runs small: 60% chose one size up”), plus size advice based on what fitted the customer before. Bracketing: show that hint at the moment someone adds a second size. Bad sellers: sellers whose return rate is twice their category’s get flagged and drop in search results. Wardrobing: tamper tags only on high-value party and ethnic wear.

Why this move: each fix targets one measured cause, so we can tell later which one worked.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): kept sales per visitor (NMV per visit), not the return rate on its own. Safety checks (guardrails): conversion rate; complaints about refused returns. Trade-off: anything that makes returns harder lowers the return rate but also lowers sales. Next time: report festive-season return rates with the category mix held fixed, so the season stops looking like a crisis.

Why this move: a return rate can be “improved” by scaring buyers away. Kept sales per visitor can only rise if customers buy and keep more.

How the answer hangs togetherClarifying the size of the rise (11% to 15%) made it worth splitting. The mix-or-rate split showed that only about a third is a real deterioration. Reason codes and rupees then ranked what is left, and the fixes follow those causes. The goal, kept sales per visitor, makes sure we cut returns without cutting sales.
Framework · Mix or rate? Splitting a change in an average

1 · The situation.An overall rate is an average across groups: here, categories. It can change because the groups changed, or because the mix of groups changed. The candidate went straight to the groups.

2 · An everyday example.Your electricity bill is 40% higher in May than in February. The fridge, fans and TV use the same power per hour as before. What changed is the mix of hours: the air conditioner, which uses the most power, now runs six hours a night. No appliance got worse; the bill still went up.

3 · The framework.Change in the overall rate = mix effect (how much the shares moved, at the old rates) + rate effect (how much each group’s rate moved, at the old shares) + a small bit from both moving together. Worked numbers in {{REF:mix}}: clothes rise from 30% to 40% of orders at a 25% return rate, so the mix effect for clothes is 10 points × 25% = 2.5 points; the rest of the catalogue shrinks by 10 points at 4%, giving back 0.4. Mix: +2.1. Rate: clothes 30% × 3 points = 0.9, electronics 20% × 2 points = 0.4. Rate: +1.3. Both moving together: 10% × 3 points = 0.3. Total: 3.7 points.

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. Pick the groups (categories) and write down each group’s share of orders and return rate, before and after.
  2. Mix effect: for each group, change in share × old rate. Add them up (+2.5 − 0.4 = +2.1).
  3. Rate effect: for each group, old share × change in rate. Add them up (+0.9 + 0.4 = +1.3).
  4. The small remainder is both moving at once (+0.3). Check the three parts add to the total change (3.7).
  5. Explain the mix part as a business change (more clothes sold); investigate only the rate part.

Common slip · mixing old and new numbers inconsistently, which double counts. Always use old rates for the mix effect and old shares for the rate effect.

4 · When it misleads.The answer depends on how you group. “Clothes” may look steady while kurtas soar and T-shirts fall, so split one level deeper where the money is. And mix can reverse a conclusion entirely (called Simpson’s paradox): a seller can have a lower return rate than a rival in every single category and still a higher rate overall, just because it sells more clothes.

5 · In the interview.“Before looking for what got worse, I’ll check how much of the rise is just the mix shifting towards clothes. Only the part left over needs a fix.”
