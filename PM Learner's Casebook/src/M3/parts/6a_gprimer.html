Guesstimates: a number from nothing, out loud

“How many searches does Google answer each second?” Nobody expects you to know. The interviewer wants to watch you break an unknown number into pieces you can estimate, pick sensible values, and check the result. A wrong answer with a clear structure scores better than a right answer you can’t explain.

Four ideas that make every guesstimate easier

1. Top-down or bottom-up. Top-down starts from a big known number and narrows it: India’s 146 crore people → online → shopping on one app → orders a day. Bottom-up starts from one small unit and multiplies it out: one dark store’s pickers → orders an hour → stores in a city. Top-down is fast and suits “how many users or searches”. Bottom-up suits “how many stores, riders or servers”, where capacity is physical.

2. Demand side or supply side. Swiggy orders an hour can be counted from the eaters (households × how often they order) or from the riders (riders online × orders each carries an hour). They should agree. When they don’t, one of your assumptions is wrong, and finding which one is the best part of the answer.

3. Proxies. A proxy is a number you know that moves with the one you don’t. UPI’s 79 crore payments a day are a proxy for how often Indians pay digitally. A city’s number of two-wheelers is a proxy for potential bike-taxi riders. Name the proxy and say why it moves with the answer.

4. Round numbers and sanity checks. Use numbers that multiply easily: 146 crore becomes 150 crore, 86,400 seconds in a day become about 1 lakh (when precision matters, keep 86,400). Then check the answer per person: “13 billion searches a day is 3 per internet user”. If the per-person figure sounds silly, a step is wrong.

The guesstimate kit

Start with a wedding caterer. A caterer in Lucknow is asked how much biryani to cook for a wedding. She doesn’t guess “100 kg”. She asks how many guests are invited (400) and what time dinner is served (late, so everyone eats). Most weddings see 80% turn up: 320 people. About 90% will take biryani: 290. At 250 g a plate that is 72 kg. She adds 10% because running out at a wedding is a disaster: about 80 kg. Then she checks: “last month, 350 guests ate 75 kg”. Same ballpark, so she cooks 80 kg. Every step of a good guesstimate is in that story.

1Pin down the unitWhat exactly are we counting, over what time, and where? Typical day or peak?Caterer: plates of biryani, for one dinner, one wedding.
2Pick the approachTop-down or bottom-up; demand side or supply side. Say which, and why.Caterer: bottom-up from guests (demand), not from pot sizes (supply).
3Draw the treeWrite the answer as a product of 3–5 pieces before putting in any number.Caterer: guests × turn-up × take biryani × grams a plate.
4Round numbers, one reason eachEvery number gets a one-line “because”. Round so the maths is easy aloud.Caterer: 80% turn up, because that is what most weddings see.
5Check it another wayA known figure, a per-person rate, or the other side (supply vs demand).Caterer: last month 350 guests ate 75 kg.
6Give a range, and what moves itName the one assumption that matters most, and what the answer would be if it were off.Caterer: 70–90 kg; turn-up is the swing factor.

Rule of thumb: clarify in two or three questions, say your approach in one sentence, and write the tree down before the numbers. Interviewers check the tree first and the arithmetic second. The cards follow the steps taken by the strongest of the fourteen guesstimates in the IIM A and IIM B casebooks; the weaker ones skip card 5.

When it breaks

A tree with ten steps multiplies ten errors. If each step could be 20% off, the answer could be off by a factor of six. Keep the tree to 3–5 steps, and spend your time on the step that swings the answer most: for a store count, orders per household; for searches, searches per person.

In the interview

“I’ll count this from the demand side, as users times searches per user, and then check it against what we know Google has said. The number I’m least sure of is searches per person, so I’ll show a range on that one.”
