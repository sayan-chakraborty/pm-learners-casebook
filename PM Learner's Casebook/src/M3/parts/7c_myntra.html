Myntra: one category, done deeply, turned into profit

Walkthrough (illustrative persona). Aditi, 24, works in Pune. She sees a kurta on an influencer’s Instagram post, screenshots it, and uploads the photo to Myntra’s visual search. Forty similar kurtas appear. On the one she likes, a small line says “runs small: most people chose one size up”, so she orders an L instead of her usual M. It is in stock at an M-Now store nearby, so it arrives in 30 minutes. It fits ({{REF:my-walk}}).

Business model, with numbers. Myntra earned ₹6,043 Cr in FY25 (+18%) and a profit of ₹548 Cr, up from ₹31 Cr, helped by about ₹138 Cr of deferred tax credit. Half its revenue is logistics fees charged to sellers, a third is commission, and ads bring ₹915 Cr. The lesson for PMs: a focused vertical with high-margin ads and loyal 18–35-year-olds can make money where the everything-stores can’t. More than 70% of its new customers in 2025 came from outside the metros.

Moat, in plain words. Long relationships with fashion brands, years of fit and return data per product, and a young audience that browses often, which makes its ad slots valuable. 2025–26 moves: M-Now scaling across cities; more AI-led discovery; growth outside metros.

Strengths

  • Profitable, unlike most rivals
  • Deep fashion data and brand ties
  • Ads grow fast at low cost

Weaknesses

  • High return rates are built into the category
  • Meesho and Shopsy win the price-conscious shopper
  • Depends on a few big sale events

What I’d build next

1. Customers order two sizes and return one → “your size here”: fit advice from the customer’s own kept orders, shown when a second size is added → bracketed orders; size-related returns.

2. M-Now orders are urgent and misfits hurt most → try-at-the-door on M-Now orders only, where the rider is nearby anyway → kept rate on M-Now; cost per M-Now order.
