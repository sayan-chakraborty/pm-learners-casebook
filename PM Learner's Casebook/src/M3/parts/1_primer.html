E-commerce & marketplaces

Jaipur, the first night of the October sale, 11:58 pm

Pooja, 26, has had a kurta in her cart for nine days. At midnight its price drops from ₹1,899 to ₹1,299. She isn’t sure whether she is a medium or a large, so she orders both. She will keep one and send the other back. Across India, shoppers are doing the same thing in the first minutes of the sale. The app shows a sale and a spike in sales. The finance team will see the cost three weeks later, when a third of the fashion orders come back.

Try it: you are the PM for kurtas on a fashion app, and 30% of them come back. Is that a problem worth solving? For whom, and how would you know? Think for five minutes before reading on.

How a ₹1,299 kurta makes (a little) money

Pooja pays ₹1,299. The kurta belongs to a seller in Jaipur’s textile market. It sits in the app’s warehouse, a delivery partner carries it 6 km, and the app earns fees on the way. If Pooja keeps it, the app is left with about ₹195. If she returns it, the fees are refunded and about ₹250 of trucking, checking and discounting is lost. E-commerce is a business where the order you keep is the only one that counts.

Who does what, and who pays whom

An online shop in India is a marketplace: an app where independent sellers list goods and the app earns fees for bringing them buyers. Five parties touch every order. The seller owns the stock and sets the price. The platform (Flipkart, Amazon, Myntra, Meesho) runs the app, the search results and often the warehouse. A logistics partner carries the parcel (Ekart for Flipkart, Amazon’s own fleet, Delhivery or Shadowfax for many others). A payment rail moves the money (UPI, cards, EMI or cash on delivery). And brands pay the platform for ads so their products show up first. Follow the money in {{REF:chain}}.

Why India’s big platforms must be marketplaces. Indian rules on foreign investment (FDI) allow a foreign-owned company to run an e-commerce marketplace but not to own the goods it sells online. Amazon is American-owned and Flipkart has been owned by Walmart since 2018, so both must work through independent sellers. Press Note 3 (2016) set this rule, and Press Note 2 (2018) tightened it: no exclusive deals, and no controlling the stock of sellers the platform has a stake in. That is why Amazon had to exit its stakes in large sellers such as Cloudtail. Indian-owned companies (Reliance’s JioMart, Tata’s BigBasket, and Blinkit since 2025) may own stock.

The unit economics, worked in rupees

Take Pooja’s kurta from the platform’s side. The seller pays a commission of about 20% on the price before GST (≈ ₹250), a shipping and fixed fee (≈ ₹90) and, this month, ≈ ₹40 of ads. So the platform takes in about ₹380. Out of that it pays the delivery partner ≈ ₹85, funds a ₹60 sale discount, pays ≈ ₹15 for payments and cash handling, and spends ≈ ₹25 per order on support and technology. About ₹195 is left ({{REF:wfk}}). That ₹195 is the order’s contribution (money left after the costs this one order caused).

Now add returns. Out of every 10 kurtas at a 30% return rate: 7 kept × ₹195 = ₹1,365, minus 3 returned × ₹250 = ₹750. That leaves ₹615, or about ₹62 an order. At a 20% return rate: 8 × ₹195 − 2 × ₹250 = ₹1,060, or ₹106 an order. Ten points off the return rate is worth 70% more money per order. No price rise or ad campaign comes close. That is why the cold open’s question matters, and why fashion apps obsess over size charts.

Key idea · Placed is not delivered, and delivered is not kept

Meesho placed 2,668 million orders in FY26 but delivered 1,710 million: 36% of orders never reached a customer who accepted them. The gap is cancellations and RTO (return to origin: a parcel refused at the door, often a cash-on-delivery order the buyer no longer wants, sent all the way back). Then some delivered orders are returned. So always ask which number a chart shows: placed, delivered or kept. Meesho FY26 results, May 2026.

The funnel: where shoppers drop off

A shopping app is a long funnel (a sequence of steps where some people drop out at each one). The first half is about finding the right thing; the second half is about getting it and keeping it. Most PM work in e-commerce targets one step of {{REF:funnel}}.

Three turning points

2016–18: the FDI rules. Press Notes 3 and 2 forced foreign-owned platforms to be pure marketplaces. Amazon and Flipkart rebuilt around third-party sellers, and “seller services” (warehousing, loans, ads for sellers) became big businesses of their own. 2021: Meesho removes commission. Sellers paid nothing to list or sell. Meesho earned instead from shipping fees and ads, reached first-time buyers in small towns with ₹200 products, and by 2025–26 forced Flipkart and Amazon to drop commission on items under ₹1,000. 2024–26: ten-minute delivery. Blinkit, Zepto, Instamart, Flipkart Minutes and Amazon Now began taking the everyday, frequent purchases (phone chargers, beauty, even kurtas on Myntra’s M-Now) that used to keep shoppers opening the big apps.

Five mechanics that aren’t obvious

  1. “Zero commission” is not free selling. Meesho, and now Flipkart and Amazon under ₹1,000, charge sellers through shipping fees and ads instead. A seller who wants to be seen on page one pays for it.
  2. Ads are the profit engine. An ad costs the platform almost nothing to show, so most of the ad money is profit. Myntra’s ₹915 Cr of ads and Amazon’s $19.8 bn a quarter explain most of their margins. The risk: too many ads push relevant products down and conversion falls.
  3. Search ranking is the shelf. Most buyers never scroll past the first screen of results. Deciding what shows in the top four slots is where the platform balances relevance, seller fairness and ad revenue.
  4. A sale week is a stress test. Big sale events bring a large share of the year’s phone and appliance sales into a few days. The site must not fall over at midnight, bank offers must be ready, and sellers must have stock in the right warehouses weeks in advance.
  5. Cash on delivery buys trust and sells risk. First-time buyers in smaller towns trust COD, so it wins them. But a COD buyer can refuse at the door for free, which drives the RTO cost. Platforms nudge towards UPI with small discounts or a fee on COD.
Real-life example · Why Meesho grew while charging sellers nothing

A seller in Surat makes ₹250 sarees. On a platform charging 20% commission, ₹50 of each sale goes in fees, which kills the margin. On Meesho the seller pays no commission and only the shipping. So thousands of small sellers listed there, prices stayed low, and buyers in small towns found products at prices they had never seen online. Meesho earns from shipping margins and seller ads. By FY26 it had 264 million buyers and had cut its loss by two-thirds.

In the interview · Questions this sector loves
  • “Return rates are rising. What would you do?” Split the rise into category mix and within-category rates before proposing anything.
  • “How would you price Prime?” or “Should Amazon cut the Prime fee?” Cost floor, value ceiling, and how many new members a cut must bring in.
  • “Design a feature using AI for a shopping app.” First ask whether the problem needs AI at all.
  • “How do you measure the health of a marketplace?” Talk about both sides and whether they meet.
  • “Should Flipkart copy Meesho?” and “How does quick commerce threaten Amazon?” Who pays whom, and which purchases move.
