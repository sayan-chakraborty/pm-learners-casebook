Case 3.2 · Measuring Facebook Marketplace MetricsMediumIIM A Product Mindset 2026–27, p. 290
Try it first. You list an old study table on Marketplace for ₹2,000. What would make you say “this app works”? Now answer as the person who wants to buy a table. Is it the same thing?
{{CASE:A290}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Restated the product in one line and stated the goal as an assumption, which the interviewer accepted.
  • Mapped both journeys, buyer and seller, before any metric, and spotted that payment and handover happen off Facebook.
  • Grouped metrics into stages, then chose one North Star and one counter metric when pushed.
  • Segmented by category: furniture and phones behave differently.
  • Never tested whether buyers and sellers actually meet. A marketplace is healthy when listings sell and searches find something. That idea, liquidity, is the heart of the case and never appears.
  • The counter metric can’t be measured. The candidate said payment happens outside Facebook, then chose purchases per week to guard the North Star.
  • No trust metric. Scams and fake listings are Marketplace’s best-known problem; “interactions” rise when scammers message people.
  • No local view. A table in Pune only matters to buyers in Pune. City-level health is the real unit.

Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing ✓ · Goal ✓ (assumed, then confirmed) · Who ✓ both sides · Today and the trigger: mature product, about a billion monthly users ✗ · Edges: which markets ✗. Metrics add-on “what decision will the metric drive?” ✗: without it, the list grew to eleven metrics and no priority.

Check · “Counter metric: purchases per week” Doesn’t hold up

Most Marketplace deals are paid in cash or by UPI at the door, so Facebook can’t see them. The observable proxies are a seller tapping “Mark as sold”, a buyer rating the seller, and a conversation that ends with both sides agreeing a time. Pick metrics you can actually count.

Check · “Unique interactions per week” as the North Star Overstated

Messages go up when buyers haggle endlessly, when a listing is misleading (“is this still available?” asked 40 times) and when scammers write to sellers. More interactions can mean a worse marketplace. The North Star should count completed matches.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

Marketplace is mature, with about a billion monthly users, so this is about health, not launch. Two questions change the answer. What decision will these metrics drive: where to invest, or spotting problems early? And is the goal more time on Facebook, or Marketplace working as a place to buy and sell?

Q

A monthly health review for the Marketplace team. Buying and selling that works; engagement follows.

Why this move: a monthly review needs a handful of numbers that point to action, not a list of eleven. And “buying and selling that works” tells us to measure deals, not clicks.

A
② Map

There are two sides, and each has one moment that decides whether they come back. For the seller: did my table sell, quickly, at a fair price? For the buyer: did I find a table near me, from someone I could trust? Every other metric is a cause of one of these two moments.

Why this move: naming the moment of truth for each side keeps the metrics tied to what users actually care about.

A
③ Liquidity

So the core question is whether listings find buyers. Illustrative, for 100 furniture listings in Pune in a week: 62 get a message within a day, 41 lead to an agreed meeting, and 30 are marked sold within 7 days ({{REF:liq}}). That 30% is the sell-through rate. I’d track it for each city and category, because a buyer in Pune can’t use a table in Patna.

Why this move: a marketplace is only as good as its chance of a match. Visits and messages are causes or side effects of that.

A
④ Goal

Goal (North Star): weekly successful local matches, meaning listings marked sold after a Marketplace conversation, or confirmed by the buyer’s rating. Payment happens off Facebook, so I pick signals we can actually see.

Why this move: one number that both sides’ success feeds into, and that Facebook can count.

A
⑤ Inputs

I’d split the levers by side so each team knows what to move. Sellers: new listings a week from active sellers; share of listings with a clear photo and a price close to similar items. Buyers: share of searches that show 10 or more nearby listings; share of searches that lead to a message. Between them: median time to first reply; share of first messages answered within an hour.

Why this move: when the goal drops, these tell you which side, and which step, to fix.

A
⑥ Guardrails

Safety checks (guardrails): scam reports per 1,000 conversations; share of listings removed for breaking rules; how often sellers block buyers. Trade-off: stricter checks on new sellers cut scams but also cut listings, which lowers liquidity. Next time: show “thin” cities separately from “thick” ones in every chart.

Why this move: scams and spam can inflate activity numbers, so the guardrails make sure growth is healthy growth.

How the answer hangs togetherThe decision (a monthly health review) told us to keep the list short. Mapping both sides gave two moments of truth, and liquidity joined them into one number: completed local matches. The inputs are split by side, so a drop points to a team. The guardrails stop scams from making the numbers look better than the marketplace really is.
Framework · Marketplace liquidity

1 · The situation.The candidate counted activity on each side, but nothing measured whether the sides met. A marketplace with many listings and many visitors can still fail, if the listings are in the wrong city or at the wrong price.

2 · An everyday example.A Sunday vegetable market (a haat). A vendor comes back next week only if most of her tomatoes sold before closing. A shopper comes back only if she found good tomatoes without walking the whole market. Count the vendors and shoppers and you learn little; count the share of tomatoes sold and the time shoppers took to find them, and you know if the market will survive.

3 · The framework.Liquidity is the chance that a participant on one side finds a match on the other side within a reasonable time. Measure it from both ends: sell-through for sellers, search success for buyers, and the time each takes. Liquidity is local: it must be read city by city and category by category, because a buyer in Pune can’t use a table in Patna. The side that is harder to attract (usually supply, the listings) gets the first investment.

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. Name each side and what success means for it: a seller’s table sells; a buyer finds a table nearby.
  2. Pick one event you can see that proves a match happened: a listing marked sold after a Marketplace chat.
  3. Build the funnel from listing to match (100 → 62 → 41 → 30) and find the biggest leak.
  4. Cut the same funnel by city and category to find “thin” markets where few matches happen.
  5. Invest in the harder side (usually listings) in the thinnest markets first.

Common slip · counting activity (visits, messages) as if it were matches. A busy market where nothing sells is a failing market.

4 · When it misleads.High sell-through can hide a price problem. If sellers price far below value (a new phone for half price), everything sells fast and sellers feel cheated later, or it is stolen goods. And a national average hides dead cities: 45% sell-through in Mumbai and 8% in small towns average to a healthy-looking 30%.

5 · In the interview.“A marketplace is healthy when the two sides find each other, so my North Star counts completed matches, and I read it city by city.”
