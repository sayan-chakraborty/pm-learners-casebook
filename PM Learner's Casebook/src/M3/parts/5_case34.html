Try it first. Prime in India costs ₹1,499 a year. Your boss wants to cut it to ₹1,299. How many new members would the cut need to bring in just to break even? Write the one line of maths before reading on.

What worked · what was missing
  • Take A split existing and new members, the right first cut for any price change, and counted shipping cost and extra shopping, not just fees.
  • Take A gave a number and a decision.
  • Take B confirmed the bundle, the market and the goal (market share) before pricing.
  • Take B named cost, value and competitor pricing, explained why cost is hard for a bundle, and valued each benefit.
  • Take B’s answer, about ₹1,500, is almost exactly the real price (₹1,499 in 2026).
  • Take A’s whole answer rests on one guess: “membership grows 20%”. It never asked how many new members the cut needs (12.5%, see below) or whether that is realistic.
  • Take A gives the cut to 200 million people who already pay. That $2 bn is the cost of the idea; a new-member offer avoids most of it.
  • Take B adds up every benefit as if each member uses all of them. Most use one or two, so the average value is far below ₹4,400.
  • Neither tests before deciding. Amazon can try a price on one group of new visitors for a month.

Check · “Let’s assume Prime membership grows by 20%” Doesn’t hold up

A 10% price cut bringing 20% more members means demand is very sensitive to price. Prime’s history points the other way: Amazon raised the US price from $99 to $119 (2018) and $139 (2022), and membership kept growing. People who haven’t joined at $99 mostly don’t shop enough on Amazon, and $10 won’t change that. The recommendation flips if growth is below 12.5%.

Check · “Companies try to capture around 1/3 of the customer’s perceived value” Overstated

There is no industry standard. The share of value a firm can charge depends on competition and on how sure customers are of the value. A rule of thumb is fine if you call it one; the stronger move is to check it against what similar bundles charge.

Stronger answer (Take A)
A
① Clarify

Why does the VP want a cut: are sign-ups slowing, is a competitor cheaper, or do we want members to shop more? Is the goal more members or more profit? And would the new price apply to everyone, or only to people who join from now on?

Q

Sign-ups have slowed. The VP wants growth, but not at a loss. Assume the cut applies to everyone.

Why this move: “growth, but not at a loss” turns the question into a sum: does the cut bring in enough new money to cover what it gives away?

A
② Floor

Start with the least we could charge. Each new member costs about $20 a year in extra free shipping, plus a share of the video catalogue. $89.99 is far above that, so we won’t lose money on any single new member. Cost isn’t what decides this.

Why this move: checking the floor first rules out one worry quickly, so the rest of the answer can focus on the real risk.

A
③ Ceiling

Now the most members would pay. Existing members already pay $99.99 and stayed through two earlier price rises, so Prime is worth more than that to them. They don’t need a cut to stay. For them, the cut is simply money given away: $10 × 200 million members = $2 bn a year.

Why this move: it finds where the cost of the idea really sits: in the discount handed to people who would have paid anyway.

A
④ Break-even

How many new members pay back that $2 bn? Each new member brings the $90 fee, minus $20 of shipping, plus about $10 of margin on the extra shopping members do: $90 − $20 + $10 = $80 a year. $2 bn ÷ $80 = 25 million new members. On a base of 200 million, that is 12.5% growth ({{REF:be}}). So a 10% price cut must bring 12.5% more members: every 1% off the price must add 1.25% more members.

Why this move: it replaces a guess (“members grow 20%”) with a threshold (“we need 12.5%”) that a test can confirm or reject.

A
⑤ Test

Before any national change, show $89.99 to a random 5% of people who aren’t members, for six weeks, and compare sign-ups with everyone else. If sign-ups rise less than 12.5%, drop the idea. A cheaper test: a first-year price for new members only. It keeps most of the $2 bn, because existing members keep paying $99.99.

Why this move: six weeks of real behaviour is worth more than any assumption, and the new-member version limits the downside.

A
⑥ Decide

My recommendation: don’t cut the price for everyone; test a new-member price. Goal (North Star): Prime profit, from fees plus the extra shopping members do. Safety checks (guardrails): how many existing members cancel; how much new members shop (bargain joiners who never shop are a loss). Trade-off: a new-member deal can annoy loyal members who notice it.

Why this move: the decision follows the numbers: the only part of the idea that can pay is the new-member part, so that is the part to test.

How the answer hangs togetherThe floor showed each member is profitable at either price. The ceiling showed existing members don’t need the cut, so it costs $2 bn. Break-even turned that into a target (25 million new members, 12.5%), and the test checks the target cheaply. The recommendation keeps the upside (new members) and drops the costly part (discounting loyal ones).
Framework · Three ways to set a price, and the break-even test for a cut

1 · The situation.Take B named three pricing methods; Take A needed a fourth tool, a break-even test, and didn’t use it. Together they cover the two pricing questions interviewers ask: “what should it cost?” and “should we change the price?”

2 · An everyday example.Your friend bakes chocolate cakes at home. Ingredients and gas cost ₹300 a cake: below that she loses money. The bakery down the road sells a similar cake for ₹600. A family ordering for a birthday would happily pay ₹900 for hers, because it’s better and delivered warm. So her price sits between ₹600 and ₹900. If she thinks of cutting from ₹700 to ₹600, she should first ask how many extra cakes that must sell to make up the ₹100 lost on every cake she already sells.

3 · The framework.Cost-plus pricing starts from what it costs and adds a margin: it gives the floor. Competitor pricing looks at what alternatives charge: it gives the anchor customers compare with. Value-based pricing asks what the product is worth to the customer: it gives the ceiling. The price lives between anchor and ceiling ({{REF:ladder}}). For a price change, add price elasticity (how much demand moves when price moves: if a 10% cut brings 5% more buyers, elasticity is 0.5) and the break-even: new customers needed = revenue lost on existing customers ÷ profit per new customer.

Using it, step by step (on this case):

  1. Floor: work out what one customer costs to serve (free deliveries, content). Never price below it.
  2. Anchor: find the price of the nearest alternative the customer would compare with (a streaming plan alone).
  3. Ceiling: estimate the value to a typical customer, counting only the benefits they actually use.
  4. Set the price between anchor and ceiling: nearer the ceiling if you are clearly better, nearer the anchor if you want share.
  5. For a change: revenue lost on existing customers ÷ profit per new customer = new customers needed. Then test whether you can get them.

Common slip · adding up every benefit as if each customer uses all of them. That inflates the ceiling and makes almost any price look cheap.

4 · When it misleads.Value-based pricing overcounts when you sum benefits nobody uses together, as Take B did: a member who only wants fast delivery values Prime at about ₹600, not ₹4,400. And break-even maths ignores second effects: a cut can pull in bargain hunters who shop little (so each new member is worth less than $80), or teach loyal members to wait for discounts.

5 · In the interview.“The price sits between what the alternative costs and what the product is worth to the user. For a cut, I’ll first work out how many new customers it needs just to break even, and whether that is realistic.”
