PM Learner’s Casebook

Preface

This book was put together in late September 2026, one sector at a time, to prepare for product-management placements. It covers nine sectors. Each has a primer on how the industry works, real interview cases, teardowns of the products that lead the sector, and a slice of the technology a PM needs. A last chapter covers behavioural interviews.

Why it is this long

The book is deliberately exhaustive, and it is meant for learning, not for cramming the night before. Getting a job is a fine goal, and every chapter is built to help with it. But the bigger hope is that it makes you curious about the products around you: the UPI app you open twenty times a day, the grocery app that promises ten minutes, the feed you can’t stop scrolling. Ask how each one helps the people who use it, who pays for it, and who carries the cost.

Some products make people’s lives better, and some make them more miserable: an endless feed that keeps a teenager awake at 2 a.m., an instant-loan app whose recovery agents call a borrower’s contacts, a ten-minute promise that sends delivery riders racing through traffic. Most do a bit of both. And almost every product has second-order consequences, the effects of the effects. Free UPI payments gave small shops a record of their sales, which let lenders offer them loans for the first time. Food delivery gave restaurants new customers, then took a share of every order and the direct relationship with the diner. Noticing these chains is what separates a PM who ships features from one who understands what the features do to the world.

How each case is laid out

Every case follows the same order (guesstimates use their own kit of the same shape):

PartWhat it gives you
Case headerNumber, title, case type (design, improvement, metrics, root cause, pricing, go-to-market, strategy), difficulty, and the source casebook and page.
Try it firstRead only the question and spend five minutes structuring an answer out loud before reading on.
The case, verbatimThe casebook’s own interview as Q&A turns: Q is the interviewer, A the candidate. Only extraction glitches are fixed; any edit is shown as a note.
What worked · what was missingWhat the candidate did well and what they skipped, including which opening questions (the scoping kit) they asked and what skipping one cost.
ChecksA claim from the answer tested against real data, with a verdict: holds up, directionally right, overstated, or doesn’t hold up.
Stronger answerA move strip showing the answer’s steps, then the answer turn by turn, each with a “Why this move” note, the maths spelled out, and a closing “How the answer hangs together”.
Framework boxOnly where the case is the framework’s natural first use: the situation, an everyday example, the framework with a diagram and a step-by-step use, when it misleads, and how to use it in an interview without reciting it.
Key idea · Argue with the stronger answers

The stronger answers are not model answers to memorise. They were written with time and hindsight the candidate in the room didn’t have, and they have gaps of their own. Read each one critically. Find the assumption you would challenge, the number you would check, and the user it forgot. Then try your own version. After ten or fifteen cases, write down the questions you find yourself asking every time. Those questions are your own thinking framework, and in an interview they will serve you better than any framework in this book.
