Try it first. Who in your circle books a restaurant table in advance, and for what occasion? Name one group of people and one reason they would book through Swiggy rather than call the restaurant.

What worked · what was missing
  • Four clarifying questions: build approach, stage, geography and goal.
  • Named the restaurant’s real fear, no-shows, and answered it with a buffer window.
  • Handled the “Zomato already does this” push with a clear difference: pre-ordering.
  • A phased plan across product, promotion and distribution, and one metric singled out: repeat bookings within 30 days.
  • Missed Swiggy’s own asset. Swiggy bought Dineout in 2022 and runs dine-out inside its app. The launch should start from those restaurants and deals.
  • The goal question got no answer, and the candidate didn’t push. Without a goal there is no way to choose between users, restaurants and revenue.
  • No target segment. “Urban millennials” and “premium restaurants” describe everyone and no one.
  • Channels listed, none tested. Instagram, YouTube, bloggers, maps and PR all at once is a budget, not a plan.

Scoping kit check (GTM add-ons): Objective ✓ asked ✗ not pinned down · Standalone or add-on ✓ · Target market ✓ cities ✗ segment · Competitors ✓ when pushed · Budget ✗ skipped.

Check · “Swiggy already has 90M+ users, so distribution is instant” Overstated

A figure like that counts everyone who has ever installed or ordered. The people who actually order in a month number closer to 2 crore (2025–26). And a push notification about table booking reaches a delivery habit, not a dining-out one. Reach is real, but an in-app banner is a channel to test, not a guarantee.

Check · “Monetise later with a ₹20–50 booking fee” Overstated

Indian diners rarely pay to reserve a table that the restaurant would give them free on the phone. Dine-out apps earn mainly from restaurants: a commission when the bill is paid through the app (usually with a discount the restaurant funds) and paid listings. A booking fee fits only scarce slots, such as New Year’s Eve.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

Before the plan: is success more dine-out occasions on Swiggy, more restaurants, or revenue in year one? I’ll assume the goal is to make dining out a habit on Swiggy, counted as bookings that were actually honoured. And is the budget large or modest?

Q

That goal is right. Assume a modest budget.

A
② Assets

Swiggy already has Dineout: restaurants signed up, bill-payment deals and Swiggy One members. So this is reservations with pre-ordering added to an existing business, not a new category. That cuts the cost of getting restaurants on board.

Why this move: “what do we already own?” is the first GTM question, and here it changes the plan.

A
③ Segment

Who books tables, and for what ({{REF:stp}})? Friday friend groups, couples on date nights, families at Sunday lunch, office teams at weekday lunch. I’d target office lunch groups in Bengaluru first: they have an hour, they hate waiting for food, and pre-ordering fixes exactly that.

A
④ Offer

Position: “For teams with an hour for lunch, Swiggy books the table and has the food ready when you sit down.” For restaurants: pay only for honoured bookings, and a ₹100 per person deposit, refunded on arrival, to cut no-shows.

A
⑤ Channels

List many channels, then test three cheap ones for three weeks: a banner at 11:30 am for users who order lunch to office addresses; tie-ups with office admin teams; a QR on restaurant tables that says “book lunch next time”. Keep the one with the lowest cost per honoured booking.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): honoured bookings per month. Inputs: share of bookings with a pre-order; restaurants with open lunch slots. Safety check (guardrail): no-show rate and restaurants leaving. Trade-off: deposits cut no-shows but also bookings, so test ₹0 against ₹100.

Framework · STP, the five GTM questions, and Bullseye

1 · The situation.The candidate had a good product and a phased plan, but launched it at “urban millennials” through every channel at once. A launch needs one group to win first and one channel that works.

2 · An everyday example.Your aunt starts a home tiffin service in Koramangala. Students want cheap, bachelors want home-style food at the office, elderly couples want less oil. She picks working bachelors in three PG hostels, promises “home food at your desk by 1 pm”, and tries three ways to find them for two weeks: posters in the PGs, a WhatsApp group, and the PG cook. The WhatsApp group brings nine of every ten customers, so she stops the posters.

3 · The framework.STP means Segment (split the market by need or situation), Target (choose the segment you can win first) and Position (one line on why you, for them). It answers the first of the five GTM questions: Who is it for? What do we offer them? Why us, why now (our assets)? How will they hear about it and buy it (channel and price)? How will we know it works? The Bullseye method (from the book Traction, Weinberg and Mares, 2014) answers the channel question: brainstorm all channels in the outer ring, test three cheaply in the middle ring, and put almost all effort into the one winner at the centre.

4 · When it misleads.Segments drawn by age or city (“urban millennials”) look neat but don’t tell you what to build; segment by situation and need instead. A channel test that is too small gives a false “no”. And a channel that works for the first 500 customers can dry up at 50,000: the tiffin WhatsApp group worked in three PGs, and then a rival joined all thirty.

5 · In the interview.“Let me pick one segment to win first and say why, give the position in one line, and then test three channels cheaply before putting money behind one.”
