What worked · what was missing
  • Asked for the goal, then picked one and gave a reason: acquisition isn’t Swiggy’s problem, so retention and revenue.
  • Chose one side of the three-sided marketplace and checked it with the interviewer.
  • Personas that differ in how often and how much they order.
  • Used journeys for three reasons to open the app, and framed pains as beliefs to change.
  • A minute of company history nobody asked for. Confirm the product in one line.
  • The priority logic contradicts itself (see the Check below).
  • Pain points float free of the journey. None is tied to a step, and none is called the worst.
  • Five ideas, no ranking. The case ends before the promised RICE. Voice ordering through Alexa solves a problem no persona had.
  • “Retention and revenue” never became a number.

Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing ✓ (too long) · Goal ✓ · Who ✓ · Today and the trigger: what’s changed, where are we losing? ✗ skipped · Edges ✓ (which vertical). Without a trigger the goal stayed vague.

Check · “Households order rarely but spend more, so prioritise them by lifetime value” Doesn’t hold up

Lifetime value is orders × margin × how long they stay, so frequency usually beats basket size. Illustrative, at 8% contribution: a household ordering twice a month at ₹900 is worth 2 × ₹900 × 8% = ₹144 a month. A professional ordering 12 times at ₹350 is worth 12 × ₹350 × 8% = ₹336 a month, more than twice as much. The households may still be a good bet for Instamart, but not on this logic.

Check · “Swiggy is considered to have a duopoly” Holds up

Zomato and Swiggy handle the large majority of India’s app-based food orders, roughly 55:45 between them by order value in 2025–26. ONDC apps such as magicpin and newer entrants are growing from a small base. The useful follow-on is the gap: Zomato food grew 20% in Apr–Jun 2026, Swiggy 17%.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

I’ll take Swiggy’s food app in India, for customers. Swiggy is the close number two and grew slower than Zomato last quarter, so I’d aim at orders per monthly user from people who already use it. Has anything changed recently that I should know about?

Q

Nothing specific. Your call on the rest.

A
② One user

Then the user is Arjun, 29, who works late and orders dinner three or four times a week. He is Swiggy’s most valuable customer and the easiest to lose: Zomato is one tap away and has the same restaurants.

A
③ Map

His weeknight journey: feel hungry → decide what to eat → pick a restaurant → pay → wait → eat → rate. I’ll mark how he feels at each step ({{REF:journey22}}). Two low points: deciding (12 minutes of scrolling through 40 look-alike options at 8:45 pm) and waiting (the promised 31 minutes becomes 45).

A
④ Worst step

Illustrative: of 100 evening sessions, 55 end in an order. Of the 45 that don’t, most leave while browsing, before a cart exists. So deciding is the worst step, and waiting is second.

Why this move: the map is only useful once it names one step to fix first.

A
⑤ Solve

“Tonight’s three”: between 7 and 11 pm the app opens on three one-tap picks: his most-reordered dish, one new dish close to his taste, and the fastest reliable option near him. An honest ETA: a range (30–38 min) and an on-time badge for restaurants that keep their promise.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): orders per monthly user among weeknight users, from 3.4 to 3.7. Inputs: time from app open to payment (12 → 6 minutes); share of evening sessions ending in an order. Safety check (guardrail): order value per order, and cancellations. Trade-off: three picks can bury small restaurants that pay for ads, so one of the three is always a discovery slot.

Framework · The customer journey map

1 · The situation.The candidate drew the steps of three journeys, then listed pain points separately. Nobody could tell which step hurt most, so nothing got fixed first.

2 · An everyday example.Think of an overnight train from Pune to Delhi. Booking: anxious (waitlisted). Reaching the station: rushed. Finding the coach: confused. The night: fine. Arrival at 6 am: tired. If you ran Indian Railways and could fix one thing, better food on board would not be it. The low point is the waitlist.

3 · The framework.A customer journey map lays out the stages a user goes through to get a job done, and for each stage what they do, think and feel. The feelings, drawn as a curve, show the low points. Pain points and opportunities are written under the stage where they occur, so every idea is tied to a moment.

4 · When it misleads.A map drawn at the team’s desk shows the team’s guesses. Plan a wedding from the host’s desk and you will map the ceremony beautifully and never see the guests’ two-hour parking queue. Build the map from real evidence: session recordings, where users drop off, support tickets, five user interviews. And map the unhappy path too: the late order, the wrong item.

5 · In the interview.“Let me walk one weeknight order step by step and mark where Arjun feels worst. I’ll fix that step first.”
