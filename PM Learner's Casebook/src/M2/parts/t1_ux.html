UX design: from “users are leaving” to a screen that works

A food app’s data shows that 18 of every 100 people who reach the bill screen leave without paying. The design team wants a new checkout; engineering wants to know what to build; the business wants the fee to stay. UX (user experience) design is the discipline of finding out why people struggle with a product and designing it so they don’t. For a PM it is less about drawing screens and more about a process that stops the team from building the wrong thing.

Design thinking: five stages, used as a loop

Design thinking is a problem-solving process that starts from the user’s experience instead of from a feature idea. The common version (Stanford d.school) has five stages: Empathise (understand users), Define (state the problem sharply), Ideate (many ideas), Prototype (a cheap version) and Test (watch real users try it). It is a loop: a test usually sends you back to define the problem better.

Research: what to learn, from how many people

Empathise uses interviews (ask about the last real time, not opinions), observation (watch someone order dinner), surveys (how many feel this) and data (where people drop off). The notes pile up quickly, so teams group them with affinity mapping (the KJ method, after Jiro Kawakita): write each observation on a sticky note, cluster similar notes silently, then name each cluster. The clusters become the problems worth defining.

Personas that help, and ones that don’t. A persona is a short, research-based portrait of one type of user. A useless persona lists age, city and hobbies (“Riya, 26, likes travel”). A useful one describes a situation and a goal: “Riya orders dinner at her desk three nights a week, has 15 minutes, and cares most about arriving on time.” The second tells a designer what to put on the first screen.

Prototypes: spend as little as the question needs

Nielsen’s ten heuristics, on a food-app checkout

Usability heuristics are rules of thumb for spotting problems without a user test. Jakob Nielsen’s ten (1994) are the standard list. Walk the screen with each one and note violations, then fix by severity × how often users hit it.

When it breaks · Good UX for the company, bad for the user

Design can be used against users: a fee revealed only at the last step, a donation box ticked by default, a cancel button hidden in grey. These are dark patterns. India’s Central Consumer Protection Authority notified guidelines in November 2023 that name 13 of them, including drip pricing (revealing charges bit by bit) and basket sneaking (adding items or charges without asking), and treat them as unfair trade practice. A PM who ships one can win a quarter’s metric and lose the regulator and the customer. Other ways UX work breaks: “design thinking theatre”, with workshops and sticky notes that never change a decision; trusting what users say over what they do; and using five-user tests for questions of preference or price, which need numbers.

Key idea · The PM’s UX decisions
  • How much research before building? More when a mistake is costly or hard to undo.
  • Which prototype? The cheapest that answers the question.
  • Which problems first? Severity × frequency, not whichever the loudest stakeholder saw.
  • Where is the line? Nudges that help users are fine; hiding costs is not.
In the interview

In any design case, one sentence shows UX maturity: “Before building, I’d watch five users try a clickable prototype of the new checkout, fix what they struggle with, and only then A/B test it on real traffic.” If asked about fees, add: “and every fee is shown before the bill, never added at the end.”
