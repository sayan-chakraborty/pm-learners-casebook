Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing ✗ · Goal: what does a “successful IPO” mean here? ✗ · Who: which investors, which businesses? ✗ · Today and the trigger: Zomato already listed and profitable ✗ · Edges ✗. Five skips in a row are why the list came out generic.

Check · “A high DAU-to-MAU ratio signals strong retention” Overstated

People order food three or four times a month, not daily. A food app with a low daily-to-monthly ratio can have excellent customers. The retention evidence investors trust is cohort retention: of the people who first ordered in January, what share still order in June, and how often.

Check · “A high AOV highlights the potential for profitability” Directionally right

A bigger basket spreads the fixed cost of the ride. But a basket made bigger by a ₹100 discount can lose more money, not less. Profitability shows up in contribution per order, and AOV is only one input to it.

⑤ Per customer

Illustrative: it costs ₹400 to win a new customer. Each then brings ₹25 per order × 3 orders a month, on a falling retention curve, so they pay back in month 12 ({{REF:payback}}). Over two years that is about ₹640 against ₹400. Customers won with heavy discounts never pay back, so I’d show payback by channel.

A
⑥ Guardrails

Goal (North Star) of the story: contribution per order, positive and rising in both businesses, while order value grows 20%+. Safety checks (guardrails): on-time %, rider earnings per hour, and complaints, so margins aren’t bought by squeezing riders or quality. Trade-off: cutting discounts lifts margin but slows growth right before listing.

Framework · Unit economics: contribution margin, LTV/CAC and payback

1 · The situation.Investors buying a loss-making company want to know whether losses shrink as it grows. Only per-order and per-customer numbers answer that.

2 · An everyday example.A chai stall sells a cup for ₹15. Tea, milk and sugar cost ₹5, the paper cup ₹1, so each cup contributes ₹9. Rent and a helper cost ₹18,000 a month, so the stall breaks even at 2,000 cups, about 67 a day. To win regulars, the owner gives a free first cup (₹6 of cost). A regular who then drinks 20 cups a month earns that back in a day; a passer-by who never returns does not.

Contribution per unit₹15 − ₹6 = ₹9 a cup~₹25–35 an order, after rider, discounts, payments

CAC (cost to acquire a customer)A free first cup, ₹6~₹400 of marketing per new customer (illustrative)
LTV (lifetime value)Cups a regular buys × ₹9Orders over their life × contribution per order
PaybackOne dayAbout 12 months

3 · The framework.Contribution margin is money per unit after the costs that unit causes. CAC is marketing spend ÷ new customers won. LTV adds up the contribution a customer brings over their life, allowing for the fact that many stop ordering. A common rule of thumb asks for LTV at least three times CAC. Payback is the month in which a customer’s cumulative contribution passes their CAC; the shorter, the less cash the company needs to grow.

4 · When it misleads.LTV is a forecast, and a forecast of five years of loyalty is mostly hope. Averages also hide cohorts: the chai stall that counts the Diwali-week crowd as regulars will think its free cups are working. And companies define “contribution” differently (some leave out discounts or customer support), so compare like with like before comparing Swiggy with Zomato.

5 · In the interview.“Growth only helps if each order makes money and each customer pays back their acquisition cost. So I’ll show contribution per order by business, and payback by acquisition channel.”
