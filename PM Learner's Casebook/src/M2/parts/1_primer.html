Food delivery & quick commerce

Mumbai, a Tuesday in July, 8:40 pm

It has rained for three hours. In Andheri West, orders on the food app are running at twice a normal Tuesday: nobody wants to step out, and everybody wants hot food. At the same moment, half the delivery riders who were online at 7 pm have logged off. Roads are flooded, two-wheelers stall in knee-deep water, and a rider who has already earned ₹900 today would rather go home dry. On the operations dashboard, the average promised delivery time has jumped from 31 to 52 minutes, and customer cancellations are climbing every minute.

Try it: you run the delivery app for Mumbai tonight. What do you change in the next 30 minutes, and what must you not break? Think for five minutes before reading on.

How a ₹450 dinner makes (a little) money

9 pm, Bengaluru. Kavya orders a ₹450 chicken biryani on Zomato and pays ₹490 with fees. The restaurant cooks it, a rider she will never see again carries it 4 km, and it arrives in 31 minutes. Three businesses were paid out of that ₹490, and the app kept less than ₹40 of it. A decade of losses and price wars in this industry came down to that last ₹40.

Three customers, one app

A food app is a three-sided marketplace: a business that earns by matching three groups who need each other. The customer wants choice, speed and a fair price. The restaurant wants orders without paying for its own riders. The rider (the platform calls them delivery partners) wants steady earnings per hour. If any one side is unhappy, the other two suffer: too few riders and food goes cold; too few restaurants and customers leave; too few orders and riders log off. Follow Kavya’s money in {{REF:money3}}.

Two business models sit under the same app icon. A marketplace (third-party, 3P) model matches buyers with sellers who own the goods: Zomato never owns the biryani, it earns a commission. An inventory (first-party, 1P) model buys stock and sells it itself, keeping the whole margin and the whole risk. Indian rules forbid foreign-owned e-commerce companies from selling their own inventory, so quick-commerce apps started as marketplaces with “partner sellers” in their dark stores. In 2025 Eternal (Zomato’s parent) became Indian-owned and controlled, and Blinkit began moving to owning its stock: higher margin per basket, but unsold milk and bread are now its own loss.

Where the minutes go

“30 minutes” and “10 minutes” sound like the same promise at different speeds. They are different machines. Break both into steps and the biggest block is in a different place ({{REF:minutes}}).

Unit economics: one order, in rupees

Unit economics means the profit or loss on one unit, here one order, before the costs that don’t grow with orders (head office, brand ads). The number that matters is contribution margin: what the order brings in minus the costs it directly causes. If it is negative, growth makes losses bigger. {{REF:wf-food}} walks Kavya’s order through.

Quick commerce is a different shape ({{REF:wf-qc}}). The basket is bigger and, since Blinkit owns the stock, it earns a product margin rather than a commission. But it also pays for a store: rent, pickers, electricity, and the goods that expire on the shelf.

Why density beats speed

A dark store is a small warehouse closed to walk-in shoppers, stocked with 3,000 to 10,000 items and placed so that a rider can reach most homes around it in five minutes. Its rent and staff cost the same whether it handles 300 orders a day or 1,500. So the question every quick-commerce PM lives with is how many orders sit inside that 2 km circle ({{REF:density}}).

Five mechanics that aren’t obvious

1. Batching. One rider carries two orders from the same area. Cost per order falls by a third, but the second customer waits a few minutes longer. The algorithm decides the trade-off hundreds of times a minute, and the PM sets the rule for how much delay a batch may add.

2. Platform fees are pure margin. Swiggy started a ₹2 per-order fee in 2023; by 2025 both apps charged around ₹10–15 in big cities. At Zomato’s ~8 crore food orders a month, each extra rupee is about ₹8 Cr a month, with no extra cost. The only test is whether conversion drops.

3. Ads are the second business. Restaurants and brands pay to appear higher in search and on the home screen: roughly 3–4% of order value, at almost 100% margin. That is why a sponsored listing can be more profitable than the order itself.

4. Riders are now covered by labour law. The Code on Social Security came into force on 21 November 2025 and asks platforms to pay 1–2% of annual turnover (capped at 5% of what they pay workers) into a welfare fund. Karnataka’s 2025 law adds a welfare fee of 1–5% of each payout, and Rajasthan passed a similar law in 2023. Rider cost per order rises a little, permanently.

5. Restaurants push back. A Competition Commission of India (CCI) investigation report in 2024 said both apps favoured restaurants that signed exclusivity or price-parity terms (the restaurant must not sell cheaper elsewhere). Restaurants also run their own delivery and join ONDC to pay lower commissions. A commission rise that looks free on a spreadsheet can push the best restaurants away.

Real-life example · What the apps do on a rain night

Back to the Mumbai monsoon. The playbook, which both big apps run in some form: a rain surge fee of ₹20–30 on each order, shown before checkout, which cools demand a little and pays for the next point; a rain incentive for riders, extra rupees per order, so fewer log off; honest, longer promised times, because a 52-minute promise kept beats a 30-minute promise broken; and switching off far-away restaurants so that riders are not sent on 8 km trips through flooded roads. What must not break: rider safety (no incentive that pays for riding through water), and trust (fees shown up front, never added after payment).

Check · “Ten-minute delivery makes riders race through traffic” Overstated

Most of the ten minutes is bought by where the store is, not by how fast the rider rides: 2 km at 25 km/h is under five minutes. The riskier part is the pressure a visible countdown creates. In January 2026, after the labour ministry raised rider safety with the platforms, Blinkit dropped “10 minutes” from its branding (press reports). The lesson for a PM: promise the customer a time, but don’t turn it into a rider’s penalty. Measure rider safety (accidents per lakh orders) as a guardrail next to delivery time.

Worked example · Should the platform fee go from ₹10 to ₹15?

Illustrative, for one city with 10 lakh orders a month. Gain: ₹5 × 10 lakh = ₹50 lakh a month, all margin. Loss: if 2% of orders don’t happen, that is 20,000 orders × ₹37 of contribution each = ₹7.4 lakh. Net: about +₹43 lakh. The fee rise pays unless more than about 13% of orders disappear (₹50 lakh ÷ ₹37 = 1.35 lakh orders). The numbers look easy, and that is the trap: the loss rarely shows up in the first week. It shows up months later as customers who order a little less often, or try the rival app whose fee is ₹5 lower. So test it in two or three cities against similar ones that keep the old fee, watch orders per customer for eight weeks, and keep the fee as a flat rupee amount shown before checkout.

Key idea · Three questions for any order
  1. Who pays for this order, and who is paid?
  2. Which step eats the minutes?
  3. Does the next order in this store, kitchen or rider shift make the first one cheaper?
