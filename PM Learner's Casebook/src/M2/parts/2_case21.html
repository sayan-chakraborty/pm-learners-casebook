Case 2.1 · A vendor platform for quick commerce Product designMediumIIM A Product Mindset 2026–27, pp. 269–271
Try it first. A dark store runs out of Amul butter at 8 pm. Trace back the chain of messages between the app’s buyers and Amul that should have prevented it. Where would a vendor platform help most?
{{CASE:A269}}
What worked · what was missing
  • Three sharp clarifying questions. Who uses it, replace or integrate, and which goal. “A layer over SAP, not a new ERP” shaped everything after it.
  • Stated the plan and got a yes before starting.
  • Chose two of four users, with a reason: procurement managers and vendors drive availability.
  • Ordered features by dependency. Scorecards need data, so they come last. That is real product sense.
  • An explicit “leave out” list. Saying what you won’t build is half of prioritising.
  • Named RICE but scored nothing. No reach, no impact numbers. Say “I’ll order these by dependency” instead, or use the numbers.
  • Treated all vendors as one. A national brand’s key-account team on EDI and a local bakery taking orders by email need different products. Most vendors by count are the small ones.
  • Never sized the pain. How many orders to suppliers go wrong, and what does a missing item cost a dark store?
  • Twenty metrics, no guardrail, and a North Star that can improve while shelves stay empty.

Scoping kit check: Confirm the thing ✓ · Goal ✓ · Who ✓ · Today and the trigger ✓ (replace or integrate) · Edges: how many vendors, which cities ✗ skipped. Skipping the edges is why the vendor mix never came up.

Check · “North Star: procurement cycle time” Overstated

A faster purchase order (PO: the buyer’s formal order to a supplier) helps little if the truck arrives with 70% of what was ordered. Quick commerce loses a sale when an item is missing at the one store near the customer. The metric closest to that is OTIF: the share of ordered lines delivered on time and in full, backed by the item fill rate at the store. Cycle time is a good input metric that feeds it.

Check · “EDI has lower reach, as not all vendors use it” Directionally right

EDI (electronic data interchange) is a standard format in which two companies’ computers exchange orders and invoices directly. Large FMCG brands use it with modern retailers; local dairies and bakeries mostly don’t. So EDI covers few vendors but a large share of purchase value. Measure reach in rupees of purchases, not number of vendors, and EDI jumps up the list.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

Two more questions. How many vendors, and how concentrated are they? And what is the cost of today’s mess: stock missing at dark stores, or staff time? I’ll assume one country and all our cities.

Q

About 3,000 vendors. The top 150 brands are 70% of purchase value; most of the rest use email or WhatsApp. The worry is stockouts: about 6% of items customers search for are missing at their store.

A
② Users

So there are two vendors to design for. Priya runs key accounts at a national snacks brand: EDI, dozens of POs a day, and her pain is orders that fail silently. Ramesh runs a bakery supplying 60 dark stores: he reads POs in email at night and confirms on the phone, and waits 45 days to be paid. Our buyer Neha chases both.

Why this move: one product for Priya and Ramesh would suit neither. Naming them keeps every later choice honest.

A
③ Workflow

The chain is: PO sent → vendor confirms quantity → truck dispatched with an advance shipping notice → store receives and counts → invoice matched → payment. Items go missing at two points: the vendor can’t supply the full quantity and nobody hears until the truck arrives, and EDI messages fail without an alert.

A
④ Size it

Illustrative: 30,000 PO lines a day × 8% arriving short or late = 2,400 lines. If each leaves an item missing in one store for a day, that’s 2,400 store-days of lost sales, every day. Knowing a shortfall even 12 hours earlier lets Neha order from a second vendor.

Why this move: it turns “procurement is inefficient” into a number the fix can be judged against.

A
⑤ Prioritise

Using impact against effort ({{REF:ie21}}). Quick wins: a mobile PO inbox where Ramesh confirms or changes quantities in one tap, and failure alerts on EDI orders for Priya. Big bet next: shipment tracking. Later: scorecards, once there are three months of data. Not now: AI forecasting and contract management.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): PO lines delivered on time and in full (OTIF), from 92% to 96% in two quarters. Inputs: POs confirmed within 2 hours; share of POs on the platform; EDI error rate. Safety check (guardrail): active vendors must not fall, and store stockouts must not rise while we migrate. Trade-off: a portal adds work for small vendors, so email and WhatsApp stay as a fallback in the first version.

Framework · Impact–Effort and MoSCoW

1 · The situation.The candidate had ten sensible ideas and one team. A good list isn’t a plan; a plan says what comes first and what never comes.

2 · An everyday example.Guests arrive in an hour and the flat is a mess. Clearing the living-room sofa takes 5 minutes and everyone will see it. Cleaning the bathroom takes 20 minutes and matters a lot. Repainting the scuffed wall takes a weekend and nobody will notice tonight. You sort by how much it matters against how long it takes, and you say out loud that the wall is not happening.

Quick winClear the sofaOne-tap PO confirmation on a phone

3 · The framework.Impact–Effort places each idea on a 2×2: how much it moves the goal against how much work it takes. Start top-left (quick wins), plan the top-right (big bets), fit in the bottom-left, and avoid the bottom-right. MoSCoW then sorts a release into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won’t have (this time). The Won’t list is the useful part: it tells engineers, vendors and your boss what not to expect.

4 · When it misleads.Both axes are guesses, and teams guess in their own favour: everything drifts into “quick wins”. The 2×2 also ignores order. You can’t cook the curry before buying the onions, however quick the curry is; vendor scorecards are the onions-first trap here. MoSCoW breaks when 80% of the list becomes “Must”. A rule that helps: Musts may use at most 60% of the team’s time.

5 · In the interview.“I’ll place these on impact against effort, then call out one dependency: scorecards need data, so they wait. And here is what I’m deliberately not building in version one.”
