What worked · what was missing
  • Textbook clarifying: what a breach is and how it is measured, how big, which categories, which areas, sudden or gradual.
  • Outside causes first, then the journey: the right order.
  • Four steps, each with metrics, then asked which one moved.
  • Turned 25% into minutes (3–4 on a 10–15 minute promise).
  • Split the last leg into “to the landmark” and “landmark to the door”, and took the hint quickly.
  • A sudden step in a few areas means something changed on a date. The first question should have been: what changed there a month ago?
  • Never asked if the number could be trusted. If riders started tapping “delivered” at the flat door instead of the gate, time would rise with no real change.
  • No fix, no measure. The case ends at the cause.
  • Missed the knock-on. A rider stuck at a gate is late for the next order too (see the framework).

Check · “The issue is in only a few areas, so tech can’t be the problem” Overstated

A good instinct, stated too firmly. Tech can fail locally: a new map version for one area, a delivery zone drawn wrongly so homes get served from a farther store, or a rider-app bug on one phone model common in one hub. A safer line: “A regional pattern makes a server-wide failure unlikely; I’ll check local changes, including tech ones.”

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

So: order-to-door time is up about 25%, roughly 12 to 15 minutes, only in some areas, as a sudden step a month ago. Two more questions. What changed in those areas around that date? And did anything change in how “delivered” is recorded?

Q

No app changes, and the recording is the same. We don’t know what else changed.

A
② What changed

A sudden step in a few areas usually means a local event: a store moved, road works, or a new rule at residential gates. Rather than guess, I’ll break the 12 minutes into steps and compare affected and normal areas.

A
③ Break down

One order ({{REF:zepto22}}): assign 0.5 + pick and pack 2 + hand to rider 0.5 + ride 6 + gate and lift 2 + handover 1 = 12 minutes. Same split for the affected areas.

A
④ Find the step

Only “gate and lift” moved: from 2 minutes to 5. That fits the interviewer’s finding that guards now hold riders at the gate. I’d confirm it by checking whether the delay sits in large gated societies and not in independent houses in the same area.

A
⑤ Knock-on

A stuck rider is also late for the next order. Illustrative: the area gets 2 orders a minute at peak and a round trip took 13 minutes, so 2 × 13 = 26 riders are busy. With 3 more minutes per trip, 2 × 16 = 32 are needed. With the same 26, orders queue for a rider, and even homes without gates get slower.

Why this move: it shows the fix pays for itself across the whole area, not just in gated societies.

A
⑥ Fix + measure

Fix: link with society gate apps so the resident pre-approves the rider at checkout; learn each building’s gate time and include it in the promise; add riders at peak until the gate time falls. Goal (North Star): delivery time in those areas back to 12 minutes. Safety check (guardrail): rider earnings per hour and time riders spend waiting unpaid. Trade-off: extra riders cost money now; broken promises cost customers.

Framework · Breaking a process into steps, and Little’s law

1 · The situation.“Delivery is slower” is too big to fix. “The gate step went from 2 to 5 minutes in large societies” can be fixed by Tuesday.

2 · An everyday example.You have reached college 15 minutes late every day this week. Time each step of your morning: alarm, bath, dressing, breakfast, lift, bus. Only one changed: the lift in your building is under repair, so you take the stairs and miss your usual bus. And the knock-on, which is Little’s law: a dhaba with 10 tables, where 20 customers arrive an hour and each stays half an hour, has 20 × 0.5 = 10 tables busy on average. If meals slow to 45 minutes, it needs 15 tables. With 10, a queue forms outside, even for people who eat quickly.

3 · The framework.Process decomposition splits a total time (or a funnel) into steps that happen one after another, measures each step, and compares with a normal baseline. The step that changed holds the cause. Little’s law says that, on average and in a steady state, items in the system = arrival rate × time each spends. It links a delay in one step to the capacity you need.

4 · When it misleads.Steps that happen at the same time can’t be added: making tea while you shower doesn’t add to your morning, and a rider riding to the store while the order is packed doesn’t add either. Averages hide spread: a few 30-minute gates can hide inside a “5-minute” average. And Little’s law holds for averages in a steady state, not in the first ten minutes of a sudden downpour.

5 · In the interview.“Let me break the 12 minutes into steps and compare with a normal area. Whichever step grew, I’ll then check its knock-on on rider capacity using Little’s law.”
