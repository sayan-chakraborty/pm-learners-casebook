Try it first. You paid for a biryani two minutes ago. What could you see on the screen that would make you cancel before the restaurant has even started cooking? List three things.

What worked · what was missing
  • Five clarifying questions, each narrowing the search: where (metros, frequent users), when in the order (before cooking), since when (2–3 weeks, no release) and which cuisines (biryani, North Indian).
  • Named who is hurt and why: professionals who need dinner on time.
  • Grouped causes into buckets before guessing.
  • Planned an A/B test to prove the fix caused the drop.
  • Ignored the best clue. Customers cancel before cooking starts, so the cause is in the first minutes after paying (see Check).
  • The buckets overlap. “Peak-hour surges” is external, “kitchens overloaded” is restaurant-side: the same event in two places.
  • “From my experience and intuition” replaced a data check, and three features followed.
  • No calendar check. A 2–3 week spike in biryani orders in metros could match a festival season such as Ramzan evenings, or a rival’s offer.
  • No guardrail. Showing a busy kitchen may cut orders along with cancellations.

Scoping kit check (RCA add-ons): Exact definition ✓ given · Size vs baseline: 4–5% of what? ✗ · Since when ✓ · Where it shows ✓✓ · Recent changes ✓ · Can we trust the data? ✗. “A 4–5% increase” could mean 3.0% → 3.1% (noise) or 3% → 7.5% (a crisis). Always ask.

Check · “The biggest contributors are wrong prep-time estimates and items unavailable after ordering” Doesn’t hold up

The interviewer said customers cancel before preparation begins. Wrong prep-time estimates show up later, as food running late. An item that turns out to be unavailable is usually the restaurant cancelling, not the customer. Early customer cancellations point to what the customer saw in the first minutes: a long “waiting for the restaurant to accept”, an arrival time that jumped after payment, or second thoughts about the bill.

Stronger answer
A
① Clarify

Customer cancellations as a share of orders: from what level to what? And how long after payment do people cancel? One more: do we trust the tagging, or could restaurant cancellations be logged as customer ones?

Q

From about 3% to 7.5% of metro orders. Most cancel within three minutes of paying. The tagging is fine.

A
② Size

Illustrative: 4.5 extra cancellations per 100 orders, on 20 lakh metro dinner orders a day, is 90,000 cancelled dinners a day. Each is a refund, a restaurant annoyed, and a customer who may try another app tomorrow.

A
③ Tree

I’ll split by the stage of the order, which has no overlaps: before the restaurant accepts, accepted but not cooking, cooking, on the way ({{REF:tree26}}). Your data puts us in the first two. Within them, the customer either waited too long for acceptance, saw the arrival time jump, got a price surprise, or changed their mind.

A
④ Clues

Biryani and North Indian, metros, frequent users, 2–3 weeks, no release: that looks like a demand spike at heavy kitchens. I’d check two numbers for those restaurants: time to accept an order, and arrival time after payment against the time shown on the menu. Suppose acceptance went from 1 to 4 minutes and the arrival time jumps by 20 minutes on 40% of orders.

Why this move: every clue the interviewer gave removes branches. Using them out loud is what they are testing.

A
⑤ Fix

Show the truth before payment, not after: calculate the arrival time from the kitchen’s live queue before checkout. Pause a restaurant for new orders when its acceptance time passes 3 minutes. Offer “similar biryani, 30 minutes” from a less busy kitchen.

A
⑥ Measure

Goal (North Star): customer cancellations within 5 minutes of payment, back to 3%. Safety check (guardrail): orders placed, since honest times may put some people off, and order value for paused restaurants. Trade-off: a few lost orders against many fewer broken promises; net orders delivered decides.

Framework · Issue trees and MECE

1 · The situation.The candidate had good clues and four buckets, but the buckets overlapped and nothing was ruled out. An issue tree turns “why?” into a list of branches you can check one by one.

2 · An everyday example.Your keys are missing. Split the search: inside the flat or outside it. Inside: bedroom, kitchen, living room, bathroom. Every place is in exactly one branch, and together they cover the whole flat. Now compare a bad split: “in my bag” and “in the bedroom”. The bag may be in the bedroom, so you search it twice, and nothing covers the car.

3 · The framework.An issue tree breaks one question into branches, and each branch into smaller ones, until every leaf can be checked with data. The branches should be MECE: mutually exclusive (no overlaps) and collectively exhaustive (no gaps). Four reliable ways to split: by stage of a process, by a formula (orders = users × frequency), by segment (city, platform, new or old users), and inside vs outside the company.

4 · When it misleads.A complete MECE tree can have fifty leaves; the skill is pruning with data, not drawing. Category splits such as restaurant / delivery / platform / external tend to overlap because causes interact: rain (external) slows riders (delivery), which makes kitchens wait (restaurant). And the neat split of the flat into inside and outside still misses keys left in the door lock, on the boundary.

5 · In the interview.“I’ll split this by the stage of the order, because every cancellation happens at exactly one stage. Your point that people cancel before cooking lets me drop half the tree straight away.”
